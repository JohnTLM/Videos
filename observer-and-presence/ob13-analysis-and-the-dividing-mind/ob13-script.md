# Chapter 13. Analysis and the Dividing Mind

Look at a cup. At once it becomes a handle, a rim and a body.

Cognition divides. It takes a whole, separates it into parts, and names them. It asks which part carries the identity of the whole. Motion becomes a before and an after. The Buddhist traditions have names for this mode of mind: vikalpa, or kalpanā. Conceptual construction: the mind that divides and names.

Nāgārjuna's verses on the Middle Way answer this habit with analysis. They take motion, fire and fuel, agent and action, and everything conditioned. They divide each, and show that none of the divisions yields what was claimed. The chariot is not in the wheels, the axle or the pole. Motion is not in the mover. The analysis turns the dividing mind against itself.

Analysis has two faces. In thought, it separates an object into pieces, and searches for the one that carries identity. In perception, the division is already built in: we see a cup as parts because we have learned to. The verses claim that this way of cutting things up is not a neutral description of what is there. It is a mode of knowing. It treats the parts as the real basis of appearance, and the whole as a heap that needs explaining.

The regress makes this visible. Each part can be divided again. The search for an indivisible basis either never ends, or ends in a part that cannot be found when it is examined. Nāgārjuna does not conclude that ordinary objects are unreal. He concludes that analysis cannot find the intrinsic nature it assumed: a nature a thing would have from its own side. The parts, too, are designated. They are named, and known through the naming.

So analysis is used to expose analysis. The dividing mind is shown to be a formation, not a discovery.

One verse names the middle way in these terms. Whatever is dependent co-arising, that is what we call emptiness. That, being dependent designation, is the middle way.

The verse holds three things together. Dependent arising is the fact that things come from causes and conditions. Emptiness is the lack of intrinsic nature that follows from that fact. Designation is the way such things are named and known, and the naming is itself dependent. So dependent designation is not a compromise between existence and non-existence. It is what the middle path looks like, once it is understood.

The verse blocks two errors at once. Eternalism treats things as having a fixed nature that stands on its own, whatever the conditions. Nihilism concludes, from the absence of that nature, that nothing exists. Designation avoids both. Things are not nothing, because they function and can be named. They do not exist in themselves, because their naming depends on causes, on parts, and on the mind that names them.

The logic runs in one direction. Whatever arises dependently comes from causes and conditions. Something with intrinsic nature would need nothing else. So whatever arises dependently lacks intrinsic nature. And that lack is what "empty" means.

Emptiness, then, is not a separate reality behind phenomena. It is what dependent arising amounts to, when its status is examined.

There is not one thing that has not arisen dependently. And so there is not one thing that is not empty.
