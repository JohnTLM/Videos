Take away the observer, and a second question waits. Does experience rest on something beneath it?

Perhaps there is a raw layer, simply there before any naming, which concepts then describe. Philosophers call it the Given.

Locke gave it one shape. Shape and motion, he said, belong to bodies themselves. Colour and warmth are only powers in bodies to produce sensations in us. Later, Wilfrid Sellars argued that a pure given in experience is a myth. What we call a sensation is already conceptual.

Buddhist thought built its own version centuries earlier, with greater rigour. Dig-nah-gah began with a symmetry. There are two means of valid knowledge, perception and inference, because there are two kinds of object. The particular, which characterises itself alone, is grasped by perception, free of concepts. The universal, a character shared by many things, is grasped by concept.

Dhar-mah-keer-tee sharpened the scheme. The particular is ultimately real because it alone can do something. It has causal efficacy, the power to bring about an effect. Universals do nothing. The fire that is merely thought of burns no one.

So the building has two storeys. Below are momentary particulars, met by perception before any concept arrives. Above are concepts, which never touch particulars at all. They are useful, but strictly they misrepresent.

This is no crude view. Dhar-mah-keer-tee posits no bare substance beneath qualities. The real is what functions, and what functions is met before any naming. It is a Given without a substrate, and the strongest form of it.

Chun-drah-keer-tee answers Dig-nah-gah's scheme with three moves.

The first concerns the word characteristic. Hardness characterises earth. If the hardness is different from the earth, earth apart from hardness has no mark. If it is the same, nothing characterises anything. Characteristic and characterised are designated together: each is picked out and named only in relation to the other. Neither is found first. A particular singled out as this, and only this, has already been singled out by contrast: the very work reserved for the upper floor.

The second concerns what perception meets. Chun-drah-keer-tee keeps perception, as the world uses it. But what the world perceives is the pot, not a momentary patch of colour. A cup on a table is seen as a cup, from the start.

The third move comes from Nah-gar-joo-nah. Means of knowing and objects known are each established only in relation to the other. Each defines the other, like long and short.

Tsong-kah-pah makes the conclusion explicit. Nothing is established by its own characteristic, not even conventionally. Other schools kept the particular at the level of convention. The Prah-sahng-gee-kah view refuses it at every level.

The refusal is narrow. Valid knowing stays. The snake seen in a rope is still corrected by a lamp. What goes is one claim: that perception's object stands there from its own side, before designation, as a floor.

And efficacy, Dhar-mah-keer-tee's deepest reason? A key's power to open is found only with the lock, the hand and the door. Efficacy is itself a relation. What looked like the stamp of independent reality is the clearest mark of dependence.

So the building has no ground floor. Perception is not a lower layer, and concept is not a top coat. Vividness and designation are mingled all the way through.
