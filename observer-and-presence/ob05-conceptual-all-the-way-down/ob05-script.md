# Chapter 5. Conceptual All the Way Down?

If there is no Given, the opposite answer beckons. Perhaps experience is concept through and through. Perhaps the world is simply what our concepts make of it.

The most careful modern form of this thought is John McDowell's. Concepts, he argued, are already at work in perception itself. That is how experience can give us reasons. The conceptual, he wrote, is unbounded. There is no outer edge where something non-conceptual waits. Readers often sum this up as "conceptual all the way down", though the phrase is a gloss, not his own.

Hubert Dreyfus objected. Think of skilled, absorbed activity. The chess master who sees the move is not applying concepts. The expert simply copes. Their exchange is one of the most instructive in recent philosophy.

Madhyamaka, the Middle Way, sides with neither. McDowell is no idealist. He insists the world constrains what we think. But a mind whose concepts reach everywhere, read as an account of what reality is, swaps the realist's substance for the idealist's concept. Read the same way, Dreyfus's absorbed coping restores a floor beneath concepts. The Given comes back, in practical dress rather than perceptual. Each makes one side come first.

What remains is a symmetry, with two halves.

First, appearance is not raw material for concepts. Ice stings with its own sharpness. No one decides the sting. A shadow cannot be called a bridge. What keeps naming from being arbitrary is the vividness itself.

Second, concepts are not a veil over appearance. The cup is seen as a cup. The duck is seen as a duck. Designation, the picking out and naming of something, is not a screen held in front of the world. It is in the appearing.

Neither is the ground of the other. Concepts draw their content and their correction from what appears. What appears is picked out and made usable through designation. They are designated together, like long and short. Even the words conceptual and non-conceptual depend on each other. Each is known only as the other's contrast.

Dreyfus's own examples show this. Riding a bicycle is not a set of rules stored in the head. Nor is it a wordless reflex beneath all rules. The skill is designated together with rider, machine, slope and wind, and found in none of them alone.

And designation is wider than language. To say "conceptual all the way down" mistakes designation for a kind of sentence. To say "non-conceptual at the bottom" mistakes the absence of words for the absence of designation.

The ordinary distinction survives. Seeing a snake is not remembering one. A perception can correct a thought, as a lamp shows the snake to be a rope. Dharmakīrti's line between perception and conception keeps its everyday use. What it loses is the claim to mark two natures, one beneath the other.

So the answer is neither concept at the bottom, nor something without concept at the bottom.

There is no down.
