# Chapter 18. Two Traditions, One Risk

A knife cannot cut itself. So how could a mind know itself?

This is where two traditions meet, and where they rub against each other. Madhyamaka, the Middle Way, refuses svabhāva, a nature that stands on its own. Dzogchen speaks of rang rig, self-cognizing awareness. And a nature that is not produced, yet is the ground of appearances, can sound very close to what Madhyamaka refuses.

So agreement between the two cannot be assumed. It has to be argued.

Candrakīrti makes the difficulty concrete. A knife cannot cut itself. In the same way, a mind cannot know itself without splitting in two: a knower, and a known.

Mipham answers with a lamp. A lamp lights itself while it lights other things. No second lamp is needed to see the first. Self-knowing, on this view, is not a second act performed on the flow of awareness. It is simply what the flow's luminosity is, its knowing clarity.

Mipham went further. He argued that the luminosity of rigpa, awareness in its natural state, is not a svabhāva. It is not established as an independent object. It is known only in self-knowing, and depends on nothing outside itself.

Gorampa presses the Middle Way's critique of intrinsic nature more absolutely. His reading shows where a Dzogchen formulation risks reification, turning what is open into a thing. The debate is useful for exactly this reason. Both sides agree that the risk is real.

Longchenpa is explicit. The ground is empty. It is not a subtle existent, standing behind appearances. Later commentators have argued at length that the two traditions point to the same thing. On one side, appearance and emptiness, inseparable. On the other, primordial purity and spontaneous presence.

That equivalence is still contested. Some lineages hold that Dzogchen is its own resultant vehicle, its own path. It is not reducible to Madhyamaka analysis, or guaranteed by it. To say the two refer to one nature is this book's own synthesis, and it is offered as one. The safer claim is narrower. Both agree that the grasp at solid substance is the problem. And their methods converge on undoing it.

They also share a danger in practice. Dzogchen teachers distinguish zhig pa, suppression, from rang grol, self-release. Suppression pushes thoughts down. It can make a stillness that becomes one more fixed state. Self-release lets thoughts dissolve, when they are not followed or fixed.

Madhyamaka makes the same point from its own side. Candrakīrti accepted śamatha, the practice of calming the mind, as a conventional means. But for him the decisive step was analysis. Analysis shows that the parts searched for cannot be found.

Stillness forced by effort can become one more thing to hold.
