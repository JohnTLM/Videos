# Chapter 14. Reification and Svabhāva as Stance

Where does a ground come from?

Not from the appearances themselves. They are many, particular, and bound up in relation. It comes from two steps.

The first step is abstraction. A common feature is drawn out from the particulars. Think of "chariot", the same across many assemblies of parts. Or "presence", across many experiences. Language, memory and ordinary life depend on it.

The second step is reification: treating an idea as a thing. The abstraction is taken to exist in its own right. It now seems to stand beneath the particulars, grounding them. Only this second step creates the sense of something standing behind.

Examined, that unity is not found apart from the faces it was drawn from. The ground was never there. It was produced by reifying a useful abstraction.

Dharmakīrti's theory of apoha, exclusion, is a helpful companion here. On that account, a general idea is understood by excluding what it is not. It does not point to a real common essence. Madhyamaka, the Middle Way school, presses further. A commonality taken as a ground has become svabhāva: own nature, a being that stands on its own.

So svabhāva is not found in the content of an experience. Redness, sound and the feeling of presence can be exactly as vivid either way. In this book's reading, svabhāva is a matter of stance: how the appearing is held. Is it held as existing from its own side, independent of conditions? Is it held as the foundation from which other things get their being?

The same red can be held in two ways. In one, it is taken as a ground. Everything else is measured against it. In the other, it simply appears. It relates to light, to the eye, to attention, and nothing is placed beneath it. The first is svabhāva. The second is dependent origination: arising through conditions. The red is the same. The difference lies entirely in the stance.

This is why the taste of presence cannot be a foundation that analysis finally reaches. Seen without anything standing behind it, appearance is dependent through and through. Its immediacy is that of something relating, not of something standing alone.

It also explains why the habit is hard to see. Through language, perception and emotion, the mind has learned to treat each vivid appearance as a thing with a fixed character. The habit is so reliable that the naming disappears into what is named. The sense of inherent existence is not argued for. It is assumed, each time an appearance is met.

Repeated looking loosens the assumption. Each time the forming is watched, the framework is seen to be added, not found.

What was taken as a fact about the world is recognised as a way of relating to it. A way that works, without needing to be true from its own side.
