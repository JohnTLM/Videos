# Chapter 21. Painted Food, Painted Hunger

A painted rice cake does not satisfy hunger.

So says an old Zen warning. Concepts are pictures of food. Realisation is eating. The monk Xiangyan, famous for his learning, could not answer his teacher's question, and burned his notes. Years later, sweeping a path, he heard a pebble strike a stalk of bamboo. He woke.

Dōgen turns the saying over. In Gabyō, a chapter of his Shōbōgenzō, he writes that there is no remedy for hunger other than painted rice cakes. Without painted hunger, he says, one never becomes a true person.

He is not praising study over practice. He is refusing the picture behind the saying: a real, unpainted cake on one side, and pale, useless paintings of it on the other.

Look for the unpainted one. Take the next thing you are about to eat, a rice cake or a piece of bread. It appears, first of all, as food. Not as a white, roughly round object that you then recognise as edible. It is already something to eat, already pulling at the hand.

Now remove that. Remove the name, and the knowing that it is made from rice. Remove the hunger that makes it look good. Remove the occasions it belongs to, the New Year table, the snack at your desk. Remove the farmer, the field, the hand that pounded it. What is left that is the rice cake itself?

Each place you look yields more appearance, and no cake behind it. To a stone, nothing on the plate is food at all. To an ant, it is a mountain of sugar. A rice cake apart from every way of meeting it is not found.

Dōgen is not saying there is nothing but the mind's painting. There is no blank canvas under the painting, and no painter standing apart from it. In this book's reading, the painting is how anything is met at all. And the painted cake feeds. Eat it, and the hunger goes. What is removed is only the assumption that an unpainted cake must be doing the feeding underneath.

Then Dōgen goes one step further. The hunger is painted too. Hunger is named together with the food that would end it, just as fuel is fuel only as what fire burns. Neither comes first. Yet hunger is no less real. It gets you up from the desk.

Xiangyan was not wrong to burn his notes. He burned the use he had made of them, as a substitute for seeing. The cure was not a better note. It was the pebble on the bamboo.

But awakening does not lie beyond all painting, in some unpainted ground. The sound of the pebble was painted too: heard as a click, as the end of a long search. Realisation did not escape the painting. It saw through the demand for something behind it.

Try this over one meal. Before eating, notice how the food appears: already as food, already wanted. Then look, slowly, for the food apart from its name, its history and its use. Look at the hunger the same way. Where is it, apart from the food, the stomach, the hour? Then put the looking down, and eat.

The meal is exactly as satisfying as before. Painted food, painted hunger, painted satisfaction, and nothing missing.
