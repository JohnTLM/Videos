# Chapter 3. Ignorance and Its Habit

A reader finishes the first part of the book and agrees with every line. The witness was not found. Vividness did not need a viewer.

The next morning someone says something unkind. Within a second there is someone who was hurt, and someone planning a reply. Nothing in the argument was false. It simply never reached the place where the habit works.

Buddhist traditions call the root of suffering ignorance, avidyā. The word is easy to misread as missing information, something a teacher could supply. The ignorance meant here is different. It is a misrecognition of what is already present. Dzogchen calls it ma rigpa, non-recognition. The nature of appearing is right here, and it goes unrecognised. The remedy is not a new fact. It is a seeing that was overlooked.

If ignorance were a missing belief, an argument could fill the gap. If it is a habit of not seeing, an argument can only change a belief about the habit. The habit carries on.

Watch an ordinary moment closely. Something touches the senses. At once it is arranged into a seer, a seeing and a seen. A feeling follows: a pull towards, or a pulling away. Then reasoning begins, and it takes that arrangement as the fact it must deal with. By the time a thought forms, the frame is already in place, and the thought defends it.

So reasoning is not the source. It arrives late. This is why someone can argue skilfully against a self, and still be wounded by a slight. The argument happened in one place. The wound happened in another.

Even real insight gets absorbed. A reader sees that the one who hears is not found, and feels the relief of it. Soon "there is no self" becomes a view to hold and defend, and the one who holds it is the seer again. Analysis works the same way. The question "who is looking?" already presumes a looker.

Why go on to logic at all, then? Because the habit is most persuasive when nothing challenges it. An argument that shows the frame is assumed, not found, gives the seeing something to rest on when feelings run strong.

The habit is also felt in the body. When someone feels wronged, the chest may tighten, the breath shortens, and attention narrows to a point. When the frame loosens, the body may soften and the attention open. This is something to test, not to believe in advance.

The habit rests on four supports. Grammar puts a doer behind every act. Memory treats remembering as proof of a rememberer. Naming turns a useful label into a holder. Deepest is the need for a ground: uncertainty feels like falling, so a floor is looked for beneath experience.

The first step is not to fight the pattern, or to replace it with a better picture. A new account of a true self beneath it would only build a second seer. The first step is to see the pattern as a pattern.

A thought of "I" arises, and it is noticed as a thought. A feeling of being wronged arises, and it is noticed as tightness and heat. Nothing is done to it.

The looking is enough to show that the seer was never standing where the habit said it stood.
