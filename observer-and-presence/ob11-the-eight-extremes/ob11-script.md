# Chapter 11. The Eight Extremes

When did it start? When will it end? Where did it come from, and where did it go?

These are the questions we ask about anything.

Nāgārjuna opens his verses on the Middle Way with a homage. In it he names eight extremes, set out in four pairs. Arising and ceasing. Permanence and annihilation. Coming and going. Identity and difference.

Under the logic of substance, the view that things stand on their own, these are not extremes at all. They are simply what one asks. Does it last, or is it destroyed? Is it the same as that, or something else?

Look closely, and each question assumes the same thing. It assumes there is something with whereabouts. A place in time, in space or in identity, that belongs to it from its own side.

Arising and ceasing put the thing at the two ends of a line. Permanence keeps it on the line. Annihilation removes it. Coming and going move it from one place to another. Identity and difference set it beside something else, to be compared. So the extremes are not eight separate errors. They are eight ways of asking where a thing is.

The logic of dependency does not give subtler answers. It turns the questions over. It does not say the thing arises in some hidden form, or slips away somewhere invisible. It says that what was supposed to have whereabouts is not found. And so the questions lose their object.

Take them one by one. Ask when something came into being. No thing is found crossing an edge, from absent to present. Yet "it began at noon" still holds, as convention. Ask when it went out of being. No thing is found falling into nothing. Ending is named within a before and an after.

Ask what stays the same underneath. No carrier is found beneath the change. Ask what was destroyed. Nothing stood on its own to be destroyed.

Ask where it came from. There are no whereabouts before it appeared. Ask where it went. There are no whereabouts after. The question simply does not apply.

Ask whether it is the same as its source. It is not. The sprout is not the seed. Ask whether it is something else entirely. It is not that either. Rice yields rice.

The decisive point is easy to miss. Dependent arising, things coming about through causes and conditions, is not one thing, and the eight negations another. It is not as if what arises in dependence were then, mysteriously, also free of arising. The negations describe dependent arising itself.

If a sprout could be found arising from its seed, it would have an arising of its own. Then it would not need to depend. That it depends is exactly why no arising is found when it is sought. Dependence and unfindability are one fact. Seen from the side of convention, it is dependence. Seen from the side of analysis, it is unfindability.

This turns ordinary experience upside down, and removes none of it.

Switch off a lamp. The room is dark, and the light went nowhere. Switch it on. The room fills, and the light came from nowhere.

Both descriptions hold of every appearance. Both were true all along.
