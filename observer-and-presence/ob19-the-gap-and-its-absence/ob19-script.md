# Chapter 19. The Gap and Its Absence

Every act of knowing seems to come with a small distance built in.

Here is a subject. There is an object. And behind the appearing, a ground that it all rests on. If knowing always carries this structure, then every taste of presence is already filtered through a gap. Adding one more act of knowing will not close it. That act would carry the gap with it.

The tradition's answer is not to build a bridge across the gap. It is that the gap was never holding anything up. When the standing behind is seen through, no witness is placed apart from what appears. No ground remains that appearances must be checked against. What remains is the immediacy of appearing itself.

This is why experience can be vivid and direct. Vividness here is not intensity added to ordinary perception. It is the absence of the mediation that usually makes perception feel distant. And the emptiness that applies to the dull applies equally to the vivid. Immediacy does not make appearances more real. It describes how they are no longer held at a distance.

Longchenpa speaks of the self-liberation of thought. It describes the same thing from the side of thinking. Thoughts arise, and are not followed. And because they are not followed, they do not become a wall.

Then comes a question. Is this an achievement, or a stopping?

If the gap is a construction, its absence is a stopping. The taste is there wherever the construction is not running. If the gap were built into things, the taste would be impossible. Practice would only show the gap again. Madhyamaka and Dzogchen both take the first view. That leaves two things to understand. Why does the construction return so readily? And what, in practice, loosens it?

One caution belongs here. "No gap, no ground" can quietly become a new ground. It can turn into a confident picture of non-duality, something to possess.

So the immediacy is best described simply. It appears when the standing behind is not being kept up. It is not a state anyone can now be sure of.
