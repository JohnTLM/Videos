# The Logic: After Anatta

Listen to a car going by. The sound is bright and clear. And it is already passing as it sounds. At no point is there a sound that stays.

Here is a koan to hold in mind. This moment ceases as it arises. Does it arise, or does it cease?

This video is about how that question is answered by two different ways of thinking. And about why the difference between them matters, not only for philosophy, but for practice.

It draws on The Logic, a section of After Anatta: notes by John Tan, known as Thusness, on what turned out to matter after the anatta insight of 1997. The seven phases of insight describe the seeing. These notes set out the logic behind it.

Start with the claim the author puts first. The logic of dependency cannot be understood clearly by reasoning alone. It needs a direct insight into what a phenomenon actually is. A sound, a thought, a cup.

The two logics can be learned as ideas. One starts with things, and asks what happens to them. The other asks how anything is picked out as a thing at all. Explained like this, the difference takes a minute to follow, and a minute to agree with.

But agreeing changes very little. The logic of substance is not a belief we hold. It is how things seem, before any thought. The sound is simply there. The cup is simply there, on its own side. As long as things are seen this way, even the new logic is quietly read through the old one.

So the author names three insights after anatta. Without seeing them clearly, and confirming them again and again in direct experience, the logic stays a clever argument.

The first. Every appearance is vivid, and it cannot be found. Look at a red apple. The red is bright, impossible to miss. Now look for it. Is it in the skin, in the light, in the eye? It is not found in any of them, and not anywhere else. Yet the apple is still red. Vivid and unfindable, at the same time. The tradition calls this emptiness. Not a void, and not a ground behind the appearing. Only the appearing's not being found.

The second. Appearance arises whenever its conditions come together. Light, a surface, an eye, attention: together, red appears. When one is missing, it does not. Nothing comes out of a hidden place, and nothing goes back into one. The tradition calls this dependent origination.

The third. We habitually and intuitively think inherently, and the way we do it follows the shape of our language. A sentence wants a subject and a verb. So we say "lightning flashes", as if there were a lightning apart from the flash. But experience is never like that. The flashing is the lightning. The tradition calls this habit ignorance. Not a lack of information, but the innate grasping at things as existing from their own side.

The first two are one fact, seen from two sides. What arises in dependence is what is called emptiness. The third is what hides that fact.

And the third runs almost by default. Whatever experiences and insights we have, it reshapes them before we notice. Many practitioners hold that direct experience is what matters, and that view and logic come second. So practice becomes the removal of concepts. Not thinking. Bare attention. Naked awareness.

These practices do something real. With the commentary quiet, the clarity of appearances can be tasted directly. But that is as far as they go. The habit of thinking inherently does not need words to work. Silence the words, and it carries on without comment. It reads the clear, wordless experience in the old way.

The author describes three ways this happens. We taste luminous clarity, and invent a witness: a knower standing behind the knowing. We meet the vividness of appearances, and make it objective: a world out there, solid and given. We taste unfindability, and make it private: all of it only in the mind.

Three real tastes. Three inventions. Each time the experience was true, and the reading was false. And the three readings are the great positions people argue about: consciousness as the ground of everything, a physical world simply there, and a world that is only mind. The author passed through each of them. The way out of the last was the logic of dependency itself. If a subject depends on its object, what makes it truly a subject? An inside that depends on an outside is not truly inside.

The great masters said the same, in their own ways. Atiśa wrote that calm abiding alone, without the wisdom that sees emptiness, does not end the obstructions. Tsongkhapa wrote that without the wisdom that realises how things are, even renunciation and compassion cannot cut existence at its root. Longchenpa advised that when thoughts arise, the direct view is crucial. And Dōgen, a Zen master, scolded those in his day who thought the old Zen stories were beyond logical thought.

Experience matters. But experience without the view cannot do the work.

Now return to the koan. This moment ceases as it arises. Does it arise, or does it cease?

The logic of substance starts with a thing: this moment. Arising and ceasing are what happen to it. So it must choose.

It arises? Then it is there. But it ceases as it arises, so it is never there to have arisen. And if it stays even for an instant, that instant has a beginning, a middle and an end, and it splits again.

It ceases? Then something passes away. But what passes away, if it never stood?

Both? Coming into being and passing away are opposites. They cannot belong to one thing at one time.

Neither? Then nothing happens at all. Yet a sound is being heard right now.

Every answer fails. Nāgārjuna put the whole trap in one verse. There is no passing away either apart from coming into being, or together with it.

At this point the logic of substance looks for a way out. A timeless Now that the moments pass through. An awareness in which they come and go. A pure flow. Each way out keeps the moment as a thing, and only moves it somewhere else.

The logic of dependency starts one step earlier. It does not begin with a moment that has arising and ceasing. It asks how "this moment" is picked out at all. A moment is picked out together with its arising and its ceasing. Arising, together with ceasing. None comes first, and none is found apart from the others.

In the author's words: the moment doesn't arise nor cease, but is merely designated as such upon conditions.

Think of a staircase. Are the stairs up, or down? In themselves, neither. They are up for someone climbing, and down for someone coming down. That depends on where the walker stands, and which way they face. And the stairs are really climbed, and really descended. The moment is the same. It is called arising from one side, and ceasing from the other. It is not nothing, and it is not only in the mind.

So "ceases as it arises" is not a paradox. It is a plain description. There is no "it" standing between the arising and the ceasing, to do either. The question is not answered. It falls away.

The eight steps of The Logic each come back to this koan from one side.

The first sets the two logics side by side: one starts with things, the other asks how anything is picked out.

The second asks what exactly is denied. Only the "it", the moment standing on its own. Deny too little, and a timeless Now takes its place. Deny too much, and nothing happens at all.

The third goes looking for the moment, the way the old masters looked for a chariot. Is it the arising? The ceasing? Both? Their owner? Their container? It is not found in any of them. Yet moments are named, counted and timed.

The fourth gives the koan two answers that do not conflict. Conventionally, the train leaves and arrives, and we can say when. Looked for, nothing that arises or ceases is found. Not two worlds. One moment, met two ways.

The fifth shows why all four answers fall together. Each one assumed an "it".

The sixth names the two traps. Say "it arises", and the mind finds somewhere for the moment to last: the eternalist trap. Say "it ceases", and the mind decides that nothing matters: the nihilist trap. Both keep the "it".

The seventh answers the worry. If no moment is found, how does anything work? A moment with its own fixed nature could never arise or cease at all. It is because nothing stands on its own that things can arise, change and work.

And the eighth gathers it all into four questions, ready for any question. What would a substance view say? What does dependency say instead? What is affirmed conventionally? What is never found?

The koan is only the sharpest case. Each step also carries the same logic beyond the moment: to a self, an object, a cause, a person.

The self. Say "I have changed." The sentence holds an "I" still, so that the changing can happen to it. Look for the one who changed, apart from the changing, and none is found. Yet a promise I made is still mine to keep.

An object. Close your hand, and a fist appears. Open it, and the fist is gone. Where did it go? Nowhere. There was never a fist apart from the hand closing. Yet a fist can still knock on a door.

A cause. A seed is called a cause only of its sprout. Cause and effect are named together. Still, rice grows from rice seed, never from barley.

The person. Look for a friend apart from the face, the voice and the years of meeting, and no one is found. Yet the friendship is real. When they die, someone lived, was loved, and is missed.

There is one more turn. We think our way of solving is logical. We bring questions like "does the self exist?" or "what happens when I die?", and try to answer them. But each question already assumes a thing with its own nature. Answered in the same frame, any answer only swaps one thing for another. The view offers something else. Not a better answer, but a way to question the question.

Nothing is lost when the "it" is not found. The sound is still heard. The clock still keeps time. Actions still bear fruit. Mutual dependence has no first and no higher. No substrate underneath, and no power hidden inside things. Dependent arising is not a lesser layer over something more real. There is nothing more real underneath for it to cover.

Listen to a sound once more. It is vivid. It is gone. And nothing went anywhere.

Ask what arises, and the koan is a paradox. See that no "it" is found, and the koan is simply this moment.
