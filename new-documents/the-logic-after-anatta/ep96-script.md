# The Logic: After Anatta

Listen to a car going by. The sound is bright and clear. And it is already passing as it sounds. At no point is there a sound that stays.

Here is a koan to hold in mind. This moment ceases as it arises. Does it arise, or does it cease?

This video draws on The Logic, a section of After Anatta: notes by John Tan, known as Thusness, on what turned out to matter after the anatta insight of 1997. Eight steps teach two logics: the logic of substance, and the logic of dependency. But the author puts one thing first. The logic of dependency cannot be understood clearly by reasoning alone. It needs a direct insight into what a phenomenon actually is. A sound, a thought, a cup. Seeing comes first.

So he names four insights after anatta. Without seeing them clearly, and confirming them again and again in direct experience, the logic stays a clever argument. And seeing them once is not enough. If we stop, the old logic quietly comes back.

The first. Every appearance is vivid, and it cannot be found. Look at a red apple. The red is bright, impossible to miss. Now look for it. Is it in the skin, in the light, in the eye? It is not found in any of them, and not anywhere else. Yet the apple is still red, as vivid as before. Not vivid first and empty later. Not empty underneath and vivid on the surface. Confirm only the vividness, and presence turns into a new ground. Confirm only the unfindability, and the world turns thin and void. Both have to be seen together, in the same appearing. The tradition calls this emptiness. Not a void, and not a ground behind the appearing. Only the appearing's not being found.

The second. Appearance arises whenever its conditions come together. Light, a surface, an eye, attention: together, red appears. When one is missing, it does not. Nothing comes out of a hidden place, and nothing goes back into one. And the conditions are not a solid network behind the display. They too are vivid, and not found. The tradition calls this dependent origination.

The third. We habitually and intuitively think inherently, and the way we do it follows the shape of our language. A sentence wants a subject and a verb. So we say "the wind blows", and "lightning flashes", as if there were a wind apart from the blowing, and a lightning apart from the flash. "I see the tree" puts a seer on one side, a tree on the other, and an act of seeing between them. The grammar becomes the picture of the world.

But experience is never like that. The flashing is the lightning. The blowing is the wind. This does not mean a pure, wordless world lies underneath the words. That would be one more floor. It means only that the picture of things standing behind what happens was never found in experience. And this habit runs almost by default. It bends every experience and insight back into things, selves and grounds before we notice. So practice is not collecting experiences. It is undoing these habits, again and again. The tradition calls this ignorance. Not a lack of information, but the innate grasping at things as existing from their own side.

The fourth. There is no given. No world is already there, with its own edges and things, waiting behind what appears. One bowl of liquid is water to us, pus to a hungry ghost, a home to a fish. The habit says a bowl of something must sit underneath, a neutral stuff that each being merely filters. Look for it, and it is not found. What appears, and how it is picked out, arise together. Beings still agree, and the world still holds together, with no hidden object beneath it. Nor is the mind the floor instead. Mind and object arise together, and neither comes first.

The first two are one fact, seen from two sides. Nāgārjuna says that what arises dependently is what is called emptiness. The third is what hides that fact. The fourth takes away its last hiding place: a given world beneath it all.

Anatta is the first door. In seeing, no seer is found: no doer behind the doing. Nāgārjuna takes the same insight through every thing: the walker, the fire, the cause, even the seeing. And where do the four insights lead? When the habit stops, and no given is left beneath, the first two are tasted together. With no doer anywhere, and nothing put in its place, nothing is making the appearing. The sound is not a product waiting to be finished. It is already complete, by itself, exactly as its conditions meet. The Dzogchen tradition calls this spontaneous presence, the last of the seven phases. Not uncaused, and not a source behind things. Dependent arising itself, met without the fixing.

Why does the logic need the seeing? The two logics can be learned as ideas. One starts with things, and asks what happens to them. The other asks how anything is picked out as a thing at all. Explained like this, the difference takes a minute to follow, and a minute to agree with.

But agreeing changes very little. The logic of substance is not a belief we hold. It is how things seem, before any thought: the cup is simply there, on its own side. Seen this way, even the new logic is read through the old one. Things are still things; now they also depend. The conditions become a new solid floor. Emptiness becomes a void, or a vast space behind it all. The words have changed. The logic has not.

So what is a phenomenon? A vivid appearing that is not found, even while it is appearing vividly. Vivid: nothing is taken away. The red is red, a sound is heard, and a car can still knock you down. Not found: not in its parts, and not apart from them. Not out there, and not in here as an image in the mind. And the mind that looks is not found either. Not even "nowhere": the absence is not a place, or a void left over. Dependent already: the red is dependent in the very moment it is red. And clear, with no knower behind it. The clarity belongs to the appearing itself. It is not a light shining from a witness, and it is empty too. No phenomenon, not even the mind, is the floor the others stand on.

Before any of this can work, one thing has to be admitted. We think inherently. Not now and then, and not only when we philosophise. It is our default. Seeing it means noticing, in an ordinary day, that our thinking is still built on things standing on their own: a self that decides, a world out there, a problem that is simply there. It does not feel like a view at all. It feels like how things are.

If this is not seen, even deep experience and genuine insight will hit a wall. The experience can be non dual, and the insight true, while the framework that reads them is still inherent. Then presence becomes a ground, emptiness becomes a void, and conditions become things causing things. The experience has changed. The framework has not.

Many practitioners, especially in Zen, hold that direct experience is what matters, and that view and logic come second. The author calls this a grave mistake. From that starting point, practice becomes the removal of concepts. Not thinking. Bare attention. Naked awareness.

These practices do something genuine. With the commentary quiet, the luminous clarity of appearances can be tasted directly: sounds, colours and sensations shining by themselves. But at most, that is all they do. Removing concepts does not show what appearances are. Tasted vividly, they can still be taken as existing from their own side. The mind can be tasted too, as pure, clear awareness, and then taken as the one thing that stands by itself. But the mind is a phenomenon like any other. Its clarity is as empty as the red of the apple.

Even the idea behind these practices pictures concepts as labels over a given world, to be peeled off. That picture is itself the logic of substance. A thing and its designation arise together. There is no unlabelled layer waiting to be uncovered. Strip the labels, and the habit stays, now without comment. That is how luminous clarity becomes a Witness, a ground, a Source. It was the author's own story in the I AM years.

Bare attention can taste the clarity of appearances. Only the view shows what they are.

The great masters said the same, in their own ways. Atiśa wrote that calm abiding alone, without the wisdom that sees emptiness, does not end the obstructions. Rongzom traced the habit behind appearances to a view: the view of real entities. Dōgen, a Zen master, scolded those in his day who thought the old Zen stories were beyond logical thought. Longchenpa advised that when thoughts arise, the direct view is crucial. Tsongkhapa wrote that without the wisdom that realises how things are, even renunciation and compassion cannot cut existence at its root. And Mipham taught that, just as the "I" is a conceptual label, so are all phenomena.

None of them says experience does not matter. Hearing that there is water does not quench thirst; only drinking does. But experience without the view cannot do the work.

For the author, after I AM, the vividness was never what was missing. What was missing was seeing what that vividness is. What changed things was seeing directly what a phenomenon is: vivid and nowhere, with no knower behind the knowing, and no ground behind the appearing. Only then did the logic of dependency become clear, not as a theory, but as how things are.

Now return to the koan. This moment ceases as it arises. Does it arise, or does it cease?

The logic of substance starts with a thing: this moment. Arising and ceasing are what happen to it. So it must choose.

It arises? Then it is there. But it ceases as it arises, so it is never there to have arisen. It ceases? Then something passes away. But what passes away, if it never stood? Both? Coming into being and passing away are opposites. They cannot belong to one thing at one time. Neither? Then nothing happens at all. Yet a sound is being heard right now.

Every answer fails. Nāgārjuna put the whole trap in one verse. There is no passing away either apart from coming into being, or together with it.

The logic of substance looks for a way out: a timeless Now, an awareness in which moments come and go. Each keeps the moment as a thing, and only moves it somewhere else.

The logic of dependency starts one step earlier. It asks how "this moment" is picked out at all. A moment is picked out together with its arising and its ceasing. None comes first, and none is found apart from the others. In the author's words: the moment doesn't arise nor cease, but is merely designated as such upon conditions.

Think of a staircase. Up, or down? In themselves, neither: up for someone climbing, down for someone coming down. And they are truly climbed. The moment is the same. It is not nothing, and it is not only in the mind.

So "ceases as it arises" is not a paradox. It is a plain description. There is no "it" standing between the arising and the ceasing, to do either. The question is not answered. It falls away.

The eight steps of The Logic each come back to this from one side. What exactly is denied: only the "it", the moment standing on its own. Looked for, like the old masters' chariot, it is not found. Yet moments are named, counted and timed. Conventionally, the train leaves and arrives. One moment, met two ways. And it is because nothing stands on its own that things can arise, change and work. The last step gathers it all into four questions. What would a substance view say? What does dependency say instead? What is affirmed conventionally? What is never found?

The same logic reaches beyond the moment. Look for the one who changed, apart from the changing, and none is found. Yet a promise I made is still mine to keep. Close your hand, and a fist appears. Open it, and the fist is gone. It went nowhere. Look for a friend apart from the face, the voice and the years of meeting, and no one is found. Yet when they die, someone lived, was loved, and is missed.

Nothing is lost when the "it" is not found. The sound is still heard. Actions still bear fruit. Dependent arising is not a lesser layer over something that stands by itself. There is nothing underneath for it to cover.

So, a few things to try. Listen to one sound until it ends. Notice how vivid it was. Then ask where it was. Don't answer with a thought; just look. Look at a colour in front of you. Is there a seer behind the seeing, apart from the colour being seen? Say to yourself, "the wind is blowing". Then feel the wind on your skin. Is there a wind apart from the blowing?

Listen to a sound once more. It is vivid. It is gone. And nothing went anywhere.

Ask what arises, and the koan is a paradox. See that no "it" is found, and the koan is simply this moment.
