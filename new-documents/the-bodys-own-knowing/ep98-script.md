# The Body's Own Knowing: Felt Sense, Inner Winds and the Rainbow Body

A dog barks suddenly behind you. Before you know what it is, your shoulders have jumped. Your breath has stopped. Your heart is already racing.

Something in you knew, before you did.

For many years the rainbow body has interested me. I wanted to read it in a balanced way. Not dismissing it, and not inflating it. And I found that this is not possible until four things are sorted out. Each one has its own video. Leave any one of them out, and the picture tips to one side.

The first is the energy practices of the different traditions. Taoist inner alchemy, Indian yoga and the Tibetan yogas all work with breath, posture, an inner channel, heat and light. Their method is shared. Their view is not. Without sorting this out, the rainbow body looks like an energy achievement, as if steering the winds could turn a body into light. And wind, or energy, quietly becomes a hidden stuff underneath everything. But the winds are not a substrate. Working with them can loosen what the body holds. What frees is seeing through the habit of reification, not where the winds go.

The second is the five elements. Tibet keeps two different fives side by side. One came from China: wood, fire, earth, metal and water, used for the calendar and divination. The other came from India: earth, water, fire, wind and space, read as qualities. Hardness. Cohesion. Warmth. Motion. Room. The rainbow body belongs to the Indian five. Without sorting this out, the five lights get mixed with Chinese phases, or with modern physics of light. Or the elements are heard as energy frozen into matter, waiting to melt back. Nothing freezes. The elements name how appearing is felt and taken, not a stuff underneath.

The third is the reports themselves. What do the traditions actually claim, and how strong is the evidence? Dzogchen, Gelug, Kālacakra, Bön and the Taoist accounts each tell it differently, and with different views. The modern cases rest on the word of those who were there. None has been checked by anyone independent. Without sorting this out, the stories are either swallowed whole as proof, or thrown out as myth. Both miss what the traditions themselves say: the rainbow body is not the measure of insight.

The fourth is how the body knows. This is the one I have not yet set out, and it is what this video is about. Without it, the rainbow body is read in one of two wrong ways. Either everything is in the head, and the body plays no part in realization at all. Or the body, its energy or its light, becomes the ground of everything. The research, and my own practice, point between the two. The body takes part in knowing and in release. But it is not a deeper floor.

Put the four together, and a balanced reading becomes possible. Not dismissing. Not inflating. The method shared, the view not. The elements as qualities, not a frozen stuff. The reports as reports. And the body as a place where the knot is held, and where it comes undone.

Its point is simple. Knowing is not only analysis. It is not only the taking apart of concepts. There is a more immediate knowing, the felt sense of presence in the body. Through it, fear, clinging, openness and spaciousness are often understood more directly than through thought alone. And the old traditions of inner winds were long recognitions of this side of knowing.

Let me start with the body that jumps.

Modern research agrees with the old traditions on one plain point. Much of our knowing happens before we think. Deep in the brain are circuits that detect threat and set off the heart, the hormones and the muscles. They work faster than conscious thought, and largely beneath it.

The body's learning can even be split off from the mind's memory. In one study, a man with damage to his memory centre could not remember a learning task at all. Yet his body still gave the learned response. Another man, with damage to the brain's alarm centre, remembered the task clearly. Yet his body had learned nothing.

So a person can know perfectly well that they are safe, and still find their body braced. The breath held. The heart racing. Knowing that one is safe is not only a conclusion of thought. Much of it is done by the body, and thought arrives later.

There is a quieter side of this too. The psychologist Eugene Gendlin called it the felt sense. It is not a single sensation, like an itch or warmth. It is a vague, whole-body sense of a situation, all about this, that can be noticed before it has words. When you attend to it and wait, a word or an image often comes that fits. And the body eases as it does.

Gendlin and his colleagues found something striking. The people who gained most from therapy were not those who talked best about their problems. They were the ones who could sense this bodily feel, and stay with it. In some matters, this knowing is the deciding one.

Brain imaging shows the same split, between knowing and telling. Researchers asked people to attend to themselves in two ways. In one, they thought about their traits and their story. In the other, they simply felt what was present in the body, with no story. The two used different networks in the brain. In people new to meditation, the two were tightly bound. Every sensation set off a judgment about me. After eight weeks of mindfulness training, they came apart. A sensation could be known directly, without the story attached.

Reading that, I was reminded of how anatta is put. In the seen, only the seen. In the felt, only the felt.

But there is a catch, and it matters. Researchers now think the brain does not simply read the body. It predicts it. What rises from the body is mostly the gap between what was expected and what is found. This explains why felt knowing is direct. It does not pass through reasoning. And it explains why felt knowing can be wrong. If old expectations are set to danger, an ordinary heartbeat is felt as a threat. The body then reports, vividly and directly, a danger that is not there.

Directness is no guarantee.

Now fear. The treatments that work best for fear do not argue it away. They let the body meet what it fears, in safety, until it learns from experience that the threat does not come. That is new learning in the body, not a better argument. Some body-based therapies do the same, more gently. In small, paced steps, so the body's alarm can settle without being flooded.

Clinging is the same. Attachment is not only a belief that something will make me happy. It is a lean of the body toward it. A tightening when it is threatened. A held breath when it is lost. You can feel it before you can say it.

I have written about this before. The habit of taking things as existing from their own side does not live only in ideas. It lives in the muscles, the diaphragm and the breath. A slight tightening behind the eyes. A held, high chest. A guarded belly. A subtle leaning forward into the next moment, as if you had to hold the world together.

When the view loosens, that bracing loosens with it. In my own experience, when the hinge in the view let go, the knot in the body uncoiled on its own. I was not forcing anything.

Openness and spaciousness are known the same way, from the other side. When the bracing goes, the release is not first a thought. It is felt. The chest unclenches. The breath drops. The edges soften. There is room.

Here the science has little to say yet. These are qualities the contemplative traditions have mapped far more fully than any laboratory. In one Tibetan teaching, fear and grasping are not fought or argued with, since that only feeds the agitation. They are met in stillness, silence and spaciousness, where the contraction can loosen from within. That is a report of their tradition. It matches what I have found.

So the old traditions saw this too.

Tibetan medicine says the mind rides the wind, like a rider on a horse. Causality runs both ways. Desire disturbs the wind. But once the wind is disturbed, by shock, worry, overwork or poor food, it disturbs the mind. Racing thoughts. Panic. Poor sleep. Frightening dreams. And since no argument calms a bolting horse, the treatment is bodily. Warm food. Oil massage. Warm compresses. A calmer life.

The Tibetan yogas of breath and movement work the same way. They settle the winds, and so settle the mind. In one trial at a large cancer centre, women having chemotherapy who practised such a programme slept with fewer disturbances than those who only stretched. They were not, though, less tired overall.

The Taoists, too, worked the body, the breath and attention together. Their inner alchemy held that mind-nature and bodily life must be cultivated together. Insight without the body's vitality, they said, stays stuck in empty stillness.

And the Buddha refused to trace every bodily feeling to past action. He named bile, phlegm and wind, the weather, careless habits and injury as causes too. And he taught mindfulness of the breath, the posture and the body itself.

These are not anatomies. The words for wind, in Tibet, in India and in China, first meant wind and breath, not a stuff called energy. The channels and the winds are maps of how breath, feeling and attention move together, drawn from the inside. Read that way, the traditions are accurate about what they map. The felt movement of experience, and how mind and body condition each other.

To stay balanced, I should say what the research does not show.

It does not show that trauma is stored in muscle or connective tissue, waiting to be shaken out. That is a popular story, not a finding. The theory many therapists use to explain the nervous system is strongly disputed by the scientists who study it. And the body-based trauma therapies are promising, but not yet proven against the established ones.

What is well supported is the main point. The body knows before thought. The felt sense guides change. Fear is unlearned through bodily experience. And knowing one's story, and directly feeling the body, run on different paths.

So where do I stand?

Felt knowing is a kind of insight, not only energy work. When fear or clinging is seen as it is felt, as this contraction, this held breath, this lean toward, that seeing is itself insight. It does not wait for a concept. And it often frees more than the concept would.

Contemplative scientists draw the line in the same place. They sort meditation into three families. The family of insight practice aims at what they call reification, taking things as solid, existing from their own side. And its method is direct inquiry into experience, not argument. Deconstruction, in their sense as in mine, is a seeing, not only a reasoning.

But two guards. This felt knowing is more immediate. It is not more fundamental. It is older in evolution and faster in time. But it is not a deeper floor beneath experience. It arises in dependence, like any knowing, and it is as empty as what it knows. To make it a ground would bring the substrate back, through the bodily door.

And direct does not mean right. The body's sense of danger can be a habit, not a perception. So felt knowing needs refining too. By experience, by gentle attention, and by the view. The view tells the body what it need no longer brace against. The body tells the view whether it has truly landed.

Analysis without the body stays in the head, and the body keeps its old bracing underneath. Felt knowing without the view can settle the body, yet leave untouched the framework that made it brace. The street between insight and the body runs both ways.

And this is where it meets the rainbow body.

The body is not outside realization. The knot of reification is held in the body, felt there, and released there. So it is fully reasonable that the traditions speak of realization reaching all the way into the body, and of the body itself being known without grasping.

What is genuinely shown is that the body takes part in knowing and in release. What is not shown is that a corpse turns into light. I hold the first with confidence. The second, I hold as a report.

Two cautions before I close. This is not treatment. If you live with trauma, panic or depression, practices like these are a help, not a cure. And do not force. Settle, and do not strain. Pleasant openness in the body is not awakening. Insight is measured by the freedom of the mind.

So, the dog barks again. The shoulders jump. The breath stops.

This time, notice it. Where it is held. How it feels, before it has a name. And whether anyone is found, holding it.

The body knew first. Let it also be where the knot comes undone.
