# Dependent Arising Section 5 Part 2: Who Ages and Dies?

Who ages and dies? It sounds like the simplest question there is.

I am John Tan, known as Thusness. These are my own notes, from my own practice and contemplation.

Part 1 set out the twelve links. From ignorance to ageing and death. And it ended on a question the list does not answer. Who is turning the wheel?

Here I stay with the tradition's own answer.

A monk once asked the Buddha directly. What is ageing and death, and whose is it?

The Buddha did not answer with a name. Not a valid question, he said. Whether one says the life and the body are the same, or that they are different, there is no living of the holy life. Avoiding both, he teaches by the middle. With birth as condition, ageing and death.

And he goes through every link the same way. Whose is birth? Not a valid question. With becoming as condition, birth.

The question asks for an owner of the chain. The reply gives the chain, and no owner.

The question comes back in other forms. And the answer is always shaped the same way.

A brahmin asks. Is the one who acts the same as the one who meets the result? That is one extreme, the Buddha says. Is the one who acts someone else? That is the other. Avoiding both, he teaches the chain.

A wandering ascetic asks. Is suffering made by oneself? Not so. By another? Not so. By both? Not so. By chance? Not so. Then is there no suffering? There is suffering, the Buddha says. I know it, and I see it. And he gives the chain.

Each time, the questioner needs a someone. A self who made the suffering. Or another who made it. Or a self who acted, and the same self who reaps. Each time, the Buddha declines the someone, and gives the conditions.

One short discourse puts it in a way I find hard to forget.

This body is not yours. Nor does it belong to others. It should be seen as old karma, shaped by intention, something to be felt.

Not mine. Not someone else's. Old karma, felt now.

This is how the tradition holds karma without an owner. Formations, the second link, are intentional actions. They shape what comes next, the way a river cuts its bed. And what comes next flows along that bed.

There is a link between what is done and what is met, reliable and exact. But there is no one standing behind the doing, who then waits for the result.

The classical reading lays the twelve links over three lives, and divides them carefully.

Past causes. Ignorance and formations. Present results. Consciousness, name and form, the six senses, contact and feeling. Present causes. Craving, clinging and becoming. And future results. Birth, and ageing and death.

Four groups, joined at three points. Past action meeting a new life. What is met turning into what is done. And present action shaping a life to come.

In this reading, a new life begins with a moment of consciousness that links it to the last. Nothing travels across. The tradition's image is a flame lit from a flame. The one reborn is neither the same as the one who died, nor another.

So the three-life reading keeps rebirth at the centre. And keeps it without a traveller. That was worked out with great care.

Behind it stands the work of the Abhidharma, the schools that set out to analyse experience completely.

Their achievement was real, and I want to say so plainly. They took the person apart. What we call I turned out to be a stream of momentary factors. Moments of contact, feeling, perception, intention, consciousness, and matter. Look for a self among them, and none is found.

The analysis was thorough. One school listed four kinds of condition. The Theravāda counted twenty four. Each factor was defined by its own mark. Contact is what touches. Feeling is what feels. Each arises, stands for a moment, and ceases.

So the tradition answers in two ways at once. The discourses say, not a valid question. The Abhidharma says, look, and you will find only these moments, linked by these conditions, and no one among them.

In this picture there is no self among the pieces. But the pieces themselves are taken as real, each with its own nature. Where I part from that, the next section will show.

This is where the teaching is most often misheard. So I want to be plain.

No owner does not mean no responsibility. Kind acts tend toward happiness, and cruel acts toward suffering. Practice changes a person. One is responsible for what one has done. The conventional I keeps its promises, and meets the results of what it has done.

The twelve links keep karma, rebirth, and the path that ends suffering. What is left out is only the owner.

And one more thing, equally plain. That rebirth needs no traveller removes one objection to it. But removing a bad argument against something is not the same as giving a good argument for it. Whether rebirth happens is a matter for its own evidence.

The next time I am hurt, I can try the monk's question. Not as philosophy, but as looking. Who is hurt?

Not to deny the hurt. It is there, sharp and vivid. But to look for the one it belongs to. What I find is the words, the feeling, the tightening, the story starting up. Each is fully present. The owner, looked for, is not found.

This is Section 5, Part 2 of the Dependent Arising series.

And when the owner is not found, the story has less to stand on.
