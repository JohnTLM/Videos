# Part 1 of 6: If You Think You Understand

This is Part 1 of 6: If you think you understand.

It comes from After Anatta: notes by John Tan, known as Thusness. The six parts make one article, called Dependent Arising: If You Think You Understand, You Don't.

"If you think you understand quantum mechanics, you don't understand quantum mechanics." Richard Feynman is said to have quipped that. The same trap waits here. If you think you understand dependent arising, you don't understand dependent arising.

On paper it sounds straightforward, even safe. When this is, that is. From the arising of this, that arises. When this is not, that is not. From the ceasing of this, that ceases. Things arise from many causes and conditions, and nothing has an essence of its own.

It reads like ordinary cause and effect. It fits easily onto the everyday, objective picture of the world. But is it really that straightforward? Take two ordinary examples, and enquire.

The first is an apple. When I toss an apple and catch it, I feel I am handling one solid, independent object. It has edges, weight and speed. This is almost intuitive. By default the mind takes the apple as solid, out there, and undeniably objective. There on its own, whether anyone looks or not.

It feels solid, out there, undeniably objective. But is it?

Pause here. Toss an apple, or just picture one.

Now ask: where exactly is this solid, independent apple? Is it the red I see? The press on my palm? The weight, the arc through the air? Is it any one of these, all of them together, or something behind them? Look, and see what is actually found.

Looked for, that solid apple is not found. What is there is many appearings met together. Shapes and colours, the press on the palm, the arc of the motion. They are gathered as one apple by designation, so quick and smooth that it goes unnoticed.

And the apple works perfectly in the hand. It follows gravity and momentum. Yet it has no apple-ness inside it, and no substrate under it. The shapes, the pressure and the motion are not a bottom layer either. They are designated too, and just as unfindable. So I am not catching a fiction. I am catching an apple that exists only conventionally, and works.

Let me go through the whole toss. I toss an apple to a friend, and my friend catches it. Look at it twice. Once as the habit sees it, and once as it actually occurs.

As the habit sees it, a solid apple flies through the air. It owns its shape, colour, weight and smell as its own. A solid thrower sends it, and a solid receiver takes it. I call this the inherent framework, the matrix. It is not a thought I hold. It is a habit in the body, before any thought, of taking what is singled out as standing from its own side.

Now look at the moment directly. No single apple-entity is hiding behind the experience. There are many dependent arisings, at once. The touch and the density, where skin, nerve, pressure and resistance meet. The shape and the colour, where light, eye and the angle of seeing meet. The scent, where what drifts from the apple meets the nose. None of these belongs to a hidden apple core. The conditions themselves are the appearing.

The apple is designated on a basis, as a chariot is designated on its wheels, axle and frame. Its basis is the colour, the shape, the touch, and what it is for: being tossed, caught and eaten. "Apple" cuts experience into a unit, so that I can toss it, catch it, or eat it. And if I look for the real apple inside the parts, I find only more bases. Cells, molecules, tones of colour, each designated in turn. No bottom brick is ever reached.

Then why does the toss still work? Unfindable as a standalone object, the apple still flies across the room and lands in my friend's hand. This is causal efficacy without causal power. When the conditions meet, the throwing, the flight and the catching happen reliably. No apple-entity is needed. The meeting of conditions is the working, completely.

The thrower is singled out the same way. Anatta is first met as no doer behind seeing: in the seen, just the seen. Dependent arising turns that same seeing onto action. Intention arises, muscles contract, momentum carries, the apple moves. No little me sits inside the head, pulling the levers. The wind blows with no wind apart from the blowing. The throwing happens with no thrower standing behind it.

Look for the hand, and I find fingers, palm, bones, muscles and nerves. The person who throws is designated on the body, sensations, intentions and movement. No ultimate thrower is reached. Thrower, apple, tossing and receiver are designated together, as one event. None comes first.

But nobody disappears. The conventional person, the mere I, is fully there to toss apples, keep promises, and catch the bus.

And the toss tastes different. Centerless: no observer sits at the middle. Effortless: no inner doer grinds away to keep it going. Seamless and intimate: no distance between thrower, apple and receiver. Yet nothing merges. The throw stays the throw, and the catch stays the catch.

The second example is a mountain. Does a mountain exist? The first answer is yes. It is a massive, immovable fact of geology. But look for the mountain among the rocks, the soil, the height, the tree line. It is not among them, and it is not apart from them. It appears vividly, and it reliably stops the climber, only in dependence on its conditions, its parts and the name "mountain". This does not make it imaginary. It was there before anyone named it. But no mountain comes with its edges drawn by nature.

Does a bat see a mountain? A bee? An ant? Ask each the same question, is there a mountain there, and the answers will not match, because the worlds do not match.

I see a bounded shape against the sky, with a summit, slopes and a base. A bat, finding its way by echo, may meet returning sound, with no clean edge where mountain stops. A bee, seeing in ultraviolet among flowers and light, may hardly meet the mountain as one thing at all. An ant takes a day to cross one boulder. It meets ridges of grain, warmth and the scent trails of its nest, and never a mountain.

This is not four creatures looking at one ready-made mountain from four angles. Body and world arise together. A different body singles out a different world.

The usual reply is that the mountain is there all the same, and only the experiences differ. That reply slips in a noun, the mountain, finished before anything meets it. That is the given. If the hidden mountain has a summit and slopes, why do the bat and the ant meet none of them? If it has no features at all, a featureless something cannot be what they all meet.

Nor is measuring a view from nowhere. Metres and maps are a human channel of contact, as reliable as any. When we agree on where the mountain is, it shows that our bodies and instruments are alike, like instruments with the same calibration. It does not show one mountain standing there by itself. And the ant is not cut off: a rockfall can crush it. So it is not one objective mountain with many subjective pictures. The mountain, the one who meets it, and the channel of meeting are designated together. None is the bedrock under the others.

When no one is looking, is the mountain there? Two claims sound almost the same, and are not. "The mountain is still there when I look away and look back" is true. It is a conventional claim, checked the ordinary way: looking again, others seeing it, the road still there to drive on. "The mountain exists from its own side, whether or not anything ever meets it" is a different claim. Nothing has earned it. It slips in because the first claim works so reliably. But reliability is not independence.

That line keeps me out of two mistakes. "The mountain is an illusion", which no one is claiming. And "the mountain exists on its own", which was never shown.

So, from the apple in the palm to the mountain on the horizon, everything is unfindable, and works perfectly all the same.

I am not the first to say it is not straightforward. In the Long Discourses, Ānanda tells the Buddha how wonderful it is. Dependent arising is so deep, and yet to him it seems as plain as can be. The Buddha stops him. "Do not say so, Ānanda. This dependent arising is deep, and it appears deep." From not penetrating it, he says, this generation is tangled like a knotted ball of thread. Ānanda was his closest attendant, and he was told he had not yet understood.

At this point, read my article The Illusion of the Given. The mountain is one case of what it takes apart: the belief that one world is already there, finished, and beings only see it differently. It starts from one bowl of liquid. A human sees water, a hungry ghost sees pus, a fish sees its home, and a god sees nectar. Then come back here.

Now the two words, as the tradition defines them. Dependent arising is arising in dependence on causes and conditions. Applied to suffering, it is the twelve links, from ignorance to ageing and death. In forward order, how suffering comes about. In reverse order, how it ends. Candrakīrti reads the word itself: meeting, or depending, and arising. Madhyamaka widens it to every phenomenon. Nothing arises without depending, so nothing has a nature of its own. Tsongkhapa sets out three levels of dependence: on causes and conditions, on parts, and, the subtlest, on designation.

Designation is naming, by word and concept, on a basis. The Abhidharma used it to sort what exists. A chariot, a person, a forest exist only as designated. The final units, they said, exist substantially. Nāgārjuna takes that floor away. The units are designated too. In one verse he joins the two words: what arises in dependence is emptiness, a designation in dependence, and that is the middle way.

Depending and being designated are one fact, seen from two sides. And "by word and concept" does not put the chariot in the mind. The concept that designates is singled out too.

I keep these definitions. One word needs care: "arising" does not mean production. Conditions meet and this appears, and nothing is born from its own side. In practice, these words open into many key insights. They matter because we do not live in the wisdom of dependent arising. If we did, every experience would be vividly appearing yet unfindable. Centerless, mutual, seamless, effortless and intimate.

And it is stranger than it sounds. Nāgārjuna praises dependent arising as free of eight extremes. No ceasing and no arising, no annihilation and no permanence, no coming and no going, no difference and no identity. What has no nature of its own cannot really come from anywhere, or go anywhere.

What arises in dependence appears vividly, yet is nowhere found. A rainbow appears with brilliant clarity, yet no rainbow-essence is found in the droplets or the light. Vividness and unfindability are not opposites. They are the same fact.

Is long long in itself? No. It is long only beside short, and short only beside long. They make each other.

A subject is a subject only with an object to know. Take away every object: where is the mind? Mind is a word for the knowing side of appearing. It is as empty as what it knows, and it is not the stuff everything is made of.

Then what is cause and effect? We picture a force: A holds a hidden power and pushes it into B. But A and B are not found, and no power passes between them. Things come about in dependable patterns, and nothing standing by itself enforces them. Causal efficacy without causal power. And because things are empty, they can arise, change and work. That does not make emptiness a source. Being empty and arising in dependence are one fact.
