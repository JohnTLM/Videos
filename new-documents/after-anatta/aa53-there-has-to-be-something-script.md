# Dependent Arising Section 3 Part 1: There Has to Be Something

It is raining. And a question comes, almost by itself. What is it, really?

I am John Tan, known as Thusness. These are my own notes, from my own practice and contemplation.

In Section 2, I asked what it is that appears at all. A sound, a colour, a thought. Each was vivid, not found, and dependently arisen. Near the end, mind and matter turned up as two boxes, and neither box was found. This section takes up mind and matter fully.

But before going further, something has to be seen. Not an argument. The mind that will hear every argument. If it is not seen first, everything that follows will be heard through it. And nothing will change.

So, the rain. The sound fills the room, soft and steady. Now louder on the window. Now fading.

What is it, really?

Notice what kind of answer we want. Not "the sound of rain". That is only what appears, and it does not satisfy. We want what it is made of underneath. What it comes down to in the end. We are looking for its ultimate constituent.

And the answers line up.

Matter. It is really air moving, water striking glass, nerves firing. The sound is how matter shows up to us.

Mind. It is really consciousness. Whatever is out there, the sound itself exists only as experienced.

Or a third kind of stuff. It is really energy, vibration, a field. Or, in the older language of China, it is qi. The Song dynasty thinker Zhang Zai taught that all things are qi, condensing and dispersing, the way ice forms from water and melts back into it.

Each of these is held by thoughtful people. And each starts from something true. Sound does depend on air and ears. It is always experienced. It does involve vibration. I do not want to make any of them small.

But look at what they share. Each takes the sound, and asks for the something it is made of. And each stops only when it reaches a stuff that is simply there. The bottom of the matter.

To such a mind, the question cannot rest until it finds what is ultimate. The sound comes and goes. Surely what it is made of does not. The world cannot just be happening, with nothing underneath doing the happening.

So it has to posit something. A ground. A substrate. A stuff. The choice varies. The need does not. Surely, there has to be something.

And this is the important part. We do not experience this as a view. We take it as reality itself. The unquestionable default. Someone who says all is matter, and someone who says all is mind, can argue for a lifetime. They never notice that they agree on the one thing that matters most. That there must be a something. And the only question is which.

Why does it feel so natural? Because the view is there before any question is asked.

We start, without noticing, from a worldview. There must be things, and things must have fixed characteristics, and a nature of their own. And we are given a language that presents the world exactly that way. A subject stands first, and a predicate is said of it. The rose is red. A thing, with a colour attached.

Nietzsche noticed that we say the lightning flashes. As if the lightning were one thing, and its flashing a second thing it does. Is there a lightning apart from the flash?

Even the rain is said this way. It is raining. What is the it?

I will not answer that. Here, the asking matters more than any answer. Look at the question itself, and at the way we ask it.

Is it ever necessary to put it that way, with an it in front of the raining?

Why do we ask in such a manner? As if behind every happening, there must be something doing it.

And must the answer always be a something?

Stay with these, before reaching for a reply. Most of us have never asked them. Because the grammar asked for us. And answered too.

Growing up inside that grammar, we are moulded to think that way, to analyse that way, and to orient ourselves that way. To analyse is to break things into parts, each with its own nature. To explain is to find the thing underneath that does the producing. To orient is to look behind what appears, for what is really there.

None of this means giving language up. The Buddha still said I and you. I still say the rose is red. The words work. What has to change is that they no longer mould what is seen. They can be used, without being believed.

So, before going further, this must be recognised, and clearly seen. Not believed, or disbelieved. Seen. The way you see a pair of tinted glasses, once you notice they are on.

If it is not seen, every step will be heard in the old grammar. Emptiness will sound like a new kind of stuff. The final answer to "what is it, really?" The argument will seem clever, and change nothing.

Here, Buddhism, and the Middle Way of Nāgārjuna above all, does something I find completely new.

Nearly every philosophy, East and West, accepts the question, what is it really, and competes on the answer. Matter. Mind. Energy. God. The One. They disagree about the something. They never doubt there must be one.

The Middle Way does not join the contest. It turns to the question.

It offers no rival something. Nāgārjuna wrote: if I had a thesis, that fault would be mine. Since I have no thesis, I am without fault. As I read him, he holds no thesis about what things ultimately are. Because the search for such a thing is exactly what he is examining.

It examines the something on its own terms. It does not set one view against another. It takes the premises the other person already holds, follows them through, and shows that they cannot stand.

It changes the question. Not, what is it made of? But, dependent on what does this arise, and how is it named?

It does not make emptiness the new answer. Emptiness is not what things are made of. It is the absence of what the question was looking for. Nāgārjuna warns that emptiness, wrongly grasped, harms like a snake picked up by the wrong end.

And it keeps the world. The rain still falls. The umbrella still works. None of it needs a something underneath.

Here I part from one way the Middle Way is often read. As endless negation, taking everything apart until nothing is left. That is not how I see it. Nothing is denied that is actually met. What falls away is only the something the question demanded. And with it, the demand.

So let me take the default apart, a little. This is not seen in one stroke. It is seen gradually. But the shape of what is found is simple. The default fails twice. Once against experience. And once against itself.

First, experience. Go back to the rain. What is actually met? The sound, soft, then louder, then fading. The coolness at the window. The grey light. A thought: it is raining. Each one arises, is vivid, and is gone. And the next one is fresh.

Where, in all of this, is the stuff it is made of? It is never met. Not once. Matter as a stuff, mind as a stuff, energy as a stuff. Each is always one step behind what appears. Inferred. Never found. What is met is always the changing, dependent happening. What is posited is always the unchanging, independent something underneath it. The default asks us to take as most real the one thing that never appears.

Second, the default against itself. It was not made up from nothing. It was derived, from premises that seem obvious. Take those premises seriously, and follow them through.

The first premise. The real is what is left when analysis ends. Then analyse. The table comes down to wood. Wood to molecules. Molecules to atoms. Atoms to particles. Particles to fields. Each floor has a floor below. Vasubandhu asked of the smallest atom: if it has sides, it has parts. If it has no sides, nothing can touch it, and nothing can be built from it. Mind fares the same. A moment of mind comes down to shorter moments, with no partless instant at the bottom. And energy, field and qi are each known only by what they do. A stuff known only by its relations is not a stuff standing on its own.

The second premise. Characteristics need a bearer. Then find the bearer. Strip away every characteristic. Colour, shape, weight, warmth. What is left to bear them? Nothing can be said of it. Not even that it is there. Leave it with its characteristics, and it is no longer the bare bearer. Only more characteristics.

The third premise. The ground must be independent and unchanging. That is what makes it a ground. But look at the job it was posited to do. It was meant to explain a world that changes, arises and passes. Something truly unchanging cannot give rise to anything. If it did, it would change. And something truly independent cannot even be known. Because to be known is already to stand in relation. The ground cannot do the one thing it was posited for.

So the default is not only unfound in experience. Analysed on its own premises, it is contradictory, illogical, and untenable.

What, then, is it? Not reality. A habit. A habitual way of understanding a happening.

A sound happens. The habit supplies something that sounds. A flash happens. The habit supplies the lightning that flashes. A thought happens. The habit supplies the mind that has it. And because the habit is old, shared, and built into how we speak, it feels like the way things are.

Two cautions. This is not "nothing is there". The rain is heard. It is wet. The window is cold. You take an umbrella, and you stay dry. What is not found is only the something underneath. Never the appearing.

And nothing replaces the something. Not mind. Not awareness. Not energy. Not emptiness as a finer stuff. To swap one ground for another is the same habit, still at work.

When the habit is seen for what it is, the insistence that there has to be something begins to loosen. Not because an argument has won. Because it is seen that the question, what is it really, was asking for something that was never needed.

This is Section 3, Part 1 of the Dependent Arising series.

The rain was only ever raining.
