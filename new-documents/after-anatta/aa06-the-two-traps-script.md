# The Two Traps

This moment ceases as it arises. Does it arise, or does it cease?

The koan has two easy answers, and each one is a trap. Say "it arises", and the mind soon finds somewhere for the moment to last. Say "it ceases", and the mind soon decides that nothing really happens. These are eternalism and nihilism.

This is the sixth step of The Logic, from After Anatta: notes by John Tan, known as Thusness. It shows that the two wrong answers grow from one root.

A snake catcher picks up a snake just behind the head, and it is safe in his hand. A beginner grabs it by the tail, and it turns and bites. Same snake, same hand. What decides is the grip. Nagarjuna uses this image for emptiness itself. Seen wrongly, it ruins the one who sees it.

Nagarjuna names the two ends. "It is" is the grasping of permanence. "It is not" is the view of annihilation. The wise take their stand on neither. The Buddha said the world mostly leans on these two ideas. For one who sees the arising of the world, "it is not" does not occur. For one who sees its ceasing, "it is" does not occur. Avoiding both, the Buddha teaches by the middle: dependent arising.

That is the koan's own shape. The trouble starts only when one half is taken on its own, as a fact about an "it".

Take the first trap. The moment arises, so it is there. But it cannot stay. So the mind moves the "it" somewhere that lasts. A timeless Now, that the moments pass through. An awareness in which moments come and go: the Witness. Or a Source, a ground of being behind everything.

The plain form is the soul, a self that lasts. These three are subtler: they survive letting go of the soul. In the author's words: "This was my own position for years after the realisation of I AM. Presence was real and unmistakable, and I held it as something on its own side. The taste was genuine. Turning it into a Self was the mistake."

The test is Step one's. Look for it apart from what it depends on. Awareness is always awareness of something, through something. It is not found standing behind the knowing. A Source, looked for apart from what it is said to produce, is not found either.

Now the second trap. The moment ceases, so it was never really there. Nothing matters, and actions have no results. Nagarjuna's opponent hears "empty" in just this way, and concludes that emptiness destroys the path. Nagarjuna replies that he has misunderstood emptiness itself.

Nihilism has a subtle form too: the disease of relentless negation. It assumes that once every essence is taken apart, wisdom is complete, and appearance is a leftover. But negation removes only the idea that appearance needed an essence. Nothing is taken away from the appearing. The red of the flower and the ring of the bell are empty and vivid at once.

The void is not even coherent. Try to think of absolute nothing, and you think of something: a darkness, a silence, yourself looking on.

Here is the root. "It arises" and "it ceases" disagree about what befalls the moment. They agree that there is a moment for it to befall. Nagarjuna puts it in one verse. "What exists by its own nature does not cease to exist": that is permanence. "It existed before and does not exist now": that brings annihilation. Both start from a thing with a nature of its own. They differ only in what happens to it.

So the void is not the opposite of substance. It is substance in reverse. To be wiped out, something must first have stood on its own. The fear of death shows it. Survival needs a bearer who carries on. Destruction needs a bearer who is wiped out. No bearer was ever found, not at death and not now.

That is why the remedy for one trap is never the other. Tsongkhapa puts the cure in one line: appearance dispels the extreme of existence, and emptiness dispels the extreme of non-existence. The emptiness of things is no void. It is only ever the emptiness of what appears.

Hear it both ways. A sound rings out. With the logic of substance, I must pick: the sound is kept somewhere, or it drops into nothing. With the logic of dependency, the same sound rings, just as vivid. No sound stands behind the sounding, waiting to arise or cease. Nothing is kept, and nothing is lost. It is like the stairs of Step one: up or down only from where the walker stands, and still really climbed.

There is one more place for the snake to bite. Work the koan through, and the moment is not found. Yet that "not found" can itself be grasped by either end. Grasped as something, it becomes the Unfound: a vast openness behind the moments. That is eternalism wearing the name of its cure. Grasped as nothing, it becomes a blank to rest in: "it is all empty anyway". That is nihilism wearing the same name. Nagarjuna warns that those for whom emptiness becomes a view are incurable.

Emptiness is a medicine. Chandrakirti compares it to a purgative. If it works, it clears the blockage, and then leaves the body too. If it stays, it becomes a new illness. So emptiness is always the emptiness of something. It does not lie behind the cup. It is the cup's not being a cup from its own side.

So the middle is not a midpoint, where the moment arises a little and ceases a little. It is what is left when nothing is found to bear either label. Nor is it a thinner world. A harsh word still hurts, and a kind one still helps. Actions still bear fruit. "Not found" never means "does not happen".

The two traps wait wherever an "it" is assumed. The self: one keeps a soul forever, the other says there was never anyone. An object: one says the cup is really there, the other takes it apart and calls it nothing. Meanwhile the cup holds tea. A cause: one makes the seed already the sprout, the other cuts them apart, so a cruel word costs nothing. And a person: held as fixed, forever the one who wronged me, or held as nothing, their pain no longer mattering. No such bearer is found, and the person can really change.

Try it. Listen to a sound and ask: does it arise, or does it cease? Notice where each answer wants to put the moment. Then look for the "it" both answers share. And when "it's all empty anyway" comes up, check whether cause and result now matter less to you. If so, the snake is held by the tail.
