# Function Without Substance

The koan of Step 1 ends with no moment found that arises, and no moment found that ceases. That leaves a worry, and a fair one. If no moment arises or ceases, how does anything happen? How is a sound heard? How does an action bear fruit?

This is the seventh step of The Logic, from After Anatta: notes by John Tan, known as Thusness. Its answer is not that emptiness does the work. It is that function never needed an "it".

Two opposite conclusions are drawn from the koan. One person reasons: sounds are heard, so there must be a real moment of sound that really arises and really ceases. Another finds no such moment, and reasons: then nothing happens, and the sound is a kind of illusion. But they share one premise: that happening needs an "it" for the happening to happen to. They only argue about whether the "it" is there.

Drop that premise, and both conclusions go. A suitcase is heavy. The clock keeps time. And there is no "it" behind the working. Each works as designated, in dependence on what it is picked out with. And it works reliably, which is all that working ever was.

Seen with the logic of substance, I lift a suitcase, and the heaviness of this moment feels like a thing in its own right. Its power to pull sits inside the case. So when the koan finds no moment, it looks as if the weight has gone too.

Seen with the logic of dependency, it is the same suitcase. Case, earth, arm and attention come together, and "it got heavy" is said. The case is set down, and "it stopped" is said. The pull on the arm is complete. Nothing in it was waiting for an "it" to carry it out. It is like the stairs of Step 1: not up or down in themselves, yet really climbed.

We habitually treat function as a power inside the thing, like a knife's power to cut. We treat a moment the same way, as if it held the next one inside it.

Look for the power, and it is not found there. A key opens this lock and no other. In the wrong lock, it opens nothing. A power that shows itself only with its partner is not an inner property. Key, lock, hand and turning are named together, and the door opens. The moment is no different. The sound came from no store, and went into none.

Now turn the koan round. Suppose this moment existed by itself, complete from its own side. Could it arise or cease? It could not. Arising and ceasing are both a becoming other, and an own-nature is exactly what does not become other. Such a moment could not change, combine or act. Conditions could not reach it, and it could not reach anything. It would be sealed.

Nagarjuna makes the point with a baker. If the baker existed on his own, already complete, he would not need to bake to be one: a baker who never bakes. Instead, the agent proceeds in dependence on the action, and the action in dependence on the agent. An action with its own nature would be permanent, so it could never have been done. Yet actions are done, and they bear fruit.

A critic objects: if everything is empty, nothing arises and nothing passes away. Then there are no four noble truths, no practice, no fruits. Nagarjuna answers that the picture is backwards. If things were not empty, there would be no arising and no passing away. It is own-nature, not emptiness, that breaks the chain. A world of own-natures would be fixed like an anvil. Not a solid world, a frozen one. Between the objection and the answer stands the line most often quoted: for whom emptiness fits, everything fits.

But this does not make emptiness a power that lets things happen, or an open space they happen in. Emptiness causes nothing. It is only what dependence comes to when it is examined. The same goes for "potential". A store of possibilities behind appearances is a substance again. A rope can be seen as a snake in dim light. A shadow cannot be walked across as a bridge.

Empty is not flimsy. A car with no essence can still hurt you. A disc of red light stops a forty-tonne truck without touching it. Take the designation away, and it is a coloured lamp. Yet nothing is more effective. Empty things act on empty things, and that is the only acting there is.

Come back to the koan's words. "This moment ceases as it arises" sounds like a moment to which arising and ceasing happen. But there is no lightning apart from the flashing. The flashing is the lightning. In the same way, the arising and ceasing is the moment. The clock still ticks. Only the passenger is let go.

The author says this was where his own view lagged behind. After 1997 the doer had gone. Walking simply walked, hearing simply heard. Yet for years he still imagined an ear here and a sound there, as if things existed first and then met. In the author's words: seeing that the participants are not found before the participation is what let the working of the world make sense. In his seven phases, this is phase six.

Then dependent arising is no longer a secondary layer, an illusion laid over the real thing. There is no real thing underneath. Nothing in it comes first. This is reality without a ground: vivid, working, and not found standing on its own. Nor is dependent arising a new ground.

Dogen turns over an old Zen saying, that a painted rice cake does not satisfy hunger. In his words: "there is no remedy for satisfying hunger other than a painted rice-cake." There is no unpainted cake behind the painting. And the painted cake feeds.

Knock on a table: nothing feels more solid. Yet at the scale of atoms it is almost all empty space. A neutrino passes straight through it, and through the whole earth. The solidity is not a stuff packed in the wood. It is the table and the hand together. And still the cup stands on it. Weight is the same: a suitcase is a sixth as heavy on the Moon, and floats in orbit. Its heaviness is named with the earth that pulls it, as the key is named with its lock.

The same logic runs beyond the moment. A self decides, and the deciding is real, yet no decider is found before the decision. A chair holds you, yet no chair-power is found in the wood. A seed sprouts with soil, water and warmth, but not in a dry jar; no sprout was stored inside it. And the mere I, found nowhere, keeps a promise for forty years. Nothing about the person is anchored, and everything a person does still counts.

So, try it. Listen to a single sound until it ends. Ask the koan: does it arise, or does it cease? Then notice that, while no "it" is found, the sound was heard in full.

Hold a key, and look for the opening in it. Then use it. The opening happens, and it was never found in the metal alone.

And if the thought comes that emptiness is what makes things possible, look for that emptiness apart from the things. Nothing is found doing the work. And the sound is still heard.
