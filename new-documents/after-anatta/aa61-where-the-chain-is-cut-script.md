# Dependent Arising Section 5 Part 3: Where the Chain Is Cut

A painful feeling is the first arrow. What we do with it is the second.

I am John Tan, known as Thusness. These are my own notes, from my own practice and contemplation.

Part 1 set out the twelve links, and traced them through one afternoon. Part 2 asked who goes round the wheel, and found the tradition's answer. Not a valid question. Karma and rebirth kept, and no one in the chain who goes round.

The twelve links come in two orders. So far I have mostly followed the first. Here I take up the second. How the chain stops, in the tradition's own terms. This part closes Section 5.

The discourses give it as plainly as the forward order. With the fading away and ending of ignorance, formations end. With the ending of formations, consciousness ends. And so on, to the end. Such is the ending of this whole mass of suffering.

So the chain does not have to be broken link by link, as if one had to defeat birth, then becoming, then clinging, each in turn. Each link stands only on its condition. Remove the condition, and what stood on it does not arise. When this is not, that is not.

The tradition points to two places where the chain can be met.

At the start, ignorance. In the discourses, ignorance is defined exactly. Not knowing suffering. Not knowing its arising. Not knowing its ending. Not knowing the way to its ending. So it ends by knowing those four. This is the work of insight.

And in the middle, feeling and craving. Contact brings feeling. Feeling is followed by craving. More of the pleasant. An end to the painful. Then clinging, and then a whole world built around it. This is one of the three joints of the chain. The point where what is met turns into what is done.

I see these as two sides of one cut. In the middle, the step from feeling to craving is where the chain shows itself most clearly. At the start, knowing what that step really is, and that it leads to suffering, is what lets it go.

The Buddha gives an image for the middle.

When an untaught person is touched by a painful feeling, they grieve. They lament. They feel two pains, the bodily and the mental. It is as if they were struck by one arrow, and then by a second.

When a taught disciple is touched by a painful feeling, they feel one pain. The bodily one, and not the mental. Struck by the first arrow, they are not struck by the second.

Notice what this does not say. It does not say the wise feel no pain. The first arrow still lands. What ends is the second arrow. The craving. The pushing away. The story of the one who was hurt.

So the cut is not numbness. One can try to stop feeling, to dull the senses, to stay above the world. That is not the reverse order. The reverse order leaves the world vivid. And removes only what was being added to it.

How does ignorance end? In the discourses, by seeing. Seeing the arising and passing away of body, feeling, perception, formations and consciousness. Again and again. Until it is plain that what arises and passes cannot be held as mine, or I, or my self.

That has a consequence I want to state plainly. Ignorance does not end by stopping thought.

A quiet, wordless mind can be very pleasant. The tradition values it highly, as the ground for insight. But thought is not the problem. The habit of fixing is. Taking what appears to stand on its own. The speaker. The words. The hurt. The one hurt.

A quiet mind does not end that habit by being quiet. When thinking starts again, the habit is still there, waiting. What ends it is seeing.

Such is the ending of this whole mass of suffering. It is easy to hear this as the end of everything. No more feeling, no more world, a blank.

The discourses do not say that. The one who has seen through still sees, still hears, still feels. The second arrow is what has gone. Asked what nirvāṇa is, one monk answers simply. The ending of becoming. What ends is the taking on of an identity, to be defended and renewed. Not the bell ringing when struck. Not the seed giving rice.

And when a wanderer asks where the awakened one goes after death, the Buddha asks him a question back. When a fire goes out, in which direction has it gone? East, west, north or south? It does not apply, the wanderer says. It went out because its fuel was used up.

The ending of suffering is not a thing being destroyed. And not someone going somewhere.

There is one more teaching I want to include, because it is easily forgotten.

In one discourse, the Buddha gives the chain, and then keeps going past suffering. With suffering as condition, there is faith. With faith, gladness. Then joy, calm, happiness, and a settled mind. Then seeing things as they are. Then turning away, letting go, and freedom.

The same sentence runs both ways. When this is, that is, describes how suffering builds. And also how freedom builds. The path is not outside dependent arising. It is dependent arising, arranged the other way.

Return to the afternoon of Part 1. Someone says something harsh to me at work.

There is contact. The words, the ear, the knowing of them. There is feeling, unpleasant and sharp. The first arrow lands, and that is fine. Then the familiar pull begins, toward an apology, toward being shown right.

But this time it is seen. Not suppressed. Not argued with. Seen as what it is. A feeling arising and passing. A pull arising and passing. With no owner among them.

The pull does not find much to hold. No clinging. No injured party taking shape. No grievance born, to be rehearsed in the car.

The words were still heard. The feeling was still felt. Nothing was taken from the afternoon.

This is Section 5, Part 3 of the Dependent Arising series, and the close of Section 5.

What changed is only that the chain was watched, instead of obeyed.
