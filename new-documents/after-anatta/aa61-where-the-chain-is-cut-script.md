# Dependent Arising Section 5 Part 3: Where the Chain Is Cut

A painful feeling is the first arrow. What we do with it is the second.

I am John Tan, known as Thusness. These are my own notes, from my own practice and contemplation.

Part 1 set out the twelve links, and traced them through one afternoon. Part 2 read them with Nāgārjuna. Karma and rebirth kept, and only the owner left out. The maker of the round is not a person, but not knowing.

The twelve links come in two orders, and so far I have mostly followed the first. Here I take up the second. How the chain stops. This part closes Section 5.

When ignorance has ceased, formations do not arise. And ignorance ceases through cultivating this very knowledge. By the ceasing of each link, the next does not arise.

So the chain does not have to be broken link by link, as if one had to defeat birth, then becoming, then clinging, each in turn. Remove the first condition. And the rest, losing their conditions one after another, do not arise.

That follows from everything in this series. If each link had a nature of its own, each would have to be destroyed separately. And none could be. Because each is only what its conditions make it, the ending of a condition is enough.

The tradition points to two places where the chain can be met.

At the start, ignorance. Ignorance ends through knowing, and with it, the formations that ignorance makes. This is the work of insight.

And in the middle, feeling and craving. Contact brings feeling. Feeling is followed by craving. More of the pleasant. An end to the painful. Then clinging, and then a whole world built around it.

Nāgārjuna adds an escape, right in the middle of his chain. If one did not grasp, one would be freed.

I see these as two sides of one cut. In the middle, the step from feeling to craving is where the chain shows itself most clearly. At the start, seeing what that step really is, a meeting of conditions with no one behind it, is what lets it go.

The Buddha gives an image for the middle.

When an untaught person is touched by a painful feeling, they grieve. They lament. They feel two pains, the bodily and the mental. It is as if they were struck by one arrow, and then by a second.

When a taught disciple is touched by a painful feeling, they feel one pain. The bodily one, and not the mental. Struck by the first arrow, they are not struck by the second.

Notice what this does not say. It does not say the wise feel no pain. The first arrow still lands. What ends is the second arrow. The craving. The pushing away. The story of the one who was hurt.

So the cut is not numbness. One can try to stop feeling, to dull the senses, to stay above the world. That is not the reverse order. The reverse order leaves the world vivid. And removes only what was being added to it.

How does ignorance end? Through cultivating this very knowledge. Candrakīrti reads this as seeing dependent arising as it really is, which is seeing its emptiness.

That has a consequence I want to state plainly. Ignorance does not end by stopping thought.

A quiet, wordless mind can be very pleasant. But thought is not the problem. The habit of fixing is. Taking what appears to exist from its own side. The speaker. The words. The hurt. The one hurt.

A quiet mind does not end that habit by being quiet. When thinking starts again, the habit is still there, waiting.

What ends it is seeing. Elaboration stops in emptiness. Not in silence. In emptiness. In seeing that what elaboration keeps fixing was never fixed.

The whole mass of suffering comes to an end. It is easy to hear this as the end of everything. No more feeling, no more world, a blank.

That is not what is said. What ends is a mass of suffering, which has nothing else in it. Appearances do not end. The bell still rings when struck. The seed still gives rice.

Nāgārjuna is direct. Nothing at all sets the round of birth and death apart from nirvāṇa. Between the two, not even the finest gap is found.

They are not two places, one to leave and one to reach. What ends was never a thing. It was a way of taking things.

There is one more teaching I want to include, because it is easily forgotten.

In one discourse, the Buddha gives the chain, and then keeps going past suffering. With suffering as condition, there is faith. With faith, gladness. Then joy, calm, happiness, and a settled mind. Then seeing things as they are. Then turning away, letting go, and freedom.

The same sentence runs both ways. When this is, that is, describes how suffering builds. And also how freedom builds. The path is not outside dependent arising. It is dependent arising, arranged the other way.

Return to the afternoon of Part 1. Someone says something harsh to me at work.

There is contact. The words, the ear, the knowing of them. There is feeling, unpleasant and sharp. The first arrow lands, and that is fine. Then the familiar pull begins, toward an apology, toward being shown right.

But this time it is seen. Not suppressed. Not argued with. Seen as what it is. A meeting of conditions. The words, a tired colleague, my own old readiness, all arising together, with no one at the centre for it to belong to.

The pull does not find much to hold. No clinging. No injured party taking shape. No grievance born, to be rehearsed in the car.

The words were still heard. The feeling was still felt. Nothing was taken from the afternoon.

This is Section 5, Part 3 of the Dependent Arising series, and the close of Section 5.

What changed is only that the chain was watched, instead of obeyed.
