One morning I noticed something simple. When I focus too hard on mental work, I feel a lack of chee. It is as if thinking itself uses the chee up.

Then I exercise for half an hour, and the chee is strong again. I felt it had something to do with blood circulation.

I am John Tan, known as Thusness. These are my own notes, from my own practice and my own checking.

So I asked a second question. Would it be better to move three times a day, for a short time each? Once in the morning. Once at lunch. Once in the evening, after dinner. The idea was simple. Keep the blood flowing. Keep the energy up. Clear the blockage that builds when I concentrate for too long.

I put both questions to an AI. Later I asked another AI tool to check the first answer against the research and the classical texts. Then I checked both myself.

What is left after that checking is short. The observation holds. The three-session plan holds too. But the most popular explanation of why it works is wrong. And the correction is more interesting than the original story.

Here is the popular story. The first answer told it neatly. The brain is only about two percent of the body's weight, yet it uses about twenty percent of the body's energy. So when I think hard, it said, the body sends a massive amount of sugar, oxygen and blood straight to the head. That drains the rest of the system. Blood and chee pool in the upper body.

It sounds right. It matches the feeling of a heavy, full head. But most of it does not hold up.

Start with the numbers. The brain does use about a fifth of the body's energy. But it uses that much all the time. At rest. Even in sleep. Hard thinking adds only a few percent on top. Solving a difficult problem costs the brain little more than daydreaming does.

And the body holds blood flow to the brain steady. Tightly. The brain is not allowed to take a big share of blood from the limbs just because I am concentrating. So thinking does not drain the body's blood.

Now the second part of the story. That blood and chee pool in the head. Here the truth runs the other way.

In 2018, Sophie Carter and her colleagues measured blood flow in a main artery of the brain. People sat for four hours without a break. Over those hours, blood flow to the brain went down. A little, but clearly. When the legs do not move, blood returns to the heart more slowly. It settles in the lower body.

In the same study, a two-minute easy walk every half hour stopped the drop completely.

So the heavy head and sluggish legs I feel after a long morning at the desk do not come from too much blood up top. They come from too little movement below. And from something happening inside the brain itself.

That is the third part. The common idea is that a tired brain has burned up its sugar. Run out of fuel. The research points somewhere else.

In 2022, Antonius Vee-ler and his colleagues in Paris scanned people's brains at the start and the end of a long working day. Those who did hard mental tasks all day had more glutamate in the front of the brain by evening. Glutamate is a chemical the brain uses to pass signals. These people also made more short-term, impulsive choices as the day ended.

The researchers suggest the brain slows its own effortful control when glutamate builds up. A kind of protective brake. That is their hypothesis, not a proven fact. And the study was small.

But it fits my experience better than running out of fuel. It feels less like an empty tank. More like a jam.

The first answer added one more image. Exercise, it said, flushes these byproducts out of the brain. It is a nice image. But I found no study showing a short workout washing glutamate out of the brain.

What is shown is narrower. Short walking breaks keep blood flow to the brain from falling. And exercise helps attention and mood. I keep the benefit, and drop the flushing story.

One more correction. Short, frequent breaks are clearly better at one thing. They break up long sitting. That is my problem. But it does not make one long workout worse in general. For fitness, both count. The three-session plan is the right tool for someone who thinks at a desk all day. It is not a law for everyone.

Now the classical side. Here I found the best anchor of all.

The Yellow Emperor's Inner Classic is the oldest classic of Chinese medicine. In the part called Soo-wen, chapter thirty-nine lists nine ways that states of mind change the chee. On thinking, it says four words: thinking knots the chee.

Then it explains. When one thinks, the heart holds on to something, and the spirit gathers there. The upright chee stays, and does not move. So the chee is knotted.

This is precise. And it agrees with the modern findings better than the popular story does. The text does not say thinking burns up the chee. It says thinking makes the chee stay, and not move. Stuck, not empty.

That matches sitting still while blood settles in the legs. It matches a brain that is jammed rather than out of fuel.

The AI report also had the classics wrong in places. It placed the saying "thinking injures the Spleen" in the Ling-shoo, chapter eight. The saying is from the Soo-wen, chapter five. The Ling-shoo says something close but different: when worry in the Spleen is not resolved, it injures the intention.

The report also said the old classic teaches that a weak Spleen fails to make Heart Blood, so the spirit cannot settle at night. That idea belongs to later Chinese medicine. It may be useful. But it should carry its own date.

And it used a saying from chee-gong and tie-jee-chwan: where the mind goes, the chee goes. In practice, that is a principle. You guide the chee with attention. Reading it as a cause of illness is the report's own idea, not the tradition's.

There is a sound point inside it, though. If attention sits in the head for hours, everything gathers there. Nothing moves below. Chinese medicine has a name for this pattern: excess above, deficiency below. As a description of how it feels, it fits well. As a claim about blood piling up in the skull, it does not.

The Taoist tradition speaks of three treasures. Jing, essence. Qi, breath and energy. And shen, spirit and mind. Inner alchemy treats them as one life force at different degrees of fineness. Its classic path refines essence into chee, and chee into spirit.

A teaching image I find useful is a candle. Jing is the wax and the wick: the body's deep reserves and basic health. Qi is the flame: breath, circulation, the living movement. Shen is the light: awareness, focus, thinking.

Read this way, a long morning of hard thinking is a bright light with a flame that is not being fed, because the body is not moving. The spirit gathers in one place, and the chee stops moving. Exercise feeds the flame, and moves it again.

I keep this as a description, not a mechanism. Blood flow, glutamate and sitting are what happens in the body. Jing, chee and shen are a way of feeling and naming the whole of it from the inside. The two do not compete.

And chee is not a hidden stuff. In its first meaning, chee was vapour and breath. It is not a substance standing behind warmth, movement and alertness. When I say my chee is weak, I mean the body feels heavy and slow, and the mind feels dull. When I say my chee is strong, I mean the reverse. The words point to how the body is working. Not to a fluid that thinking drains away.

So here is the plan I asked about, with what the checking adds.

In the morning, movement wakes the body and gets the blood going before the day's desk work. Feed the flame before the light is asked to shine.

At lunch, a walk breaks up a long morning of sitting, and brings blood flow to the brain back up. It unties the knot that thinking tied.

In the evening, an easy walk after dinner lowers the rise in blood sugar after a meal, because working muscles take up sugar on their own. Kept gentle, it helps the body wind down for sleep. The chee comes down from the head to the belly, so the spirit can rest and the essence can recover overnight.

The checking added one more piece. Brain blood flow drops when sitting goes on for hours without a break. So three sessions may not be enough on their own during a long morning of writing. Getting up for a short walk in between matters too. The study tested two minutes, every half hour.

And one thing I dropped. The report said morning exercise boosts the rise in cortisol that comes after waking. I could not find good support for that. So it is not in my plan.

A last word on checking AI answers. The first answer was warm and fluent. It called my observation profoundly accurate, and my plan perfect. But its central explanation, blood rushing to the head, was wrong. Praise is not proof.

The second, a research report, was better. It corrected the blood-flow story and cited two good studies. But it also misplaced classical quotations. It stretched one study further than it went. And it listed several sources I could not trace at all. One was called a peer-reviewed preprint. A preprint is a paper before peer review.

What survived the checking is small, but solid. My own experience. One line from the Soo-wen. And two studies.

That is enough.
