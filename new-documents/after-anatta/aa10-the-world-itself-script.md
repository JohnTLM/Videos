# The World Itself

Watch a whirlpool in a river. It has a shape you could sketch, and a place you could point to. Children throw leaves into it, to see them spin. Yet not one drop of its water stays for more than a moment. Ask what the whirlpool is, apart from the water passing through it, and nothing is found. Ask whether it is therefore unreal, and the leaves go on spinning.

This is one of the question pages of After Anatta: notes by John Tan, known as Thusness. Its question is about the whole world, not one whirlpool. If nothing is the ground floor, what is the world standing on?

What is the world made of, finally? Debates about mind, matter and physics often stall at the same place. Each side assumes that something must be fundamental. They argue only about which. Every picture of the world seems to need a bottom layer. This page puts each candidate for that layer through the test, and asks what is left when none of them is found.

Start with the substance reading. It pictures a building with two storeys. Upstairs is the world we live in: cups, people, colours, names. Downstairs is what is simply there, standing on nothing else, and holding it all up. The candidates for the ground floor change with the century. Atoms, or energy. A universal mind. An empty space that would exist with nothing in it. Or, more subtly, a field or web of relations, out of which things are made.

The tradition looked at these candidates one by one. Nāgārjuna, in the fifth chapter of his Middle Way verses, takes space. Space cannot exist before its mark, the absence of obstruction. And the mark cannot exist without something it marks. So space and what is located in it are designated together. There is no empty box waiting first. The books call that picture the container myth: space as a box that would exist with nothing in it.

Candrakīrti takes the mind. Conventionally, he says, outer objects exist as much as the mind that knows them. Ultimately, neither is found. To deny the objects and keep the mind would make mind the last substance.

And Tsongkhapa names the mark of the whole view. Nothing is established by its own characteristic, not even conventionally.

Three voices, one point. Space, the mind, a mark of its own: each is looked for as the floor, and none is found. And nothing they say takes the world away.

Now run the four questions of Step 8 on the world.

The first question. What would a substance view say? Beneath the world of cups and people lies something that is simply there: matter, mind, energy, space, or a web of relations. The world is built on it.

The second question. What does dependency say instead? Each candidate meets the same search, and none is found standing on its own. Mind is known only with its objects. Space is named only with what it holds. A relation needs what it relates. And dependence is not the new floor either. Things and their links are designated together, and neither comes first. The world is seamless, but not fused into one stuff.

The third question. What is affirmed conventionally? The whole working world. Cups and rivers. Inside and outside, near and far. The laws of physics, as some of the most reliable designations we have. A wall still stops a hand. A friend's thoughts are still their own, and not yours to read.

The fourth question. What is never found? A ground floor. Not matter, mind, energy, space or a field. And not emptiness either. Emptiness is not a void beneath things, and not a fullness within them.

Even the subtlest floor fails. Buddhist logicians such as Dharmakīrti kept one: momentary particulars, met by perception before any concept. They count as real because they alone can do something. The books call this the Given: something met before any designation, as a floor beneath concepts. Candrakīrti answers that what the world perceives is the pot, not a bare patch of colour. The pot is met as a pot from the start.

And doing something is no floor either. A key's power to open is found only together with the lock, the hand and the turning. Working is itself a mark of dependence, as Step 7 shows. When the particular is not found, the two-storey building has no ground floor. Seeing is not a lower layer, and concept is not a top coat.

Then why is the world orderly? Barley seeds grow barley, and fire burns. The regularity is dependent arising itself. It needs no hidden power to make it hold. No hidden force sits inside the seed, pushing. Seed, soil, water and season arise together, and barley comes, reliably. The page on Cause and Effect takes this further.

And the last question people reach for: "But why is there anything at all?" That question expects one last ground. When none is found, the question stops applying. It is not answered. It falls away.

What remains is not a final picture of reality, but freedom from needing one. There is no deeper level beneath appearances, and no higher level above them. The world as it appears and works is fully valid. It is the only "real" there is.

There are two traps. The first is too much: eternalism. "Fine, not matter. But energy, or pure awareness, or the web of interdependence, is what is really there." The habit retreats to a new floor, and calls it progress. The author knows this trap from the inside. In his words: "Before 1997, Presence was that floor for me: the one reality behind everything."

The second is too little: nihilism. "No floor, so the world is unreal, a void." But absolute nothingness cannot be posited, any more than substance can. The ice is cold. The chariot carries passengers.

So, try it. Take a cup. Notice its colour, its weight, its warmth. Then look for the cup. In the clay. In the handle. In the shape. In the word. Then drink from it. The tea is still warm. Nothing was lost in the looking.

When the looking finds nothing, watch for the next floor. "But energy is real." "But awareness is real." Put that through the same test.

And one more. Stand at a window at dusk, when the glass shows the room and the street at once. Notice that inside and outside are drawn in the seeing. And they are still useful. The glass does not divide two worlds. There is one world, without walls, and it still has a room and a street.

Back to the river. Look beneath the whirlpool for a floor of its own, and none is found. Look beneath the world, and none is found there either. Nothing is missing. And the leaves go on spinning.
