# Does It Arise or Cease?

This moment ceases as it arises. Does it arise, or does it cease?

A koan is not a riddle with a clever answer. It is a question built to catch the habit in the act. Before any answer, the question has given us an "it". A moment, that must either arise or cease.

This is the koan page of The Logic, from After Anatta: notes by John Tan, known as Thusness. It works the koan with the logic of substance, then with the logic of dependency, then through Nagarjuna's five core logics.

First, what the koan points at. Listen to a bell, or a car going by. At no point is there a sound that stays. The sound is already passing as it sounds. The arising is already the ceasing.

The ordinary picture is different. Things arise, stay for a while, and then cease. The early Buddhist schools made this exact: arising, abiding and ceasing. The picture needs the middle one. Only something that abides can be the "it" that first arises and later ceases. The koan quietly removes the abiding. And then asks its question about the "it" anyway.

The logic of substance starts with things, and asks what happens to them. So it takes "this moment" as a thing, and arising and ceasing as what happen to it. That is the grammar of the sentence, subject then verb, taken for the shape of the world.

Given a thing, the question must be answered. There are four answers, and each one fails.

It arises. But it ceases as it arises, so it is never there to have arisen. And if it stays even for an instant, that instant splits again, into what is gone and what is not yet.

It ceases. But what passes away, if it never stood?

Both. But coming into being and passing away are opposites, like light and dark. They cannot belong to one thing at one time.

Neither. Then nothing happens at all. Yet a sound is being heard right now.

Nagarjuna puts the whole trap in one verse. There is no passing away, either apart from coming into being, or together with it.

So the logic of substance looks for a way out. Each way out keeps the "it", and moves it somewhere else. A timeless Now, in which the moments pass. An awareness behind the moments, which stays. A pure flow. Point instants. Or nothing happens, which keeps the "it" and declares it absent. All five are the same logic, one step further on.

The logic of dependency starts somewhere else. It asks how "this moment" is picked out at all. A moment is designated in dependence on its arising and its ceasing. Arising is designated against ceasing, and ceasing against arising. None comes first. None is found apart from the others. And there is no one thing for them to belong to.

Then "ceases as it arises" is not a paradox. It is a plain description. There is no "it" standing between the arising and the ceasing. When the "it" is looked for and not found, the question is not answered. It falls away.

Nothing is lost. Sounds are still heard. Clocks still keep time. The firewood still burns. Just as the flashing is the lightning, the arising and ceasing is the moment. There is change, vivid and exact, and no changer behind it.

Now the five core logics. Each searches a different hiding place of the "it".

One, the four corners: its source. Does the moment arise from itself, from another, from both, or from no cause? Each fails. Seen with dependency, the moment needs no source. It is designated with its conditions, the moment before among them. Nothing passes over, and nothing is missing.

Two, same or different: its identity. If arising and ceasing are the same, nothing ever comes to be. If different, something must stand between them. Like fire and fuel, they are not one and not two.

Three, the three times: its present. What has arisen is not arising. What has not arisen is not arising. And no now arising is found between them. The moment is met, vividly, without a present that holds it.

Four, infinite regress: its marks. If arising is a real event, it needs its own arising, and that needs another, for ever. Seen as a designation, there is no regress. In Nagarjuna's words, what is arising is at peace, and so is arising itself.

Five, mutual dependence: its standing on its own. All four failed for one reason. Each needed something established on its own first. Dependent arising is what we call emptiness. Emptiness is not a thing behind the moment. It is how the moment is: dependent, and not found on its own.

Each logic lands in the same place. No "it" is found, and the moment is still here.

So, does it arise or cease? Two answers can be given, and they do not conflict. Conventionally, it arises and ceases. The sound starts and ends. We can say when the bell was struck, and when the ringing stopped. Nothing in the analysis touches this.

Looked for, nothing that arises and nothing that ceases is found. Nagarjuna opens his whole work this way: without ceasing, without arising. That is not a denial that things appear. It is said of dependent arising itself. Not two worlds, but one moment, seen two ways.

So the koan is not answered by choosing a corner. It is answered when the "it" is seen never to have been there. Then each moment is complete where it is. Nothing carried over, nothing left behind. Dogen says it of firewood and ash: each with a before and an after, yet cut off from them. Dzogchen calls it self-release. A thought, seen as unfixed, releases as it arises, like a drawing on water.

Three insights meet here. The moment is vivid, and not found. It appears when bell, air, ear and attention come together, from no hidden place. And the "it" is the habit at work, carried in by the question.

Why does it matter? If the moment is an "it", every experience is something to keep or to lose. A clear moment must be held on to. A painful one must be got rid of. Craving and fear follow from the "it", as surely as the paradox does.

Seen without the "it", there is nothing to keep, and nothing to lose. This is not indifference. The moment is met fully, because it is not being held on to.

So, try it. Listen to a single sound until it ends. When did it arise? When did it cease? Look for a sound between the two, apart from the sounding.

Watch a thought come and go. Ask where it went. Then notice the question assumes a thought that went somewhere.

And the koan, once more. This moment ceases as it arises. Does it arise, or does it cease? Ask what arises, and it is a paradox. See that no "it" is found, and the koan is simply this moment.
