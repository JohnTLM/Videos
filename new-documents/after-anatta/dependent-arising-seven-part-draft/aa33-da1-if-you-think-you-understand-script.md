# If You Think You Understand

"If you think you understand quantum mechanics, you don't understand quantum mechanics." Richard Feynman is said to have quipped that. The same trap waits here. If you think you understand dependent arising, you don't.

I am John Tan, known as Thusness. These are my own notes, from my own practice and contemplation. This is the first of seven parts on dependent arising.

On paper it sounds straightforward, even safe. When this is, that is. From the arising of this, that arises. When this is not, that is not. From the ceasing of this, that ceases. Things arise from many causes and conditions, and nothing has an essence of its own.

It reads like ordinary cause and effect. It fits easily onto the everyday picture of a world out there. But is it really that straightforward? Take two ordinary examples, and look.

The first is an apple. When I toss an apple and catch it, I feel I am handling one solid, independent object. It has edges, weight and speed. By default the mind takes it as solid, out there, there on its own whether anyone looks or not.

Pause here. Toss an apple, or just picture one.

Now ask: where exactly is this solid, independent apple? Is it the red I see? The press on my palm? The weight? The arc through the air? Is it any one of these, all of them together, or something behind them? Look, and see what is actually found.

What is found is many appearings, met together. Shapes and colours, where light, eye and the angle of seeing meet. The press and the density, where skin, nerve and resistance meet. The scent, where what drifts from the fruit meets the nose. They are gathered as one apple by a naming so quick and smooth that it goes unnoticed. Behind them, no apple is found.

And the apple flies, lands in my friend's hand, and is eaten. Hold that for now. How it works, with nothing found behind it, is what the next parts are about.

The second example is a mountain. Does a mountain exist? The first answer is yes. It is a massive, immovable fact of geology. But look for the mountain among the rocks, the soil, the height, the tree line. It is not among them, and it is not apart from them. It appears vividly, and it reliably stops the climber, with its conditions, its parts and the name mountain. This does not make it imaginary. But no mountain comes with its edges drawn by nature.

Does a bat see a mountain? A bee? An ant? Ask each, is there a mountain there, and the answers will not match, because the worlds do not match.

I see a bounded shape against the sky, with a summit, slopes and a base. A bat, finding its way by echo, meets returning sound, with no clean edge where mountain stops. A bee, seeing in ultraviolet among flowers, may hardly meet the mountain as one thing at all. An ant takes a day to cross one boulder. It meets ridges of grain, warmth and the scent trails of its nest, and never a mountain.

This is not four creatures looking at one ready-made mountain from four angles. Body and world arise together. A different body singles out a different world.

The usual reply is that the mountain is there all the same, and only the experiences differ. That reply slips in a mountain finished before anything meets it. If that hidden mountain has a summit and slopes, why do the bat and the ant meet none of them? If it has no features at all, a featureless something cannot be what they all meet.

Nor is measuring a view from nowhere. Metres and maps are a human channel of contact, as reliable as any. When we agree on where the mountain is, it shows that our bodies and instruments are alike. It does not show one mountain standing there by itself.

When no one is looking, is the mountain there? Two claims sound almost the same, and are not. The mountain is still there when I look away and look back: that is true, and checked the ordinary way. The mountain exists from its own side, whether or not anything ever meets it: that is a different claim. Nothing has earned it. It slips in because the first claim works so reliably. But reliability is not independence.

My article The Illusion of the Given takes this apart in full. It starts from one bowl of liquid. A human sees water, a hungry ghost sees pus, a fish sees its home, and a god sees nectar.

Here is the turn I had to make, and keep making. The things, the substrates, and everything I took as given, undeniable and there from its own side, turn out to be frameworks. I built them. And I grew so used to them that I never questioned them.

This is not mainly a theory anyone chooses to believe. It is an innate, default way of perceiving. It assumes that separate things exist first, from their own side, and only afterwards enter into relations. Because the habit is so reliable, the act of naming disappears into what is named, and a convention feels like a fact.

Modern physics points in a similar direction. Again and again, what was supposed to be the bottom layer turns out to be defined by interaction and relation, rather than by fixed properties of its own. But this view does not rest on physics, and does not need it as proof. Physical theories are themselves valid frameworks. Reliable within their domains. Open to revision.

I am not the first to say it is not straightforward. In the Long Discourses, Ānanda tells the Buddha how wonderful it is. Dependent arising is so deep, and yet to him it seems as plain as can be. The Buddha stops him. Do not say so, Ānanda. This dependent arising is deep, and it appears deep. From not penetrating it, he says, this generation is tangled like a knotted ball of thread.

Now the two words, as the tradition defines them.

Dependent arising is arising in dependence on causes and conditions. Applied to suffering, it is the twelve links, from ignorance to ageing and death. In forward order, how suffering comes about. In reverse order, how it ends. Madhyamaka, the Middle Way school, widens it to every phenomenon. Nothing arises without depending. Tsongkhapa sets out three levels of dependence: on causes and conditions, on parts, and, the subtlest, on designation.

Designation is naming and singling out, on a basis. Nāgārjuna joins the two words in one verse. What arises in dependence is emptiness. It is a designation in dependence. And that is the middle way.

So depending and being designated are one fact, seen from two sides.

One word needs care. Arising does not mean production. Conditions meet, and this appears. Nothing is born from its own side.

And one small example shows the shape of it. Is long long in itself? No. It is long only beside short, and short only beside long. They make each other. Neither was there first.

So here is where we are. The apple is not found behind its appearings. The mountain is not found apart from what meets it. And the apple flies, the mountain stops the climber, and the toss is caught.

How?

The quick answer is, it has no essence, yet it still works. I will come back to why that answer is a trap. First, what dependent arising actually says. That is Part 2.
