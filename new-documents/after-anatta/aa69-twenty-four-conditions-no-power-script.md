# Dependent Arising Section 7 Part 3: Twenty-Four Conditions, No Power

The Theravāda counted twenty-four ways one thing conditions another. And it defined a condition as what assists, not what produces.

I am John Tan, known as Thusness. These are my own notes, from my own practice and contemplation.

I say often that conditions condition, and that no causal power is ever found in them. A reader who knows the Theravāda will ask: is that not just the Abhidhamma?

The Theravāda has a whole book on conditions, the Paṭṭhāna, the Great Treatise. It is the longest work in its canon. In Section 5, I mentioned it only in passing. This last part meets it.

Buddhaghosa defines a condition in one line. A condition has the characteristic of assisting. It helps a state to arise, or to stay. Not produces. Not creates. Assists.

He also says that no single cause gives a fruit. Only many causes give many fruits.

That is close to what I mean. Things function. A match lights, bread rises, karma ripens. What I never find is a power sitting in the cause and handed across to the effect. Buddhaghosa's word already leaves that power out.

The Paṭṭhāna names twenty-four. I will take four, because I have been using them without their names.

Mutuality. Things that support each other at once, like the three sticks of a tripod. The four great elements stand like this. None comes first. None produces another. These are the two sheaves of reeds from Section 5: each stands because the other does, and there is no first.

Decisive support. Strong support from far off: faith, virtue, a teacher, food, the weather, a place to stay. Practice works through these. No one is in charge of them. We arrange them, and something grows.

Kamma from another time. An action conditions its fruit later. And the commentary is plain that nothing travels across. Its images are an echo, a lamp's light, a seal, and a reflection. This is what I call neither carried, nor cut.

Absence. A mind moment that has ceased helps the next one, by giving opportunity. Nothing passes. The moment before is simply among the conditions of this one.

So the reeds, the arranged conditions, the uncarried karma and the moment before are all already here, in the Theravāda's own map.

The Theravāda keeps one thing I do not keep: the pieces.

Its commentaries say each dhamma bears its own nature. Hardness is what is hard. Contact is what touches. Apart from its own nature, there is no separate thing called a dhamma. The dhammas are also borne by conditions, so this own nature is conditioned, softer than in the schools Nāgārjuna argued with. But the pieces remain the real units, and the conditions relate them.

That is where I part. The Abhidhamma saw through the self, the person and the chariot. It kept the dharmas as the real pieces. Nāgārjuna finds the dharmas empty too. Feeling has no nature of feeling from its own side.

So I would say it the other way round. It is not real pieces, related by conditions. Conditions are the appearing. Hardness, moisture, warmth and motion appear together, and we cut that one appearing into qualities, for our own purposes. The cuts are useful. They are not the floor.

None of this throws the map away. I keep the Abhidhamma as a conventional map, for meditation, for ethics, and for understanding rebirth. It is a very good one. The twenty-four conditions are a precise way of seeing how a life is held together, with no one inside holding it.

What I add is only this. The map's pieces are designated too. Then assists is not one real thing helping another. It is how appearing goes together.

This is Section 7, Part 3 of the Dependent Arising series, and the close of the series.

Twenty-four conditions. No power. And nothing lost.
