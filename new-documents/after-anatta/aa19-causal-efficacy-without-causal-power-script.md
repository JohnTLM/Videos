# Causal Efficacy Without Causal Power

Strike a match. There is a flame. Leave the yeast out of the dough, and the loaf comes out flat.

This is After Anatta: notes by John Tan, known as Thusness.

Nothing seems more certain than that one thing brings about another. Rice grows from rice seed, never from barley. Medicine heals. Actions bear fruit.

But a quieter habit comes with this certainty. We picture the match as holding a power to light. We picture the seed as holding the sprout inside it, and the angry word as holding the anger it causes. The cause becomes a little thing with a force in it, and the effect becomes a second thing that receives what was handed over.

That picture is the same habit that builds a self. A self is the doer behind the doing. A causal power is the doer behind the causing. When anatta is seen, the doer behind seeing falls away. When this insight is seen, the doer falls away from everything: the seed, the match, the action, the moment.

So this is my view, in one line. Causes work. A power inside them that makes the effect is never found. Both stand.

Let me take the two halves in turn.

First, causes work. Nothing in this stops you lighting the stove. Barley seeds grow barley, and fire burns. The order of the world is untouched. What goes is only one picture: a finished thing being pushed across an edge by another finished thing.

Second, no power is found. Take a key. It opens this lock and no other. Put it in the wrong lock, and it opens nothing. So where is its power to open? Not in the key alone. It shows itself only with its partner. A power that shows itself only with its partner is not an inner property.

Or take a seed. To say a seed grows because it has the power to grow is to say it grows because it grows. The word "power" adds a name, not an explanation.

It helps to see that the word "cause" is used in three ways. The first is a producer: something existing by itself that makes an effect outside itself. That is the one Nāgārjuna refuses. The second is a condition that lines up: factors that let a result appear when they meet. That one stands. The third is a name: "cause" is what we call the condition that attention picks out when it follows a process. Keeping the third in view stops the second from sliding back into the first.

Now turn the usual worry round. People fear that if things are empty, nothing could work. It is the other way. Suppose this moment existed by itself, complete from its own side. Could it change? It could not, because to change is to become other, and a nature of its own does not become other. Could it combine with anything? No, because it depends on nothing. Could it act? No, because to act is to make a difference to what is not itself. A world of things with their own nature would be fixed, like an anvil. Things work, and what works is found empty. And emptiness itself causes nothing. It is only what dependence comes to when it is examined.

Now the part I most want to make clear. Conditions are the appearing.

Even after the power is gone, the mind keeps one last layer. It pictures the conditions as little things lined up underneath, which come together and then give rise to the appearing. Spark, fuel and air first. The flame afterwards, built out of them.

But look at the flame. It is not assembled from spark, fuel and air the way a wall is built from bricks. What is there is the burning, vivid and whole. Spark, fuel and air are that same appearing, divided up.

Who divides it? A way of looking that wants to explain something, or get something done. Think of a loaf of bread. A cook says it rose because of the yeast, the warmth and the time. A chemist names the sugars, the enzymes and the gas. A physicist names molecules, energy and pressure. A baker in a cold kitchen names the draught by the window. Each picks out different conditions, and each works. None of them is the list of parts the loaf was truly made from, waiting to be read off.

The parts appear as parts only in relation to a framework that breaks appearances into parts, for explaining and for getting things done. And that framework works within a set of agreements, inside a setting. In a kitchen, leave out the yeast and the loaf is flat, every time. Take the cook's account to the scale of atoms, and it explains nothing. Take Newton to the speed of light, and it fails. So the reliability is enough to stake a life on. But it belongs to the framework working in its setting, not to parts that exist from their own side.

Then see what this does to causation. If the conditions are the appearing, cut up for a purpose, then "the conditions produce the appearing" turns out to be the appearing, divided, said to produce itself. The picture of production has nothing left to stand on. And nothing is lost. The cook still bakes. The engineer still repairs the engine.

Two wrong ways to hear this. One: so the conditions are only concepts, and nothing really happens. No. The framework works. That is the whole point. Two: so the appearing is the true whole underneath, and the parts are illusions laid on it. No. The whole and the parts are named together, and neither is found standing by itself.

Is this orthodox? Let me check it against Nāgārjuna and his readers.

The Middle Way opens with four corners. Not from itself, not from another, not from both, and not without a cause: no thing anywhere is ever found to have arisen. I read this as Candrakīrti does: all four answers are refused, and no fifth is put in their place. Nāgārjuna also refuses a producing activity inside the cause. That is exactly what I mean by causal power. And he says conditions are called conditions because something arises in dependence on them. A seed in a dry jar is just a seed.

In his twentieth chapter, Nāgārjuna looks at the handover. If the cause gives its power to the effect and then ceases, the cause would have two selves. If it ceases without giving, the effect has no cause. And if effect and cause were one, producer and produced would be the same. If they were separate, a cause would be no different from a non-cause. So nothing passes from cause to effect.

One caution here. When I say cause and effect arise together, I mean they are named together, as long and short are. I do not mean they happen at the same moment. In the world's terms, the seed comes before the sprout. Nāgārjuna refuses a cause and effect that sit side by side, and so do I.

Candrakīrti refuses production from another even in the world's own terms. Someone who plants a seed says, "I planted this tree." They hold no theory of two separate things. What the world uses is mere arising, not production from its own side.

Tsongkhapa adds a careful point. In his school, a functioning thing is defined as that which is able to perform a function. So he keeps a capacity, in the world's terms: the seed is able to give a sprout. What he refuses is a capacity that exists from its own side. Some of my older lines leaned towards saying there is no capacity at all, only patterns. I would now put it this way. No power is found from its own side. The capacity, named in dependence, works. The key opens this lock.

And this is not the philosopher Hume, who dropped the power but kept the events, each standing alone, linked only by habit. For me there were never two separate things that needed a link.

Now the hardest part: karma across time. An action ceases. How does its fruit come years later?

Tsongkhapa answers that the action's having-ceased is itself a functioning thing. And its imprints are said to rest on the mere I: the I that is only named on the body and mind, not found among them, yet able to act and to meet the result.

I keep the mere I as the person the world speaks of. "I did it, and I will meet the result." That is a sound account of responsibility, ethics and karma. What I refuse is the mere I as a bridge: the thing that must be there for the imprints to rest on, so that an action can reach its fruit.

First, a bridge does not explain. It only passes the problem on. "Neither carried nor cut" is close to Tsongkhapa's own refusal of the storehouse consciousness. Once we start adding a layer to explain karma, rebirth or continuity, there is no end to it. Ask of any carrier: does it last, or does it change moment by moment? If it lasts, it cannot take in changing imprints. If it changes, each of its own moments has to get to the next, and that needs another carrier. And so on.

Tsongkhapa's having-ceased is momentary too, a continuum that runs until the fruit. If each of its moments follows the next with no further link, then the action's own sequence needs no link either. Nothing has been added.

Where does the need for a bridge come from? From the habit of the inherent framework: moments taken as things standing on their own, with a gap between them. Experiential reality is never like that. "Neither carried nor cut" offers no other way across. It denies both: that the act travels to the fruit, and that the act ends in nothing before the fruit comes. It puts nothing in their place. That the fruit follows the act was never a riddle. Rice still comes from rice seed. Only the demand for a bridge has to go.

Dōgen says it from the other side. Firewood burns to ash, and ash does not turn back into firewood. Yet do not think of firewood as before and ash as after. We do not say winter becomes spring. Winter is wholly winter, spring wholly spring, and no winter-stuff crosses over. Firewood stays in its own place, fully firewood; ash in its own place, fully ash. Before and after are cut off, but this is not the cut of annihilation: nothing that existed was destroyed into nothing. And the order holds: firewood burns to ash, never the other way round. Nothing travels, so nothing is carried. Nothing falls into nothing, so nothing is cut. And the fruit still follows the act.

There is a second reason. Tsongkhapa himself rejects a mind that knows itself, even in the world's terms. It was posited to explain memory, and he says it is a theory built to fill a gap, not something the world knows. When the mere I is asked to be the bridge for karma, it does the same job. It fills the gap. The world does say "I did it", so the person stays. Only the bridge goes. And the having-ceased of an action, as a functioning thing, is even more clearly a theory. No one in the world knows of it.

So I keep the phrase "neither carried nor cut". Nothing is carried across, and nothing is cut off. It is not meant as a better theory. It is meant to make you face something.

It leaves the mind uneasy. Something seems to be missing between the act and its fruit. Do not answer that feeling. Look at it, again and again, until you see what it is. The gap is felt only because the habit of seeing things as existing in themselves needs something to be there. Analysis shows, every time, that no bridge can be found. Moment-to-moment experience never meets one. And still the feeling returns. That shows how deep the habit of confused seeing runs, though no power to blind is ever found.

And when the dismantling has nowhere left to go, no bridge, and no break either, then with nothing left to link, nothing is missing.

That is where you can glimpse what Dzogchen calls being beyond cause and effect. I do not read it as a place where causes stop working. I read it as the end of the demand for a producer, and for a final why. The display is complete and present as it is, owing nothing to anything behind it. Nothing higher, nothing lower.

Here I part from some readings, and I want to say so plainly. Many Dzogchen texts describe spontaneous presence as the presence of the ground, not made by causes and conditions. I read spontaneous presence as dependent arising itself, met without the habit of fixing. Not uncaused, and not a source behind things. That is my own synthesis.

It is closest to Mipham, who holds that appearance and emptiness are one, and who uses analysis to bring certainty. But on bridges, Mipham and I part. He keeps a mind that knows itself, in the world's terms. I am stricter. Even so, his self-knowing is not a second act turned back on the first. It is the clarity of knowing, not lit by anything else. And that is very near what I mean when I say clarity is never missing.

So how is this seen through? Not by a better theory of causes. By looking for the power and not finding it, and then seeing that nothing was lost.

Strike a match and look for the moment the flame passes from not being to being. At no moment is a finished flame handed across.

Or take angry words. "Your words made me angry." The anger arose with the words, the hearing, an old hurt and a tired evening. No power to anger sits inside the words. Yet the words can be apologised for. This is where the insight matters most. Suffering is kept going by conditions that can change, not by a power that must be fought.

When this is seen, the search for a power, a carrier and a ground stops. The last why stops applying, though "why did the loaf fail?" still has its answer. Conditions matter more, not less. And appearing is met as it is: vivid, working, complete, and not found standing by itself. Mind and matter lose their weight, and become light, magical and plastic.

Why does this problem never come up in seeing? In the seen, just the seen, the question of how the act reaches its fruit does not arise. Not because experience answers it. The question needs a split that is not there.

It needs two things standing on their own, a before and an after, with a gap between them. In seeing, neither is found. Each instant is complete. The seeing is wholly seeing, the sound wholly sound.

It needs someone outside, looking. To see a gap between two moments, there must be a place outside both. That place is the observer. When anatta is seen, there is no seer behind the seeing, and in the same move no causer behind the causing.

And it needs time as something on its own. Past and future are never met as places. Remembering is this appearing, now, as its conditions come together.

So the gap is never found in looking. It comes in when thought lines the instants up and asks how one gets to the next. Without that framework, it is one complete stroke. This is not a oneness: seeing is seeing, hearing is hearing, with no pure consciousness behind them. And it is not a state to keep up. Maintenance is the split. Clarity is never absent; it is only not abstracted out.

Try it with the gap. Recall something you did years ago whose result you live with now. Notice the feeling of a missing gap between then and now. Look at that feeling. Is the gap found anywhere? Stay with it until the asking loosens. The act still bore its fruit.

And one more. Try it. Turn on a tap. Watch the water come. Look for the moment the tap makes the water flow. Then look for the flowing apart from the tap, the pipe, the pressure and the turn of your hand. Neither is found.

And the water still flows.
