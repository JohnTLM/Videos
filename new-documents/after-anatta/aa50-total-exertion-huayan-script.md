# Dependent Arising Section 2 Part 10: Total Exertion: Huayan

Worlds in a grain of dust. A rafter, and the building it makes. A net of jewels, each shining in all the others. Beautiful images. But what do they mean, and what must they not mean?

I am John Tan, known as Thusness. These are my own notes, from my own practice and contemplation.

In Parts 6 to 9, I tried to say how what appears can be complete, and yet wholly dependent. Through Dōgen's four images, each event spends itself completely.

Total exertion is Dōgen's phrase. But the taste is not his alone. Its great classical home is the Huayan school of Chinese Buddhism, named after the Flower Ornament sūtra. Dōgen's Zen grew up in a Chinese Buddhism that Huayan had helped to shape. And the family resemblance is plain.

For me, Huayan matters a great deal. Its images say, more vividly than any other tradition I know, what the world is like when nothing stands on its own, and yet everything works together. I want to give them their full weight here. And I also want to say clearly where I keep a guard. Because the same images that open the taste can, if heard carelessly, put a new floor under it.

First, worlds in a grain of dust.

The Flower Ornament is vast. A scripture of light, and countless worlds. Its signature image is this. In a single grain of dust appear buddha fields, as many as the grains of dust in all the worlds. And none of them crowds out another. The small does not shrink to make room. The large does not squeeze in. They do not obstruct each other.

Surely, the habit says, this is fantasy. Or at best, poetic exaggeration. A grain of dust is a grain of dust. It has its own size and its own edges. And worlds are much bigger than it.

Or look at what the image is pointing to. A grain of dust has no size or edge of its own, as Part 2 found with the atom. It is what it is only with the light that shows it, the eye that sees it, the floor it rests on, the wind that moved it there, the scale at which it is picked out. Nothing about it stands apart from those.

So it does not exclude anything. A thing with a solid core of its own would push others away to make room for itself. A thing with none pushes nothing away.

That is the point. And it is not about cramming worlds into a small space. Reading it as worlds packed into a tiny location would bring back the empty box of space that Part 3 took apart. It is about non obstruction. What lacks its own core does not block.

Second, the rafter.

The great systematiser of Huayan, Fazang, put this into a famous dialogue, about a building and its rafter. I give it in my own words.

What is the building? The rafter is the building.

How can one rafter be the whole building? Because without that rafter, the building is not complete. It is a broken building, not this building. When the rafter is there, the building is there. So the rafter makes the whole building.

But surely the tiles, the walls and the beams make the building too? Yes. And each of them makes the whole building in the same way.

And here is the turn. A rafter on its own, lying in a yard, is not a rafter at all. It is a piece of wood. It is a rafter only in the building, with the tiles, the walls and the beams.

Look at what this says. The rafter is not a small part of a big building. It is fully exerted as this building. And the building is nothing apart from the rafter, the tiles, the walls and the beams. Neither the part nor the whole stands first. That is total exertion said in Chinese, centuries before Dōgen.

Here I should say where I part from Fazang, fairly. His argument really does say the rafter is the building. And he builds on mutual identity, against a background of the one mind. Nāgārjuna would not say is. Whatever arises in dependence on something is neither that thing, nor different from it. So I read the rafter as fully exerted as this building. Neither the same as it, nor apart from it. That is my reading of Fazang, not a summary of him.

And it is also the boat. The rower is a rower only in the rowing. The rafter is a rafter only in the building.

Third, Indra's net.

The best known Huayan image is Indra's net. Over the palace of the god Indra hangs a net, stretching in every direction. At every knot is a jewel. And every jewel reflects every other jewel. And each reflection reflects all the others again, without end. No jewel is the central one. No jewel is the original.

This is the taste of seamlessness without fusion. Each jewel stays itself, clear and distinct. Nothing melts into one. And yet nothing is sealed off. Everything shines in everything else. The invisible walls are gone, and the jewels are still jewels.

Huayan says it in a short formula. One is all, and all is one. And its teachers set this out as four ways of seeing, which Chengguan later arranged as the four realms.

First, things. The everyday world of distinct things. The cup, the tree, the bell.

Second, principle. Their emptiness. The absence of a self standing nature.

Third, principle and things without obstruction. Form is emptiness, emptiness is form. Emptiness is not somewhere else. It is how the cup is.

Fourth, things and things without obstruction. Each thing and every other thing, meeting without obstruction, each including all.

The fourth is the one closest to total exertion. It is not a teaching about emptiness behind things. It is about things themselves. The cup and the hand and the water, meeting completely. None of them blocking the others. None of them standing alone.

What total exertion is, for me.

The taste came after anatta, in ordinary moments, with no special state and no vision. Walking. Hearing traffic in the distance. Watching leaves move in the wind. Washing a cup. The washing was no longer someone handling an object. There was only the washing.

In my book I put it this way. The whole universe appears to be completely occupied with this one event.

I keep the word appears in that sentence on purpose. That is how it is tasted. The whole of what is happening is this washing, with nothing left over, standing outside it. It is not a statement that a cosmic substance is packed inside the cup. That is the difference between a taste and a theory. And it is where I am most careful.

For me, Huayan gives the taste its full colour. Dōgen gives it its precision, in a single act. This stroke of the oar. This firewood. Nāgārjuna gives it its grammar. Designated together, neither found on its own side. I find I need all three.

Where I keep a guard.

Huayan is sometimes read in a way I do not follow. And I want to say where, fairly.

Not a claim about space. The worlds in a dust grain are not worlds packed into a tiny place, in a shared container. It is about non obstruction, not about size.

Principle is not a ground. Huayan also speaks of everything arising from the one true nature, closely tied to buddha nature and the one mind. Some readers take this principle as a positive, all embracing source, from which things come. A cosmic mind behind the display. A Middle Way reading takes principle as emptiness. And emptiness is not a source, or a ground, of anything. Huayan can be read either way. I take the second.

The net is not found either. Each jewel depends on the others. But the relations are not found from their own side, any more than the jewels are. Take the net as the reality, with things as its knots, and a ground has come back, as a web.

One is all is not one thing. It does not mean everything is one substance. Or that a single cosmic Whole is present in each moment, as its true reality. That is holism again. Each jewel stays a jewel. Each rafter stays a rafter. Intimate without merging. Distinct without walls.

With those guards in place, I find Huayan's images among the most beautiful ever made, for what is actually here.

So, which holds?

By experience. When I wash a cup, is the washing a small part of a larger world, going on around it? Or is the washing, while it happens, complete, with the water, the hand, the cup and the warmth all coming through as it? What is met is the second. And at no point is a cosmic substance met inside the cup, or a net met behind it.

By logic. A rafter that was a rafter on its own, apart from the building, would be a rafter lying in a yard. A piece of wood. A building that was a building apart from its rafters would need no rafters. Each is what it is only with the other. And a Whole behind all things would need its parts to be a whole. It is no better off.

By working. Nothing works the less for this. The building stands. The net holds. The cup gets washed. Non obstruction does not make the world thin. It is why things can meet at all. Things with solid cores would push each other away.

So separate things in a container, or one great Whole behind them, is not the truer view. Both are the default.

None of this makes my everyday words false. The rafter holds up the roof, and the cup is clean. What fails is only a rafter, a cup, or a cosmos, standing on its own.
