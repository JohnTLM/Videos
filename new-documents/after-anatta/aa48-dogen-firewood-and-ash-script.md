# Dependent Arising Section 2 Part 8: Dōgen: Firewood and Ash

Firewood burns, and there is ash. Does something pass from the wood into the ash? Or does nothing?

I am John Tan, known as Thusness. These are my own notes, from my own practice and contemplation.

In Part 7, the shore and the boat took away the seat outside the event, and the doer behind it. A third way something could stand outside the event, and complete it, is time. A past that leads up to it. A future it leads into. A line, on which it is one small point.

Dōgen's image for this is firewood and ash. Of his four images, it is the one I find closest to the heart of total exertion. Because it shows most plainly the point Part 6 began with. Ash is just ash, completely. And ash is the wood, the fire, the air and the time, appearing as ash.

First, firewood and ash.

In the Genjōkōan, right after the boat and the shore, comes firewood. Firewood becomes ash, and ash cannot turn back into firewood.

But, Dōgen says, do not think of ash as after, and firewood as before. Firewood stays in its own dharma position, as firewood. It has a before and an after. Yet the before and after are cut off. Ash stays in its own position, as ash.

In the same way, life does not become death. So we speak of no arising, and no ceasing. Life is a position of its own time. And death is a position of its own time. Like winter and spring. We do not say that winter becomes spring, or that spring becomes summer.

How is this usually read? Each moment is complete in itself. And practice is not a means to a later awakening. Practice and awakening are one, each complete in its own moment.

Here is how I see it.

The habit runs a line through time. Firewood is the cause. Ash is the effect. And something travels along the line, from one to the other.

Anatta loosens the line. There is no one standing on it, measuring the moments and stitching them together.

But here is what I most want to say. Ash is just ash, completely. It is not half firewood. And it is not waiting to become something else. In the ash, just the ash.

And at the same time, ash is the wood, the fire, the air and the time, appearing as ash. Both hold, in the same moment. Ash is complete. And ash is dependently arisen. Wood is wood, and ash is ash. And that is exactly how dependent arising is met.

Nāgārjuna says this precisely. Whatever arises in dependence on something is neither that thing, nor different from it. And so it is neither cut off, nor permanent.

Dōgen's two phrases fit Nāgārjuna's two denials. Cut off from before and after. Nothing permanent travels from the wood into the ash. With a before and an after. The ash really does come from the wood. It is not cut off.

And firewood and ash are not labels on one continuous stuff that runs through both. If they were, that stuff would carry over. And the dharma positions would be only names on its surface. Nor are the positions little moments with natures of their own. Part 2 found no such moment. They are designated, not found. There is no real timeline underneath, on which they sit.

That is the taste of this image. Nothing carried over. Each moment is fresh, wholly itself. And light, because nothing has to be held from one moment into the next.

And where I part. Some readers stop at, each moment complete. And then the moments become islands. I keep the other half in the same breath. Complete, as its dependence.

Second, being time.

In another essay, Uji, Dōgen starts from a common phrase. For the time being. And he reads it as being time. Being is already time, and time is already being.

For the time being, standing on the highest peak. For the time being, walking the bottom of the ocean. The pine is time. The bamboo is time. Do not think, he says, that time only flies away. And he says, in a line many readers dwell on, that each moment is all being, the entire world.

He also speaks of time's passage. And passage, he says, is like spring. Spring has many features, and that is called passage. But there is nothing outside spring that passes through it. The passage of spring is just spring, passing.

How is this usually read? Time is not a container that things sit in. And things are not apart from their time. Each moment is complete, and contains all time. Some readers take that broadly. The whole universe, present in every moment.

Here is how I see it.

The habit makes time a river, or a road. Something already there, that things move along. Then time itself becomes the given. The floor under every event.

Dōgen takes that away. There is no time apart from the pine, the bamboo, the standing on the peak. Nāgārjuna examined time the same way, and did not find it. Time is not found apart from things. And since things are not found standing on their own, how could time be?

The passage of spring is the line I find most exact. Nothing outside spring passes through it. There is no carrier. No substance of time flowing underneath. No thread, on which the days are strung. Spring passes, as spring. That is the dharma position, seen from the inside. Passage, without anything carried, and without a line.

And where I part. I read each moment is all being as total exertion. Nothing outside the moment is needed to complete it. Nothing stands beyond it, as a larger time in which it sits. I do not read it as a cosmos packed inside the moment, or all of time stored in each instant. The moment lacks nothing because nothing outside it is needed. Not because everything is stored in it.

Third, birth and death.

In a short essay, Shōji, Birth and Death, Dōgen says: just understand that birth and death is itself nirvana. Then there is nothing to escape as birth and death. And nothing to seek as nirvana.

It is a mistake, he says, to think that birth turns into death. Birth is a position of its own time, with its before and after. When birth comes, there is just birth. When death comes, there is just death. Do not cling to them. And do not push them away. This birth and death, he says, is the life of the Buddha.

How is this usually read? Freedom is not an escape from birth and death into some other realm. It is meeting each fully, as it comes. And the life of the Buddha is often heard as one great life, present in all lives.

Here is how I see it.

This is where firewood and ash stop being a teaching about wood. When life is present, it is completely alive. Not a prelude. Not a journey already heading for a destination called death. When death comes, it is its own position. Not life, turned into something else. Nothing migrates across a line from one to the other. Life is the total working of life. Death is the total working of death.

And because each is complete as its conditions, neither is sealed off. Life arises dependently, wholly. And so does death. There is nothing to escape, because there is no line to escape along. And no one standing on it.

And where I part. I do not take the life of the Buddha as one great life behind all lives, living through them. That would put a Whole behind birth and death. Birth is complete as birth. Death as death. And nothing stands behind them.

So, which holds?

By experience. Is ash met as half finished firewood? Or as a stage on a line? Is this moment met as a point on a road that runs off behind, and ahead? What is met is this, wholly itself, and fresh. The ash. The spring. This breath. And at no point is a thread of time met, running underneath.

By logic. If the ash were the same as the wood, nothing would have burned. If it were wholly other than the wood, it could appear without any fire. If something carried over from wood to ash, it would be permanent, and could never burn. If nothing came from the wood, the ash would be uncaused. Neither the same, nor different. Neither permanent, nor cut off.

By working. Nothing works the less for this. The fire still burns, and the ash still feeds the field. Spring still comes after winter. Memory, plans and promises work, each in its own moment.

So the view of a line of time, with something carried along it, is not the truer one. It is the default, carried by habit, and by the words becomes, and turns into.

None of this makes my everyday words false. The wood burns to ash. Winter gives way to spring. What fails is only a time, a wood, or a self, standing on its own, and travelling across.
