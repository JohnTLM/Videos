# Two Logics

Here is a koan. This moment ceases as it arises. Does it arise, or does it cease?

Read it slowly. Before any answer comes, the question has already handed us an "it". A moment, standing there, that must do one thing or the other.

This is the first step of The Logic, from After Anatta: notes by John Tan, known as Thusness. The step names two ways of reasoning, and reads the koan through both. Everything that follows uses them, because the difference between them is the difference his own practice turned on.

Listen to a bell. The answer comes before any thought: the sound is fading. Something is there, and something is happening to it.

That answer follows a pattern. Call it the logic of substance. It starts with things, and then asks what happens to them. The thing comes first. Its arising and its ending are events that befall it. Read this way, the koan starts with "this moment" as a thing. And it demands an answer, because a thing that is there has to be doing one or the other.

The logic of dependency starts one step earlier. Before asking what happens to a thing, it asks how anything is picked out as a thing at all. And it finds that a thing is only ever picked out in dependence. On its parts. On its causes. On what it is set against. And on the name under which it is known. No thing stands there first, waiting to be named.

Read this way, the koan asks something else. How is "this moment" picked out? Only by its arising and its ceasing. And each of those only against the other. Moment, arising and ceasing are designated together.

The two logics begin in different places. So the same words, change, cause, identity, the path, lead them to different worlds.

Seen with the logic of substance, a moment is there, small and real, sitting in the present. Arising happens to it. Then ceasing happens to it. The question feels urgent, because the moment feels solid.

Seen with the logic of dependency, there is the sounding, vivid and exact, and no moment found standing inside it. "Arising" is said looking from before. "Ceasing" is said looking from after. In the author's words: the moment does not arise nor cease, but is merely designated as such upon conditions. The question has nothing to hold on to. And the sound is still heard.

It is like a staircase. Are the stairs up, or down? In themselves, neither. They are "up" for someone climbing, and "down" for someone coming down. The moment is the same. It is called arising or ceasing depending on what is picked out, and from which side. That does not make it nothing, or only in the mind. Stairs are really climbed, and really descended.

Ask people whether moments are things, and most will say no. But watch what we do, not what we say. Every sentence about change holds a subject still, so that the verb can happen to it. The moment passes. The flame flickers. I have changed. The koan is built the same way. Its grammar sets up the "it" before we have looked.

So the logic of substance is not mainly a belief. It is a habit. It switches on when we explain, when we name, when we feel threatened, when we lose. Agreeing with the logic of dependency in theory changes very little. Seeing through the habit, where it actually works, changes a great deal.

The logic of dependency comes down to one simple test. Look for the thing apart from what it depends on.

Try it on the koan. Listen to a sound until it ends. It is already passing as it sounds. Look for the moment apart from its arising and its ceasing, and none is found. A whirlpool is the same. It is not found apart from the water passing through. Yet leaves thrown into it still spin.

Both halves matter. Nothing is found when the moment is looked for on its own. That is emptiness. And the moment still works. The bell is heard, and we can say when it was struck and when the ringing stopped. That is conventional truth. The logic of dependency keeps both. Dependence and unfindability are one fact, seen from two sides.

Three misreadings keep the koan's "it", and move it somewhere else.

It is not "nothing exists". The sound is still heard. What is denied is a thing existing from its own side. Not the sounding, and not the burning.

It is not a better substance. It is tempting to swap things for something subtler: a process, a flow, a timeless Now, or a pure awareness in which moments come and go. Each of these becomes a new thing for everything else to happen to. The test applies to them too.

And it is not beyond logic. It relies on ordinary reasoning throughout. Its force comes from holding the logic of substance to its own rules.

The author reads his own seven phases of insight through the two logics. In the early phases, presence was vivid and real: I AM, then I AM everything, then nothingness, then a clear mirror reflecting all things. Each was a real insight. But each was held through the logic of substance. Presence was something on its own side. A Witness behind experience. A Source. A Mirror, untouched.

In 1997 the logic of dependency was turned on the subject. Looked for apart from what appears, the mirror was not found. In seeing, only the seen. Thinking, and no thinker. This is anatta.

Then the same test had to be turned on everything that appears. The scenery, the sound, the moment itself. This is the emptiness of phenomena. And it did not follow from anatta by itself. Anatta removes the one who watches the moment. It does not, by itself, remove the moment as a thing that arises or ceases. That is why the koan belongs here.

And in the last phase there is nothing left to turn the logic on. Awareness was never a witness standing apart. In his words: "Anatta is a seal, not a stage." Presence was vivid from the start. What changed was the logic through which it was read.

How fast are you going? Sitting still, it seems: zero. But you are still only against the room. With the earth's turning you are moving at over a thousand kilometres an hour, and around the sun at about a hundred and seven thousand. Which is your real speed? The logic of substance wants one, sitting in you. The logic of dependency sees that speed is only picked out against something: the room, the ground, the sun. Yet it is not arbitrary. A car doing a hundred and twenty still gets a fine.

The koan is only the sharpest case. Say "I have changed", and look for the one who changed, apart from the changing. Close your hand, and look for the fist apart from the closing. None is found. Yet a promise is still yours to keep, and a fist can still knock on a door.

So, for a moment, wherever you are. Say the koan once. Notice the "it" the question hands you. Then look for that moment, apart from its arising and its ceasing, in the sound or sight in front of you.

Do not try to reach an answer. Notice that the looking finds more vividness, more detail, more change. And no thing standing behind it.
