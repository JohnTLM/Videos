# Designated, Not Found

Here is the koan. This moment ceases as it arises. Does it arise, or does it cease?

The question hands us an "it". This step goes looking for that "it", the way an old teacher once went looking for a chariot.

This is the third step of The Logic, from After Anatta: notes by John Tan, known as Thusness. Step one gave the test: look for the thing apart from what it depends on. Step two said the test removes own-nature, a thing being there from its own side, and only that. Here the test runs on this moment. What is left is a name. Not an empty name, and not a sticker on something underneath. It is the way anything at all is there.

The monk Nagasena asks King Milinda how he came. By chariot, says the king. Then show me the chariot, says Nagasena. Is it the pole? The axle? The wheels? All of them together? Something apart from them? Each time, the king says no.

Later, Chandrakirti made the search complete. He checks seven places. The chariot is not the parts, for there are many parts and one chariot. It is not other than them, for no chariot is found apart from them. It does not own them. It is not in them, and they are not in it. It is not their collection, for a heap of parts on the lawn is not a chariot. And it is not their shape, for the shape is itself named. Found in none of them, yet named in dependence on its parts, it still carries passengers.

Now search the same places for this moment. Listen to whatever sound is here. Where is its moment?

Is it the arising? Then it would be all coming and no going. Is it the ceasing? Then something passes that never came to be. Both together? Arising and ceasing are two, and the moment is one. Other than them? Take them away, and no moment is left. Their owner? This is the koan's "it", standing beside its arising and ceasing as a subject stands beside its verb. That is grammar, not something found. Their container? This is the timeless Now in which things come and go. Looked for apart from what comes and goes, it is not there.

In Nagarjuna's words: coming into being and passing away are not found without a thing. And a thing is not found without coming into being and passing away.

With the logic of substance, the sound is a slice of time, and arising and ceasing happen to it. With the logic of dependency, the same sound is just as vivid, with nothing behind the sounding. "Ceases as it arises" is then a plain description, with no "it" in the middle.

Here is where most readers go wrong. They hear "the chariot is not found", and take it to mean there is no chariot. But the king came by chariot. The search removes nothing the road relies on. It removes only the idea that the chariot is there from its own side, waiting to be found.

So what is the chariot? It is designated in dependence on its parts. "Designated" means picked out, taken as this rather than that. "In dependence" means on a basis: these wheels, this axle, set up so that someone can ride. The name, the parts and the riding come together. The moment is the same: named, counted and timed. A race is won by a hundredth of a second.

Nagarjuna puts the whole Middle Way in one verse. Whatever arises in dependence, that is emptiness. That is designation in dependence. That is the middle way. Not found, and still counted, is one fact, seen from two sides. The moment is named arising and ceasing, upon conditions, and that is all it ever needed to be.

Now a second mistake. When the mind hears "designation", it pictures a sticker: "sugar", pressed onto a jar that was there before it. That picture keeps an object finished before anyone takes it as anything. But look at a cup. It does not first appear as a patch of colour, later labelled "cup". It appears as a cup, already something to lift. Peel the name off, and nothing bare is found underneath. Tsongkhapa goes further: even the basis is named. The wheels are named in dependence on rim, spokes and hub, all the way down. We never see ourselves naming, so it is felt as the world itself. In the koan, that is the "it".

The opposite mistake: if things are only named, can I call a shadow a bridge and walk across it? No. A name holds or fails as it is used. Tsongkhapa gives three checks. It is known to ordinary seeing. A second look does not overturn it. And searching for how things ultimately are does not overturn it. In dim light, you see a snake in the corner. The lamp comes on: a coiled rope. The snake fails the second check. A rope there from its own side fails the third. The rope as named, in dependence on its strands, passes all three. A name is valid not because it mirrors a thing behind it, but because it works.

The author first met the chariot as a clever argument about a cart. By then the self had already gone missing when he looked for it. The chariot gave him a way to understand the world as he had already tasted it. The taste was free of substance. The view was not. He still pictured an ear here, a bell there, and something crossing between them. In the author's words: "Walking is the chariot. Hearing is the chariot." Remove the axle, and there is not a lesser chariot. There is no chariot. Bell, striker, air, ear and sound are named together. After this, in his words, "intimacy no longer meant closeness. It meant there was never any separation to close."

The seven places work on anything. The self is not body and mind, nor other than them. Yet we keep promises and answer to our names. Pull a car apart in your mind: wheels on the lawn, engine in the garage. Put it back, bolt by bolt, and it is there again. Nothing called "car" was added on the last turn of the spanner. A seed is called a cause only of its sprout. Still, rice grows from rice seed, never from barley. Look for a friend apart from the face and the voice, and no one is found. Yet a promise to a friend is kept.

Try it. Listen to one sound. Is the moment its arising? Its ceasing? Both? Other than them? Their owner? Their container? Then notice the sound is still here, heard in full.

Or pick a chair near you. Go through the seven places, slowly. Then use it.
