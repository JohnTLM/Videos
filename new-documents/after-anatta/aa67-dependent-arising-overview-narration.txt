Seven sections. Twenty-nine parts. One thread. Nothing appears with a nature of its own. And what appears still works.

I am John Tan, known as Thusness. These are my own notes, from my own practice and contemplation.

If you think you understand dependent arising, you don't. I can agree that nothing has an essence of its own, say so fluently, and still hold the world exactly as before. Solid. Out there. Mine. Agreeing is not living in it.

So the series does not argue first. The early sections set two ways of seeing side by side. One takes things to stand on their own. The other looks at how they form. Then they ask which one holds. By experience, by logic, and by how things work. The later sections take up the logic, the tradition, Nah-gar-joo-nah, and the hardest questions.

This overview says what each section asks, and what runs through all of them.

First, the thread. It has two halves, and both matter.

Nothing appears with a nature of its own. Look for the apple apart from seeing, touching and tasting, and it is not found. Look for the sound apart from the bell, the air and the hearing. Look for the one who ages and dies. Each time, what is looked for is not there by itself.

And what appears still works. The apple is still bitten. The bell still rings. A car still knocks you down, and the grief of a family is not an illusion. Nothing is taken away. What goes is only the extra weight of taking things to stand on their own.

Drop the first half, and the world stays solid, out there, mine. Drop the second, and emptiness turns ghostly, a void behind things. The series keeps both, in every section.

Section 1 asks: is the world already there, finished, waiting to be seen? Or is it formed?

It starts with an apple. Nothing seems more plainly there. Then it looks at how the apple forms. Sight, sound, smell, taste, touch and mind, meeting, and named apple in the meeting.

Next, one bowl of liquid. A human sees water. A hungry ghost sees pus. A fish meets its home. A god meets nectar. No finished world waits underneath, seen differently.

Then two physicists, where they echo this, and where I part from them. And last, the conclusion. Without essence, nothing turns ghostly. It still rains. You still get sick.

Section 2 asks: given all this, what is it that appears?

A sound, a colour, a thought. Each is vivid, not found, and dependently arisen, all at once. Then the habit retreats and looks for a floor. The real instant. The smallest piece. Space itself. None is found. The solid, the dreamlike and the described are set side by side, and none of them is the floor. Not even the web of conditions.

Then clarity. Is there a knower behind it? No layer is found. Nothing stands between the hearing and the sound. The clarity is the sound itself.

From there, how what is seen can be complete, and yet wholly dependent. Three parts follow Doh-gen's images: the shore and the boat, firewood and ash, the fish and the bird. One part goes to Hwah-yen, with its net of jewels, and says where I keep a guard. And the last part shows where the big ideas come from. Privacy, objectivity, causal power, inside and outside. Each is a real feature of appearing, misread as a thing.

Section 3 asks: once neither mind nor matter is the floor, is either worth leaning towards?

Hear the rain and ask, what is it, really? The question looks innocent. It already carries a worldview. There has to be something underneath. Mind, or matter, or some third stuff.

Then the place this habit is felt most sharply: the Hard Problem. How does grey matter give rise to the felt redness of red? Seen through emptiness, the question is not answered. What it stands on is not found.

And last, the living fact. Divide mind from matter, and the gap cannot be bridged. Reach for a cup, and no gap is ever crossed.

Section 4 asks: how do we reason, once nothing is taken to have a nature of its own?

Watch a candle burn, and the answer comes before any thought. The flame is changing. Something is there, and something is happening to it. That answer is a whole way of reasoning. First a thing, then what happens to it.

The other way starts a step earlier. It asks how anything comes to be picked out as a thing at all.

Then the questions we ask about anything. When did it start? Where did it go? Is it the same one? Each asks where a thing is. When no thing is found, the questions lose their object. And the conventional is not merely conventional. It is where truth lives.

Last, continuity without a carrier. Not a passenger, but a melody you know by heart. The path becomes arranging conditions.

Section 5 asks: how does the tradition teach dependent arising, and who goes round the wheel?

Here I stay with the tradition. When this is, that is. The twelve links, forward and in reverse. The chain is reeds leaning on each other, not a line of dominoes.

Then, who ages and dies? Not a valid question, the Buddha says. Karma and rebirth are kept, with no one in them who goes round. That removes one objection to rebirth. It does not prove it.

Last, where the chain is cut. The pain still comes. What ends is the second arrow, what I add to it. And ignorance ends by seeing, not by stopping thought.

Section 6 asks: how does Nah-gar-joo-nah read the same teaching?

He bows to the Buddha for teaching dependent arising, without ceasing, without arising. How can arising be without arising? The answer is how he reads when this is, that is. A pattern, not a handover.

Then his chapter on the twelve links, the one chapter of his Middle Way with no refutation in it. Karma, rebirth and the path are kept. Only the owner is left out.

Last, how the chain ends. Ignorance is not a thing to destroy. It is a way of taking things: elaboration. And elaboration stops in emptiness.

Section 7 asks: what does this view do for others, and does it survive its strongest objections?

First, compassion. Emptiness does not cause it, and does not diminish it. As I see it, compassion has two sides. An open heart, which shows itself as the habit of standing apart loosens. And a learning, which grows as it is practised.

Then six objections, put as fairly as I can. Is this nihilism? Is it all mind? Is it only concepts, so anything goes? Is dissolving the Hard Problem just avoiding it? Is the physics stretched? Is emptiness, or the web, the new absolute?

How to read it. In order, if you can. Each section leans on the ones before. The conventional of Section 4 is used in Section 7. The seamless interaction of Section 3 comes back in compassion.

Or start where your question is. Each part can be read alone. If your question is mind and matter, start at Section 3. If it is karma and rebirth, start at Section 5.

And try the tests. Every part has small tests, to be tried in ordinary moments. They are gathered in one place, in the practice guide. Because agreeing is not living in it.

This is the overview of the Dependent Arising series.

Nothing appears with a nature of its own. And the bell still rings.
