# Dependent Arising Section 7 Part 2: Objections, Fairly Answered

If all this is empty, nothing arises and nothing ends. Nāgārjuna put the strongest objection to his own view in his own book. And then he answered it.

I am John Tan, known as Thusness. These are my own notes, from my own practice and contemplation.

Over six sections I have argued that nothing appears with a nature of its own. Not the apple. Not the moment. Not mind, not matter, not the links of the chain. A view that says this will meet objections, and it should.

Some come from philosophers. Some from scientists. Some from other Buddhists. And some from one's own mind, at three in the morning.

Nāgārjuna did not avoid them. He gives the opponent's case, in the opponent's words, for six verses, before he replies. That is the method I want to follow. State each objection at its strongest. Then answer it on its own terms. There are six.

The first. This is nihilism.

If nothing has a nature of its own, then nothing really exists. If nothing really exists, nothing really matters. Not suffering, not kindness, not the path. Emptiness, followed honestly, ends in a shrug.

Nāgārjuna's opponent says it exactly. If all this is empty, there is no arising and no passing away, and the four noble truths do not exist.

He turns it round, word for word. If all this were not empty, there would be no arising and no passing away. Then it is for you that the four noble truths would not exist.

A thing with its own nature could not arise, because it would already be what it is. It could not change. It could not end. Suffering with its own nature could never be relieved. A path with its own nature could never be walked. It is the world of own natures that is frozen, not the empty one.

And the shrug rests on what it claims to deny. It is all empty, so it doesn't matter, only works if mattering needs a solid core. That is the same assumption as the eternalist's, mourned instead of kept. Something can be cut off only if it was there from its own side first.

The person who is not found is the person who keeps the promise.

The second. Then it is all mind.

You have taken away the hard little things out there. What is left is appearing: sights, sounds, feelings, all vivid, all known. Surely, then, everything is mind.

This is the most natural next step, and I have watched it happen many times. The producer is gone. The hearer is gone. And mind is left standing as what everything is. That is the ground coming back, this time as mind.

But the ringing of a bell is there only with the bronze, the strike, the air and the ear. Being dependent, it is not mind from its own side, any more than it is bronze from its own side.

Clarity is never absent from what appears. But lifted out, and made what everything is made of, it is one more reification. When matter is seen through, the habit does not give up. It changes its stuff. Matter stuff traded for mind stuff is still stuff.

The third. Then it is only concepts, and anything goes.

If everything is named in dependence, then it is all labels. Labels are ours to choose. So any view is as good as any other.

Designation is not arbitrary, and it is not free. It works, and it can be wrong. A forged passport fails. A mistaken face in a crowd is corrected when it turns. A rope seen as a snake in dim light is put right by a lamp.

Tsongkhapa gives three tests. Is it known to ordinary cognition? Is it not overturned by further ordinary cognition? Is it not contradicted by analysis of the ultimate? The snake in the rope fails the second. The rope with its own nature fails the third. The rope, as named, passes all three.

Conventional truth, for me, is the only kind of validity there is, and it is fully valid. Merely conventional was never meant as merely made up.

The fourth. Dissolving the Hard Problem is not solving it.

Philosophers have asked for decades how physical processes give rise to felt experience. You say the question falls away. But the mystery is still there. Why does this brain activity go with this taste of coffee?

I want to be careful here, because the objection is partly right.

What falls away is one question: how does a stuff whose nature is to lack feeling produce a stuff whose nature is to feel? That question needs two banks and a bridge. And the banks were built by the framework that defined mind and matter as inherently different. A third substrate does not help. It only pushes the problem up a level, and calls it the ground.

What does not fall away is the ordinary, workable question. Which conditions go with which experiences, and how reliably. Which part of the brain, which chemical, which training, which injury. That work is real, and nothing here stands in its way.

I am not offering a theory of how matter makes mind. I am saying that the demand for one rested on a picture. Without the picture, what is left is not a mystery, but a field of study.

The fifth. The physics is being stretched.

Every few years someone finds the Buddha in quantum mechanics. Physicists object, rightly, that their theories are about measurements and mathematics, not about emptiness.

I agree with the caution. In Section 1, I set two physicists beside dependent arising as echoes, not proofs. Emptiness does not depend on physics, and physics does not prove emptiness.

I also said where I part from them. If relations become what is really real, or a field becomes the new substance, then physics has been used to rebuild exactly what dependent arising takes apart.

What I take from physics is modest. Even our best descriptions of matter do not show finished things, waiting to be measured. The view stands or falls on looking, not on any theory.

The sixth. Then emptiness, or the web, is the new absolute.

You deny every ground. But you keep saying emptiness. Isn't emptiness your ground? Or the web of relations, connecting everything to everything. Isn't that the real thing at last?

Nāgārjuna saw this coming. Emptiness was taught as the way out of all views. But those for whom emptiness becomes a view, he called incurable.

Emptiness is not a thing behind things. It is the absence of what was never there: a nature of their own. Taken as a void, a state to reach, or a view to defend, it has become the next object of the old habit.

The web is the same. Relations are named together with what they relate. A connection between two things that have no standing of their own is not a third thing with standing. The relating is as empty as what it relates. And it works exactly as well.

Most of these objections are not only out there, in books. They arise in my own mind. Then nothing matters. Then it is all mind. Then it is all just labels. Each comes with a small sense of relief, as if a floor had been found at last.

When one comes, I find it useful to ask what it is relieved to have found. Usually it is a ground. Something to stand on, or something to give up on. Neither is needed.

This is Section 7, Part 2 of the Dependent Arising series. Next, in Part 3, one more question: is all this not just the Abhidhamma?

The bell still rings when it is struck.
