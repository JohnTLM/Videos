# The Four Questions

Here is the koan once more. This moment ceases as it arises. Does it arise, or does it cease?

This is the eighth and last step of The Logic, from After Anatta: notes by John Tan, known as Thusness. Each step came back to the koan from one side. This one gathers Steps one to seven into four questions, and runs them on the koan, in order.

The four questions are the koan's method, made portable. The koan does not answer its question. It looks at what the question takes for granted, and the question falls away. The four do the same for any question: a self, a moment, a cause, a death, a world.

They are a test, not a conclusion. Step one found that the logic of substance is a habit, not a belief. So, like a pilot's checklist, the questions are done, not remembered. "Nothing has own-nature" can be agreed with in a second, and change nothing. The habit lives in the felt sense, before words, that this thing is simply there on its own. And it retreats rather than gives up, to a timeless Now, or to awareness. So the questions are made to be run again.

The four are these. What would a substance view say? What does dependency say instead? What is affirmed conventionally? What is never found?

The first question. What would a substance view say? State it fairly, the way the question looks when the habit is running. There is a moment, and arising and ceasing are what happen to it. So it must do one or the other. The point is to find what is being taken as standing on its own, as it actually works in your own mind. So ask: what here feels simply there, by itself? In the koan, it is the word "it".

And it has to be asked of the one asking. Moments come and go, says the habit, but something watches them. In the author's words: "In the years of I AM I asked this question of everything except the Witness."

The second question. What does dependency say instead? Look for the thing apart from what it depends on. Check three kinds of dependence. Causes: a sprout depends on a seed, water and warmth. Parts: a chariot depends on its wheels and axle. And designation: as long depends on short, and knower on known.

In the koan, a sound depends on its source, the air, the ear and attention. Most of all, the moment depends on designation. "This moment" is picked out together with its arising and its ceasing. None comes first. Look for the moment apart from them, and it is not found. Look for it as the two together, and there is no one thing for them to belong to. So the answer is not "it is all connected", which can become a new floor. It says how the thing is picked out. Then "ceases as it arises" is a plain description, not a paradox.

The third question. What is affirmed conventionally? This question protects the world. In the koan, everything is kept. We can say when the car came, and when it had gone. Clocks keep time. Actions bear fruit.

There is a caution here. Removing a bad argument against something is not a good argument for it. So what is affirmed comes from where conventions come from: ordinary knowing, the tradition, and evidence. That a clock keeps time is ordinary knowing. That actions bear fruit beyond this life is the tradition, and the author says so. He does not derive rebirth from emptiness.

The fourth question. What is never found? Name it plainly: a moment with a nature of its own, standing between its arising and its ceasing. Naming it keeps the result from blurring into "nothing happens". That is the nihilist trap. A sound is being heard right now.

Then check what has been put in its place. A timeless Now in which the moments pass. An awareness behind them that stays. A pure flow. Each keeps the "it", and moves it somewhere else. That is the eternalist trap, and it goes back to question one. Emptiness gets the same treatment. It is not a backdrop the moments flash against. Held as the final truth, the medicine would have become a new illness.

Ask what arises, and the koan is a paradox. Run the four questions, and no "it" is found, nothing is put in its place, and the sound is still heard.

Yet we usually go about it the wrong way. We keep trying to answer, under the influence of the very habit we want to undo. Does the self exist or not? What happens to me when I die? Each question already assumes a thing with its own nature. Any answer in the same frame swaps one thing for another: the self for a Witness, the Witness for pure Awareness, a thing for nothing. What feels like logic is a habit that has never been checked.

Nagarjuna's Middle Way gives an alternative. Not a better answer in the same frame, but a way to examine the frame itself. He follows each answer to the end, until it undoes itself. The question is not answered. It falls away. The view lets us question the question.

The same logic works beyond the moment. The self: "I am the one who decides." Look for a decider apart from the weighing and the wanting, and only the deciding is found. Yet the one who decided is responsible. A cup: look for it apart from clay, shape, maker and use, and it is not found. Yet it holds tea. A cause: "Your words made me angry." The anger arose with the words, the hearing, an old hurt and a tired evening. No power to anger sits inside the words. Yet the words can be apologised for. And a person: a family leaving the house of twenty years. "Home" is named on a house, the people in it, and the years of coming back. The grief stays. What loosens is the sense that something solid is being torn away.

So, try it. Tap a glass, and listen until the sound ends. Then ask the four questions, one line each. What does the substance view say about this sound? How is it picked out? What is still true of it? What was not found? Check the last line. Has a Now or an awareness slipped in?

Run them on your own weight. One: the weight is in me. Two: it is picked out with the earth's pull, the floor and the scale. Three: the number is true, and the knees still ache after the stairs. Four: never found is a heaviness sitting in the body on its own. And check that "the whole universe" has not slipped in as a new floor.

Run them slowly, in what is in front of you. Nothing needs to be held. What appears goes on appearing.

And the koan, one last time. This moment ceases as it arises. Does it arise, or does it cease? The question has nothing to hold on to. And the sound is no less clear.
