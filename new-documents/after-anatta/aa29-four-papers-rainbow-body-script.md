# Rainbow Body: Four Papers, Read with Emptiness

A body is left in a small room in eastern Tibet. Over a week it grows smaller. Rainbows are seen above the roof. When the door is opened, only hair and nails remain.

Four papers tell this story. They tell it well. They also tell it in a way that can quietly bring back the very thing the story is meant to free us from.

This is After Anatta: notes by John Tan, known as Thusness. In this video we read four outside papers on the rainbow body, light and the five elements. We keep what holds. And we look closely at three places where each of them slips.

The four papers come from different angles. One is an essay on light and mind in Bön and Dzogchen. Bön is the old religion of Tibet, with its own Dzogchen. One is a scholarly comparison of light across three systems: Kālacakra, Nyingma Dzogchen, and Bön. One is a report on Bön practices with the five elements. And one is a beginner's guide to the rainbow body.

First, what they report. And here they are mostly right.

In Dzogchen and in Bön, the rainbow body is said to come at death. Over several days the body shrinks. Sweet scents replace decay. Rainbows and beams of light appear. In the end only hair and nails are left. A rarer kind, the great transference, is said to need no death at all. The body itself becomes a body of light. The tradition names Padmasambhava and Vimalamitra.

The papers also give the path. In Dzogchen there are two practices. Cutting through means recognising the knowing that is empty and clear at once, and resting in it. Direct crossing works with the display itself. With certain postures and gazes, lights appear in open space. Small drops of light. Fine threads. They grow into patterns, and then into whole mandalas. The texts call these the four visions. The fourth is called the exhaustion of phenomena.

The texts also speak of lamps. One lamp is the eyes. Another is the open sky the lights appear in. Another is the circles of light themselves. And the last is the wisdom that knows them as its own display. The lights do not appear apart from seeing. They are seeing, recognised.

So far, good reporting. Now the three slips.

The first slip is the biggest. Light becomes a stuff.

The papers say that light is the building block of the cosmos. That matter is light condensed. That the solid world is a collapse of open space, cooling and hardening like ice. One of them says that awareness is the space in which the light plays.

It sounds deep. But follow it through. If light is what everything is made of, then light is one more ground. A finer substance, shining instead of solid, but a substance all the same. And if awareness is the space it plays in, then awareness is a container.

Dzogchen itself does not need this. Its ground has three aspects. Its essence is empty, pure from the beginning. Its nature is clear, spontaneously present. And its display never stops. Hold the first one in view, and the picture changes. The five lights are display with nothing behind them. They do not condense out of a shining stuff.

Then what do the texts mean when they say the lights harden into the elements? Read with care, it names how appearing is taken. When the display is not recognised, it is grasped as something other, standing over there. That grasping is what makes the elements seem solid. Nothing turned solid. Nothing was ever frozen. There was only a deep habit of seeing things as if they stood by themselves.

Try it now with something in your hand. An apple, a cup. There is hardness. There is coolness. There is colour. Each appears as its conditions meet. Then habit ties them into one solid thing, out there, as if they all belonged to a core underneath. That knot is the hardening. Untie it, and nothing is lost. The hardness, the red, the coolness are all still here. Only the extra solidity drops.

This is also how to read the fourth vision. The papers say that everything dissolves back into primordial emptiness. As if emptiness were a place things return to. But emptiness is not a place, and not a source. What is used up in the exhaustion of phenomena is the holding of appearances as things with a nature of their own. Appearing itself is not wiped out.

The same care is needed with light inside the body. One paper says the heart is a vessel that holds a radiant mandala, waiting to break out. The tradition does use that image: a youthful body in a vase. But it is a simile. Taken literally, it is a light stored inside a container. In John Tan's reading, clarity is never stored anywhere. It is the knowing clarity of appearing itself, and it is as empty as whatever appears.

So the first correction is simple. Light names how appearing shines when it is not grasped. It is not what the world is made of.

The second slip is quieter. Reports become facts.

The papers say the rainbow body is proven. That hundreds of witnesses verified it. That it is physical, empirical proof of liberation. They list cases with dates, some going back many centuries, some as recent as the last few years.

Here we have to be honest. These are reports. Some are moving. Some come from people who were there. But none has been checked by anyone independent. And when the lists are looked at closely, they do not all hold.

One famous master is listed as a rainbow body case, though his body was ordinary at death and his relics were kept. Another master's death is dated to one year in one source and seventeen years later in another. The sixteenth Karmapa is listed too. But what was reported after his death is tukdam, not a rainbow body. Tukdam is a state in which a practitioner is said to rest in meditation after death. The body stays fresh, and warm at the heart, for days. It does not shrink. It does not vanish. In 2021, a team from the University of Wisconsin measured thirteen such cases. They found no brain activity. But they could only begin recording a day or more after death, so the question stays open.

And the most often told modern case, Khenpo A Chö in 1998, rests on eyewitness accounts gathered afterwards, and on a local press report that the body shrank to the size of a bean. A priest and scholar, Francis Tiso, went to look into it. What he could gather was what people told him.

None of this says the reports are false. It says what kind of thing they are.

And it matters less than it seems. Within Dzogchen itself, teachers warn against taking the shrinking body for the rainbow body. The rainbow body is first a realization. When the fourth vision is complete, everything, the body included, is seen as the display of the five lights. That seeing is the rainbow body. What happens to the corpse is at most a sign of it, and not always a sign at all. Some teachers call a body that shrinks and disappears a partial rainbow body. A sign that the work was not quite finished.

So the second correction: keep the reports as reports. The realization does not wait on them.

The third slip is about traditions. Three become one.

The papers put Kālacakra, Nyingma Dzogchen and Bön into one table, as if they were one system with three names. They share words. Light, drops, channels, winds. But they are not the same path.

Kālacakra speaks of empty form. In its night and day yogas, vivid images appear: smoke, a mirage, fireflies, a lamp, a flame, the moon, the sun, darkness, lightning, a great drop of light. Its texts say these images are made of no atoms at all. They go beyond existence and beyond non-existence. They are compared to the image a young girl sees in a divining mirror. Vivid. Clear. And nothing behind the glass.

This is the most beautiful line in all four papers. The Italian scholar Giacomella Orofino traces it in her study of light in the tantras. And it fits the view here closely. What appears is vivid, and it is not found.

But Kālacakra's goal is not the rainbow body. It speaks of a union of empty form and unchanging bliss. The word for that is union, not body of light. Joining the two makes a neat table, and loses both.

Bön, in turn, has its own Dzogchen, its own lamps and visions, and its own rainbow body. One of its great masters, Shardza Tashi Gyaltsen, is said to have attained it in 1934. Bön also has rituals that Buddhism does not frame the same way. The four guests, who receive offerings. A dough shaped in the hand and offered to beings one owes a debt to.

Even the tables of colours shift. Which element goes with which Buddha, which wisdom, which poison: these differ from text to text. Any single table is one lineage's pairing. It is a map for practice. It is not a chart of what the world is made of.

So the third correction: let each tradition speak in its own voice.

One more caution belongs here. One paper presents the Bön element practices as a plan for health and even for mental health. That they draw illness and trauma out of the body. Nothing like this has been tested. These are spiritual practices. They do not replace a doctor, or care for the mind. And forced breathing, or trying to stay awake through sleep, has left some practitioners sleepless and unsettled. Such practices need a qualified teacher.

There is one more thing the papers carry, and it is worth keeping. A Tibetan image. Raw leather is stiff. Rub butter into it under the sun, and it grows soft. But a leather bag used only to store butter, year after year, slowly hardens like wood. Then no butter will soften it again. Knowledge never rubbed in by practice does the same. It hardens.

That may be the best way to read these four papers. Not as facts to store, but as an invitation to look.

So, what turns into light?

Read with emptiness, the answer is quiet. Nothing found turns into light. The body appeared solid because it was held. When the holding ends, it no longer appears so. Nothing is produced. Nothing is lost. The same hardness, the same warmth, the same colour, vivid and appearing as their conditions meet.

That is why the rainbow body is not the measure of insight. And it is not a goal to aim at. To practise for a body of light is still to practise for something.

The lights were never a stuff behind the world. They were the world, seen without grasping.
