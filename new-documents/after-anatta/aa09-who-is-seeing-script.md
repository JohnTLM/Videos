# Who Is Seeing?

The Buddha told a wandering ascetic named Bahiya: in the seen, there will be just the seen. No one behind the eyes, doing the seeing.

Nagarjuna, in chapter three of his Fundamental Verses, says: without a seer, there is no seen. And without the seen, there is no seer.

Read quickly, these two sound opposed. The Buddha takes the seer away. Nagarjuna seems to put it back, and then tie it to the seen. This video is about why they say the same thing.

This is After Anatta: notes by John Tan, known as Thusness.

Start with how we usually talk. "I see the tree." There is someone here, the seer. There is something there, the tree. And there is an act that crosses the gap between them, the seeing. Subject, verb, object. Three parts.

That shape feels like a fact about the world. It is a fact about sentences. A sentence holds a subject still, so that the verb can happen to it. The habit runs so deep that we feel it must be so. Every action needs someone doing it. Every seeing needs a seer.

This is the logic of substance. Things are there first, finished and on their own. Then they act, and are acted on.

Bahiya was told to drop that shape, not by arguing, but by looking. In seeing, just the seen. When this is tasted, it is very plain. There is the tree, vivid and bright. Look for a seer standing behind it, and none is found. Not a seer hidden somewhere else. Simply none. The seeing has no centre. This is the insight of anatta, no self. It is not a theory. It is direct.

But insight is not yet understanding. Many who have tasted it keep the old grammar in their thinking. They say "there is no seer", and still picture a subject that went missing, a gap where it used to be. Or they turn the seer into a big, impersonal awareness, and the subject is back, wearing a larger coat.

This is where Nagarjuna comes in. He does not give a new experience. He shows why the old grammar cannot hold.

Watch how he argues. He takes the logic of substance at its word. You say a seer is there, on its own side. Very well: is that seer seeing, or not?

If it is not seeing, in what sense is it a seer? A seer that does not see is a seer in name only.

If it is seeing, then the seeing is counted twice. Once to make it a seer, and once more for the seeing it does. Nagarjuna runs the same test on walking, in chapter two. The walker does not walk. Not because nobody walks, but because "walker" and "walking" are not two things, one doing the other.

So the seer is not found with seeing, and not found without it. Then he turns to the seen. A thing is only "seen" in relation to seeing. Take seeing away, and nothing is left that is seen.

Now read his verse again: without a seer, there is no seen. He is not saying, "there is a seer, and it leans on the seen." He is saying the seer cannot stand on its own. Neither can the seen. Each is named only in relation to the other. Take away the relation, and neither is left over.

That is not a seer put back. It is the seer taken apart, inside the very grammar that made it.

So many readers get Nagarjuna backwards. They hear "seer and seen depend on each other", and picture two solid things, joined by a thread. But "depend" here does not join two things. It shows there were never two things there to join. This is the logic of dependency. Look for the thing apart from what it depends on, and it is not found.

Nagarjuna uses fire and fuel the same way, in chapter ten. Fire is not a thing that arrives and grabs the wood. Wood is only fuel in relation to burning. Burning is only fire in relation to fuel. Neither is the other, and neither stands apart.

So the two teachings meet. Bahiya's teaching is the direct taste: in seeing, just the seen. Nagarjuna's is the same taste, said in logic. One shows that no seer is found when we look. The other shows why no seer could be found, however we look.

Each needs the other. Insight without the logic drifts back into nouns. The seer returns as a witness, a ground, a vast awareness. Logic without insight stays a clever argument about words. Together, the view and the taste agree.

But there is one more step, and it is easy to miss. Once the seer is gone, the mind wants to keep something. "Fine, no seer. Just seeing, then. Pure verb." But a verb can be made into a thing as easily as a noun. If "seeing" becomes a stream, a process, a field that everything happens in, the substance is back. It has only moved from the subject to the verb.

So the seeing, too, is looked for. Apart from the colour, the light, the eye and the tree, where is it? Not found. Seer, seeing and seen are named together, on conditions. None is found on its own. In Nagarjuna's words, whatever arises in dependence, that is emptiness. And it is the middle way.

None of this takes anything away. The tree is still green. You still read road signs and catch a ball. Because nothing is there from its own side, things can change, meet and work. If a seer were truly fixed, it could never learn to see differently.

So why must it be said as subject, verb and object? It need not be. That shape is useful for talking. It is not the shape of what appears. "I see the tree" can stay, as a convenient way of speaking. Nothing has to be found behind the words.

Try it now. Look at something near you. Notice the colour and the shape, vivid, just as they are. Then look for the one who is seeing. Do not argue. Just look. Then look for the seeing itself, apart from what is seen.

What is left is not a blank. It is the scene, bright and complete, with no one standing behind it, and nothing standing under it.

In seeing, just the seen. Now you know why.
