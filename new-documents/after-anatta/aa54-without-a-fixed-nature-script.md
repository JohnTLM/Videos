# Dependent Arising Section 3 Part 2: Without a Fixed Nature

How does grey matter give rise to the felt redness of red? Many people think it is the deepest open question we have.

I am John Tan, known as Thusness. These are my own notes, from my own practice and contemplation.

In Part 1, I set out our default mode of thinking. Faced with anything that appears, we ask what it is, really. We look for what it is made of, mind, matter or some third stuff. And we cannot rest until a ground is found. Taken apart, that turned out to be a habit, not reality.

Now I take the habit to the place it is felt most sharply.

My claim is simple to state. Seeing emptiness does not solve this problem. It shows a problem that was never there. Not because the question is too deep. Because what it stands on is not found.

First, the problem, stated fairly.

The philosopher David Chalmers named it the hard problem of consciousness. Science can explain, at least in principle, how the brain picks out colours, stores memories and steers the body. He called these the easy problems. Not because they are simple, but because we know what an answer would look like.

The hard problem is different. Why is any of this felt? Why is there something it is like to see red, rather than processing going on in the dark?

Tell the whole story of wavelengths, cones and neurons. The felt red still seems left over. As if it could be taken away, and the story would stay the same. Philosophers call this distance the explanatory gap.

This is a serious question, asked by careful people. I do not want to make it smaller than it is.

Now look at what has to be in place before the question can be asked.

First, matter must have a fixed nature. And that nature must be to lack experience. Galileo set this out four hundred years ago. Tastes, smells and colours, he said, live only in the one who senses them. Shape, number and motion belong to bodies. Physics has been written that way ever since. Mass, charge and wavelength out in the world. Redness and bitterness somewhere else.

Second, mind must have a fixed nature too. And that nature must be to be felt. Inner, private, qualitative.

Grant both, and the question is forced. How does something whose very nature is to lack experience produce something whose very nature is experience?

And it can never be answered. Nāgārjuna gave the reason long ago. If something existed by its own nature, it could not become otherwise. A nature that turns into its opposite was never a nature.

So the gap is real. But only after two fixed natures have been put in place. The gap was made by the starting point.

Let me show the same move with something so ordinary that no one has ever found it a problem.

Close your hand. There is a fist. Open it. The fist is gone. Nobody is puzzled. Nobody asks where the fist went.

Now let me make it puzzling. I only need the same two steps.

First, give the hand a fixed nature. Bones, tendons and skin, complete in itself, and by its nature without any fist in it. List every bone and every angle. Nowhere in the list is there a fist.

Second, give the fist a fixed nature too. A thing in its own right. Hard, compact, able to hit.

Now ask. How does a hand, whose nature is to have no fist in it, give rise to a fist? Where was the fist before the hand closed? Where does it go when the hand opens? Describe every bone of the closed hand, and the fist still seems left over. There is my explanatory gap. Call it the hard problem of the fist. No amount of anatomy will ever bridge it.

Yet nothing was ever missing. Fist names the hand closed. It is not a second thing hiding in the hand. And the hand is not a thing whose nature is to lack it. The problem was made in the two steps. Just close the hand.

Mind and matter are the same shape. Define matter as complete, and by its nature without experience. Define experience as a thing in its own right. Then ask how the one produces the other. We made it unsolvable, when no such issue was there to begin with.

One caution. I am not saying mind is a shape the brain takes, the way a fist is a shape the hand takes. That would make matter the floor again. The hand is singled out just as the fist is. The example shows only the shape of the mistake. Fix two natures. Then look for the bridge.

Why do the two steps feel so natural? I set that out in Part 1. The worldview that there must be things with natures of their own is in place before any question is asked. The hard problem is the sentence, matter produces mind, turned into a question.

Emptiness is exactly this. Without essence. Without a fixed nature. The old word for a fixed nature is svabhāva: what a thing would be from its own side, apart from its conditions.

Empty does not mean that nothing is there. The tea is hot. The red is red. The neurons fire. What is empty is the nature each one seemed to carry from its own side.

Turn this on the two boxes.

Matter has no fixed nature of lacking experience. Mass, charge and wavelength were never given apart from what is measured, seen and reported. They are singled out, by instruments and agreements, from what appears. A world complete without experience is that abstraction, taken for the ground. Then experience has to be squeezed out of it.

Mind has no fixed nature of being felt, either. Looked for, no inner container is found. No light switched on before its objects. Consciousness is always of something, through something. The Buddha said that eye consciousness arises dependent on the eye and forms. Seeing does not see itself.

So neither side owns its characteristics. And then there is nothing to cross. No nature of matter has to be overcome before experience can appear. Nothing has to be turned into its opposite. Because nothing had a fixed nature to begin with.

People sometimes hear "without a fixed nature" as a loss. As if the world were thinned out. It is the other way round. For me, this is the heart of it.

Nāgārjuna put it in one line. For whom emptiness is possible, everything is possible.

Because nothing has a fixed nature, differing characteristics can arise, each one dependent on its conditions.

Take red.

Felt redness arises with an eye, light, a surface and attention.

A firing pattern in the visual cortex arises with electrodes, instruments and a laboratory.

A wavelength of about six hundred and fifty nanometres arises with a spectrometer, and the theory that defines it.

Each is real within its conditions. Each is reliable, and each can be acted on. The painter mixes. The neurologist treats. The engineer builds. None of them is what red really is, with the others as its effects or its shadows.

So where is the redness? Not in the rose. Not in the brain. Not in a mind. It belongs to the coming together. The way a chord belongs to no single string. Pluck one string, and listen for the chord in it. It is not there. Yet the chord is not somewhere else. And it is not nothing.

Now see what the hard problem asks. It takes one set of characteristics, the wavelength and the firing, and treats it as the owner of a fixed nature. Then it asks it to produce another set, the felt red. When nothing has a fixed nature, nothing is basic. And when nothing is basic, nothing has to be derived from it.

Here is where I part from most of the answers on offer. And the parting matters.

Many serious thinkers have seen that the two fixed natures cause the trouble. So they change one of them, or put something new underneath both.

Idealism says all is mind, and matter is its appearance. Panpsychism says matter was experiential all along, down to its smallest parts. Neutral monism says one hidden stuff, neither mental nor physical, shows up as both. Others put process, or information, under everything.

I respect each of these. Each one sees something true about the old split. But each keeps a fixed nature somewhere. It has only moved. And wherever a fixed nature is kept, the gap comes back, one level down.

If the base is mind, how does it give rise to the solid, shared, measurable world? If the smallest parts have tiny experiences, how do they add up to one felt red? Panpsychists themselves call this the combination problem. The question has not gone. It has changed address.

Emptiness without replacement puts nothing in the place the essence was taken from. Not mind. Not awareness. Not process. Not a ground. It removes what was assumed, and puts nothing in its stead. That is the only way I know for the problem to go away, rather than move somewhere else.

And the clarity of what appears is not a replacement either. It is never absent from what appears. But the moment it is lifted out and named awareness, it is one more thing singled out. Not the stuff everything is made of.

The hard problem is a problem like time divided into past, present and future.

Cut time into three, and give each part a nature of its own. The past kept somewhere. The future waiting. The present a thin slice we stand on. Then ask how the past passes into the present. No answer can be found. If the present had a fixed nature, it could never become the past. Nāgārjuna shows that the three times are defined through each other. And no time is found apart from what changes. Without a fixed nature, change is simply what happens. And the clocks still work.

Cut what appears into mind and matter, and give each a nature of its own. Then ask how matter gives rise to mind. No bridge can be found, for the same reason.

There is a third form. A princess, Elisabeth of Bohemia, put it to Descartes. How can a soul that only thinks move a body that is only extended in space? Give mind and body fixed, separate natures, and a thought can never move an arm. Without them, the intending and the moving arise together, within their conditions. The doer is named on the doing, and the doing on the doer. The arm lifts. Nobody has to cross over to lift it.

All three are the same move. Fix the natures first. Then look for the bridge. In every case, the bridge cannot be found, because the two sides were never found standing apart. And in every case, the ordinary thing still works. The train leaves at nine. The hand reaches for the cup. The red is seen.

So I do not say the hard problem is solved. I say it does not arise.

Someone once asked the Buddha where a fire goes when it goes out. North, south, east or west? The Buddha said, that does not apply. The fire burned dependent on grass and sticks. When they are used up, there is no where for it to go. The question was not too hard. It assumed a fire with a nature of its own, which would then have to go somewhere.

How does matter give rise to mind? That is the same kind of question. It assumes a matter with a nature of its own, which would then have to produce something else.

What remains is ordinary, and it stays important. Science keeps working out which conditions go with which experiences. Medicine keeps acting on the brain. Anaesthesia stops pain. A stroke can take away speech. This shows dependence, plainly. It does not show production by a brain standing on its own.

This is not a retreat to "all is mind". And it is not "nothing is there". The red is seen. The tea is tasted. The arm lifts. What is denied is only the fixed nature, on either side, that the problem needed.

The reasoning shows that the gap was built into the question. Seeing makes it plain.

Take a sip of tea. Look for the point where matter turns into feeling. Where the warm liquid stops being only matter, and becomes the taste. There is no such point. There is the warmth. The bitterness. The swallow. Nothing stands outside the tasting, waiting to be turned into it. And no taster is found behind it, receiving the tea from the other side of a gap.

When this is seen, something more than a question goes. The mind that took things to have a fixed nature had a way of looking at everything. And that way turns too.

Before, appearances looked mechanical, fixed and rigid. Matter was dead stuff, pushed along by other dead stuff. Mind was a light shut inside, looking out at it. Every sight and sound was a finished thing, set in its place, waiting to be explained by something behind it.

After, the same appearances are met as spontaneous, and luminously plastic.

Spontaneous, because nothing stands behind them making them happen. The red appears whole, just when its conditions meet.

Luminous, because appearing is never in the dark. Every appearance is clear, known as it appears. That clarity is not a light shining from behind. It is the appearance's own.

Plastic, because nothing has a fixed nature. Characteristics are not locked into things. They arise, change and fall away with their conditions.

Plastic does not mean vague. And it does not mean anything goes. The red is precise. The tea can name its garden. The medicine works. It is precisely because nothing is fixed that things can arise exactly as their conditions allow.

The world does not lose its order. It loses its rigidity.
