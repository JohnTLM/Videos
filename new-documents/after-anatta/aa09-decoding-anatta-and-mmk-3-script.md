# Decoding Anatta and MMK Chapter 3

The Buddha told a wandering ascetic named Bahiya: in the seen, there will be just the seen. No one behind the eyes, doing the seeing.

Nagarjuna, in chapter three of his Fundamental Verses, says: without a seer, there is no seen. And without the seen, there is no seer.

Many people are confused when they hear these two together. The Buddha takes the seer away. Nagarjuna seems to put it back, and then tie it to the seen. So they see a contradiction. There is none. One is the direct insight. The other is how the logic of dependency expresses that same insight and taste.

This is After Anatta: notes by John Tan, known as Thusness.

I want to make this as plain as I can, because everything after anatta turns on it. It also answers a second question. When I tell people that, to me, dependent arising is spontaneous presence, they ask why. How can the hard logic of Nagarjuna lead to something as effortless as spontaneous presence? It is the same answer, carried one step further.

Start with how we usually talk. "I see the tree." There is someone here, the seer. There is something there, the tree. And there is an act that crosses the gap between them, the seeing. Subject, verb, object. Three parts.

That shape feels like a fact about the world. It is a fact about sentences. A sentence holds a subject still, so that the verb can happen to it. The habit runs so deep that we feel it must be so. Every action needs someone doing it. Every seeing needs a seer. This is the logic of substance. Things are there first, finished and on their own. Then they act, and are acted on.

We say "the wind blows", as if there were a wind first, and then it did the blowing. But when the blowing stops, no wind is left over, resting somewhere. The blowing is the wind. Keep this in mind. Everything that follows does the same thing to something else.

Bahiya was told to drop that shape, not by arguing, but by looking. In seeing, just the seen. When this is tasted, it is very plain. There is the tree, vivid and bright. Look for a seer standing behind it, and none is found. Not a seer hidden somewhere else. Simply none. The seeing has no centre. This is the insight of anatta, no self. It is not a theory. It is direct.

It is exact to say what falls away. The person does not fall away. You still have a name, catch the bus, keep your promises. Seeing does not stop. It is brighter than before. What falls away is one picture: someone standing behind the action, making it happen. A doer behind the doing.

But insight is not yet understanding. Many who have tasted it keep the old grammar in their thinking. They say "there is no seer", and still picture a subject that went missing, a gap where it used to be. Or they turn the seer into a big, impersonal awareness, and the subject is back, wearing a larger coat.

This is where Nagarjuna comes in. He does not give a new experience. He shows why the old grammar cannot hold. In the substantialist framework, an action must always be started by an agent. Nagarjuna is refuting that. Many think he is affirming it.

Watch how he argues. He takes the logic of substance at its word. You say a seer is there, on its own side. Very well: is that seer seeing, or not? If it is not seeing, in what sense is it a seer? A seer that does not see is a seer in name only. If it is seeing, then the seeing is counted twice. Once to make it a seer, and once more for the seeing it does. So the seer is not found with seeing, and not found without it. Then he turns to the seen. A thing is only "seen" in relation to seeing. Take seeing away, and nothing is left that is seen.

Now read his verse again: without a seer, there is no seen. He is not saying, "there is a seer, and it leans on the seen." He is saying the seer cannot stand on its own. Neither can the seen. Each is named only in relation to the other. Take away the relation, and neither is left over. That is not a seer put back. It is the seer taken apart, inside the very grammar that made it.

So how does "no seen without a seer" become "in the seen, just the seen"? Nagarjuna does not remove a seer that was there. He shows that "seer" was only ever a name for one side of the seeing. Take away the colours, the shapes and the light, and look for what is left over that was the seer. Nothing. Just as "wind" adds nothing to the blowing, "seer" adds nothing to the seeing.

Think of "here" and "there". "Here" makes sense only in relation to "there", and "there" only in relation to "here". Look for "here" by itself, and it is not a place you can stand. The seer is like that "here": the point the scene seems to be seen from. Seen through, there is no "here" behind the view. There is just the view.

This is how the Buddha goes on in his teaching to Bahiya. When in the seen there is just the seen, there is no "you" in it. Then you are neither here, nor there, nor anywhere in between. The Buddha says it from the direct taste. Nagarjuna gives the logic for it: here and there are named together, so neither is found by itself.

And this is why the verse must run both ways. "Just the seen" is easy to misread: the seer gone, and an object out there left standing. That is still the logic of substance, with one side missing. But there is no seen without the seer either. So "just the seen" is the appearing itself, vivid and complete, with nothing standing behind it, and nothing standing apart from it. Seer, seeing and seen are three ways of speaking about one appearing.

So many readers get Nagarjuna backwards. They hear "seer and seen depend on each other", and picture two solid things, joined by a thread. But "depend" here does not join two things. It shows there were never two things there to join. This is the logic of dependency.

Chapter three is not a special case. Nagarjuna makes the same move again and again. Each time he takes something that seems to stand behind an action, doing it. Each time he asks: is it the same as the action, or other than it? Each time it is found as neither.

The walker does not walk. Not because nobody walks, but because "walker" and "walking" are not two things, one doing the other. Fire is not a thing that arrives and grabs the wood. Wood is only fuel in relation to burning, and burning is only fire in relation to fuel. And chapter one goes furthest of all. It takes the doer out of causes. A cause does not stand there first and then make its effect, the way a factory makes goods.

So the two teachings meet. Anatta finds no doer in seeing, and it finds this by looking. Nagarjuna finds no doer anywhere: in the walker, the fire, the cause, the seen. He finds this by reasoning, and carries the same finding to every thing.

It did not happen by itself for me. Anatta dawned around 1997. But for years afterwards my experience and insight were non-substantialist, while my view was not. In experience, the taste was implicitly non-dual. But when the mind tried to understand it, it could not, because its logical thought was always substantial. Insight without the logic drifts back into nouns. The seer returns as a witness, a ground, a vast awareness. Logic without insight stays a clever argument about words. Only together do the view and the taste agree.

We must understand why "found as neither" is emptiness. Take a car. If "car" pointed to something that stands on its own, we could point to it. It could be the parts. It could be a whole, over and above the parts. Or it could be their shape. No single part is the car. All the parts in a box are not a car. Take every part away, and no car is left over to be the whole. And the shape is only the parts in a certain relation. These are all the places a car standing on its own could be. Found in none of them, it has no nature of its own. That not-finding is what "empty" means. Not that the car is made of emptiness. Only that, looked for on its own side, there is nothing there to find.

And still the car drives you home. The search looked for one thing only, a car from its own side. It never touched the car that works. Looked for, it is not found. Met, it is there, doing what cars do. These are not two facts. The seer is the same. The same as the seeing? Then "seer" adds nothing. Apart from it? Then it is nowhere to be found. Neither, so the seer is empty. The seen gets the same test. What is left is not a remainder. It is the seeing, vivid and complete.

There is one more step, and it is easy to miss. Once the seer is gone, the mind wants to keep something. "Fine, no seer. Just seeing, then. Pure verb." But a verb can be made into a thing as easily as a noun. If "seeing" becomes a stream, a process, a field that everything happens in, the substance is back. It has only moved from the subject to the verb. So the seeing, too, is looked for. Apart from the colour, the light, the eye and the tree, where is it? Not found. Seer, seeing and seen are named together, on conditions. None is found on its own.

There is one more place the mind likes to hide something. It comes most of all to practitioners who taste that appearances are one's own clarity. The sound is not out there, heard by someone in here. The sound itself is clear, itself the knowing. The taste is genuine. But read with the old logic, it turns into a conclusion: everything is made of one stuff, and that stuff is mind.

The physical world can seem to point the same way. Weight feels like the most private thing there is. But weight is a pull between the body and the earth. On the Moon it is a sixth. In orbit it is gone, and not a gram of you is lost. Ernst Mach argued that even the body's resistance to being pushed comes from its relation to all the matter in the universe. So where is the line between my body and the world, in the heaviness itself? It is not found. Matter turns light, magical and plastic. And it is easy to take one more step, and say: so it was all mind.

But in Buddhism everything is a phenomenon, mind and matter alike, and the knowing is on that list too. So mind cannot be the ground of the rest. It is on the list, not under it, and it meets the same test. There is no hearing apart from the sound, the ear and the attention. Making matter the floor and making mind the floor are the same mistake. "Own clarity" does not mean owned. The clarity is the appearance's own, not lent to it by a knower behind it. Mind-like is not made of mind. If everything is my mind, then appearances belong to someone after all, and the owner is back in the largest coat of all. Appearing is clear and complete, and it belongs to no one.

Now the point everything depends on. There are two ways to say no. One way says no, and then puts something else in its place. "There is no small self, so there must be a big Self." "There is no seer, so there must be pure awareness." Each time, the doer leaves by the front door and comes back by the back door.

Nagarjuna's no is the other kind. It removes, and adds nothing. No seer, and nothing in the seer's place. No producing cause, and nothing producing instead. Even dependence is not kept as a new thing. The conditions are not a web or a machine standing behind what appears. They, too, are not found from their own side.

Now go back to the wind. If no wind stands behind the blowing, pushing it, the blowing is not being done. It is simply the blowing, complete as it is. Widen that to everything. If nothing makes the sound of a car going by, the sound is not a product. It is not waiting for anything to finish it. It shows up whole and vivid, exactly as its conditions meet. If nothing makes the thought, the thought appears and goes by itself. No one has to hold it, or push it away.

That is what spontaneous presence means. Appearing is already complete, because nothing is hidden behind it, doing it. It is the last of the seven phases: presence is spontaneously perfected. Not a new state reached at the end, but how it always was, once the doer is gone everywhere. Anatta is a seal, not a stage.

This is also why it is effortless. It is not difficult to see, especially in meditation. There is an energetic openness and release, as if everything is in its own self so, without an actor. That lets us understand that holding, pushing and managing are the agent itself: one happening split into two, a doer and its doing, through a confusion that language and its structure lead us into. When no doer is found anywhere, there is no one left to push.

Two cautions keep this right. Spontaneous does not mean uncaused. The car still has to go by for its sound to appear. What is gone is the maker, not the order. And spontaneous presence is not a source, a ground that gives off appearances. That would bring the doer back once more, in its most beautiful coat.

So when I say that dependent arising is spontaneous presence, this is what I mean. They are one appearing, described in two ways. Dependent arising is how it is described when we analyse. Spontaneous presence is the same appearing when the habit of fixing stops: complete, effortless, with nothing behind it.

One thing must be said plainly here, or all of this can be misheard. You cannot talk about phenomena without clarity. A sound is clear as sound. A colour is vivid as colour. A thought is known as it appears. Dependent arising is never a machine working in the dark, waiting for a knower to light it up. The clarity is always there.

So the question is never whether there is clarity. It is only whether the clarity is abstracted out and reified, made into a thing. Clarity shows up in every sight, every sound, every thought. The mind notices this sameness and lifts it out: awareness, presence, the one thing that is there in all of them. Then it takes that abstraction to stand on its own. It becomes the background that everything appears in, the witness that knows it all, the Mind that gives off appearances. The substrate is back, now made of clarity.

Left as it is, the clarity is not lifted out. It stays the appearing's own: this sound's clearness, this colour's vividness, found only as them, on their conditions. It is not a second thing the sound has.

The same lifting out, done to other things, is the same mistake. Abstract the conditions, and you get a web or a machine behind the world. Abstract the appearing, and you get an object out there. Abstract emptiness, and you get a ground, a field, a space everything sits in. Dependent arising does not take the clarity away. It only stops it from being made into a thing.

When nothing is abstracted, nothing stands behind the appearing: no maker, no knower, no background. Appearing presents itself, clear by itself and complete by itself, as its conditions meet. That is spontaneous presence.

None of this takes anything away. The tree is still green. You still read road signs and catch a ball. Because nothing is there from its own side, things can change, meet and work.

In one line: anatta finds no doer in seeing. Nagarjuna finds no doer anywhere, and puts nothing in its place. With no doer anywhere, and the clarity not lifted out and made into a thing, everything appears clear and complete, by itself, as its conditions meet.

Try it now. Look, or listen. A colour, a car, a fan, a voice. Notice it, vivid, just as it is. Then look for the one who is seeing or hearing. Do not argue. Just look. Now look for the seeing apart from what is seen. Look for something that makes it happen. Nothing is found. Do not put anything in its place.

What is left is not a blank. It is the scene, bright and whole, coming and going by itself, with no one standing behind it, and nothing standing under it.

In seeing, just the seen. Now you know why.
