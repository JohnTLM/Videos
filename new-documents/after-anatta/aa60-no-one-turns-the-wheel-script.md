# Dependent Arising Section 5 Part 2: No One Turns the Wheel

Who ages and dies? It sounds like the simplest question there is.

I am John Tan, known as Thusness. These are my own notes, from my own practice and contemplation.

Part 1 set out the twelve links. From ignorance to ageing and death. Forward, as suffering arising. In reverse, as suffering ending. And it ended on a question the list does not answer. Who is turning the wheel?

A monk once asked the Buddha directly. What is ageing and death, and whose is it? The Buddha did not answer with a name. Not a valid question, he said. Whether one says the life and the body are the same, or that they are different, there is no living of the holy life. Avoiding both, he teaches by the middle. With birth as condition, ageing and death.

A brahmin asked it another way. Is the one who acts the same as the one who meets the result? That is one extreme, the Buddha said. Is the one who acts someone else? That is the other. Avoiding both, he teaches the chain.

Both times, the question asks for an owner. Both times, the reply gives the chain, and no owner.

Nāgārjuna gives the twelve links a whole chapter of his Middle Way. And it is an unusual chapter.

For twenty five chapters, he shows that nothing is found standing on its own. Not motion. Not the senses. Not the self. Not time. Not cause. Then he stops refuting. He simply sets out the twelve links, one after another, and explains how suffering works.

A reader may be surprised. After all that unfindability, how can he speak so plainly of one link arising from another?

As I see it, that is exactly the point. Emptiness does not stop the world working. A world of things with their own natures could not work at all. Here, dependent arising is shown doing its everyday job. Producing suffering while ignorance is present. And ending it when ignorance ends.

And the working chain is the very chain that was found empty. A chain whose links had natures of their own could never stop. Ignorance with its own nature could never be removed. Because nothing in the chain has a nature of its own, it works. And because it works that way, it can end.

The chapter opens with rebirth. Covered over by ignorance, one makes formations, and through these actions goes to a new destiny. With formations as condition, consciousness enters a new destiny.

Enters sounds like a traveller moving house, carrying a suitcase from one life to the next. It is not meant that way. Formations are not deeds done and gone. They shape a direction, the way a river cuts its bed. And what follows flows along that bed. Nothing passes over. The old flame lights a new one.

The chain ends with a phrase the Buddha used often. In this way, this whole mass of suffering comes to be.

The word for whole means only. Unmixed. Nothing else in it. Candrakīrti reads it as, without a self, or anything belonging to a self. There is no one who owns the suffering. There is only the suffering, arising from its conditions.

Then comes the line I take as the heart of the chapter. The one who does not know makes the formations that are the root of the round. The one who does not know is the maker. The one who knows is not, because they see how things are.

The maker of the round is not a person. It is not knowing. This is not a fate imposed from outside, and not a punishment. Take away the not knowing, and nothing is left to make the formations.

If not knowing is the maker, what becomes of the one who acts?

Nāgārjuna answered that earlier. The one who acts depends on the action. The action depends on the one who acts. We see no other way they could be established.

The one who is bound, the one who acts, the one who reaps. Each is named together with the binding, the action and the fruit. Not found before them. Not found apart from them.

He gives an image. Suppose a magician makes a magical person, and that person in turn makes another. The doer is like the first. The action, like the second. Afflictions, actions, bodies, doers and fruits are like a city in the clouds, like a mirage, like a dream.

Like a dream does not mean nothing happens. In a dream, a fall still frightens. The acting is real as acting. What is not found is someone standing behind it, turning the wheel.

This is where the teaching is most often misheard. So I want to be plain.

Everything that matters is kept. Kind acts tend toward happiness, and cruel acts toward suffering. Practice changes a person. One is responsible for what one has done. The conventional I keeps its promises, and meets the results of what it has done.

The twelve links keep karma, rebirth, and the path that ends suffering. What is left out is only the owner.

And I want to be equally plain about what this does not show. Emptiness removes one objection to rebirth. Since nothing ever has a carrier, rebirth does not need one. But removing a bad argument against something is not the same as giving a good argument for it. Whether rebirth happens is not proven by emptiness.

If not knowing makes the formations, where does it get its grip?

Nāgārjuna traces it back. Freedom comes from the ending of action and affliction. Action and affliction come from conceptual construction. And these come from elaboration. The mind's endless spinning of it is, it is not, mine, not mine.

Elaboration stops in emptiness. So the cure is not a better belief about the twelve links. It is seeing.

The next time I am hurt, I can try the Buddha's question. Not as philosophy, but as looking. Who is hurt?

Not to deny the hurt. It is there, sharp and vivid. But to look for the one it belongs to. What I find is the words, the feeling, the tightening, the story starting up. Each is fully present. The owner, looked for, is not found.

This is Section 5, Part 2 of the Dependent Arising series.

And when the owner is not found, the story has less to stand on. The wheel turns more slowly when no one is pushing it.
