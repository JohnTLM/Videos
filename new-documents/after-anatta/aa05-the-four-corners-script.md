# The Four Corners

This moment ceases as it arises. Does it arise, or does it cease?

The question offers four answers. It arises. It ceases. It both arises and ceases. It neither arises nor ceases. Each one fails.

This is the fifth step of The Logic, from After Anatta: notes by John Tan, known as Thusness. The step asks why each answer falls, why all four fall together, and why "neither" is not a secret fifth place to stand.

These are called the four corners. When the Buddha was asked whether the awakened one exists after death, he set the question aside, with all four answers. And Nagarjuna's most famous verse has the same shape. Not from itself, not from another, not from both, and not without a cause: no thing is ever found to have arisen. The four corners are what the logic of substance asks about anything, and they feel complete.

It arises. Then the moment is there, from its own side. But a thing there from its own side needs nothing to become what it is. There is nothing for arising to do, and nothing could make it go. This is the view of permanence. Yet this moment ceases as it arises.

It ceases. Then something real passes into nothing. This is the view of annihilation. An absence is always the absence of something. "No milk in the jug" makes sense only against milk. If the arisen moment is not found, its ceasing has nothing to borrow from.

Both. Then two failed answers are joined, with the faults of each. Light and darkness cannot sit in one spot. Two wrong answers do not make a right one.

Neither. The subtlest corner. But "neither arising nor ceasing" is built out of arising and ceasing. It makes sense only if those two were set up first. And if something really were neither, who could know it, and by what?

So the four corners fall together, because they rest on one thing. Each assumes there is something findable, a bearer with a status of its own, there to exist or not exist. In the koan that bearer is the "it". The old word for an extreme means an edge: a place where one could stand. Each corner is a place for a thing to be.

The Buddha showed this most simply to a wanderer named Vacchagotta. When a fire goes out, in which direction has it gone? "That does not apply," Vacchagotta says. Not because the fire went too far to see, or into nothing. The question asks for a fire apart from its burning, and no such fire was ever there. Nagarjuna adds that the four answers fail even while the Buddha is alive. The question was never about death.

This is not a trick that breaks logic. Is the present king of France bald? Neither yes nor no, because there is no present king of France. The question has nothing to be about. The koan has exactly this shape.

Here the habit makes its most refined move. All four corners have fallen, so surely the truth lies beyond them. The Absolute. The Ineffable. Pure Awareness, prior to every concept. Or a timeless Now the moments pass through.

The author knows this pull from the inside. In his words: "In the years of I AM, I negated a great deal: thoughts, sensations, the body, the world, not this, not that. What was left seemed beyond every description, and so beyond every question. It felt like the one thing the corners could not touch. It was in fact the one thing I had never looked for."

Nagarjuna closes this door himself. Rejecting all four answers is not a fifth theory. It removes, and installs nothing. One should not say "empty", nor "not empty", nor both, nor neither. Yet they are spoken, to designate. "Empty" is a word that does a job: it undoes a grasp. In the author's words: "Beyond words means there is nothing further to grasp, not something words fail to reach." Read any other way, the "beyond" is the same substance with a quieter name. The fifth corner gets no exemption.

With the logic of substance, a moment is here, and arising and ceasing happen to it. One corner must be true, and the koan feels like a trap. With the logic of dependency, no moment is found standing there first. A sound is sounding. Seen from before, it is called arising. Seen from after, ceasing. This is not the fourth corner, giving the "it" a strange status. It gives the "it" no status of its own at all. And the sound is fully heard.

None of this makes the four corners forbidden words. Nagarjuna uses them as medicine. Everything is real. Not real. Both. Neither. Chandrakirti reads these as steps for different people, each answering a particular grasping. "The bell began at noon" is still true. The sound starts and ends. What fails is an "it" found doing the starting and the ending.

What is left when the four corners fall is not a blank. Nagarjuna's word for what stops is elaboration: the mind spinning out "it is", "it is not", "it came", "it went", around something it takes to be there. When that "it" is not found, the spinning stops by itself. That stopping is called peace. A thought appears. Where did it come from? Where does it stay? Where did it go? Nothing has the whereabouts the questions ask for. Yet the thought was thought, and you acted on it. One caution: a quiet mind is not the same as this. Stillness with the habit intact has only paused.

The four corners can be asked of anything. The self. Does it exist? Then it could never learn or change. Does it not? Then who remembers and keeps promises? Each answer looks for a self found on its own. Thinking, choosing and caring go on as before.

An object. Sit down, and you have a lap. Stand up, and a child asks: where did your lap go? Every answer sounds a little foolish. There never was a lap apart from the sitting, and nothing was lost.

A cause. Does a sprout come from itself, from another, from both, or from no cause? None is found. Yet rice seed gives rice, never barley, and the farmer's work is not wasted.

The person. Where has someone gone who has died? "That does not apply." No person was found apart from the living, even while they lived. As one flame lights another, one life conditions the next. Love and grief stay exactly where they were.

So, try it. Listen to one sound until it ends. Ask: did it arise? Did it cease? Both? Neither? After each, look for the "it", a sound apart from the sounding. None is found, and the sound was heard in full.

And when you reach "it is beyond all that", look for that too, the way you looked for the sound.
