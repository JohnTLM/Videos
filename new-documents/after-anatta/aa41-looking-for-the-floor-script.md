# Dependent Arising Section 2 Part 2: Looking for the Floor

When the thing is not found, where does the habit go next?

I am John Tan, known as Thusness. These are my own notes, from my own practice and contemplation.

In Part 1, a bell, a red flower and a passing thought were each looked for, and not found. Each was vivid, not found, and dependently arisen, all at once.

The habit does not give up when it hears this. It retreats.

It agrees that the sound does not last, and moves one step down. The sound changes, it says, but surely at each instant something is really there.

It agrees that the flower comes apart, into petals, cells and molecules. And it moves one step down again. Surely the smallest pieces are real.

And if even the pieces fail, it has one more place to stand. Surely space itself is real. The one empty room that everything sits in.

This is not a theory anyone reasons into. It is a habit of seeing, in place before any thought. The tradition calls it innate grasping. And it is hard to dislodge, because it gives ground without giving up.

The retreat has a long history. The Abhidharma schools agreed that the person and the cup are only designations. Underneath them, they looked for what was not designated. Partless moments. Partless atoms. And, among the unconditioned elements, space. These were the floor. And the everyday world was built on it.

So here I follow the habit down. Three times. In time, in matter, and in space.

The point is not to disprove time, matter or space. Moments, particles and room stay as useful as they ever were. What is looked for is only the one thing the habit wanted. A piece of reality, however small or however vast, that is real in itself.

First, a moment.

Strike the bell again. The tone rings out, and fades. It is never the same from one instant to the next.

Surely, then, the instant is real. The sound changes, but at each moment something is truly there. A now, however brief, before it gives way to the next. String the instants together, and you have the ringing.

Or, and it is a big or, look for that instant.

Does the present moment last for some time, however short? If it lasts at all, it has a beginning, a middle and an end. Nāgārjuna points this out in the Precious Garland. A moment with an end must also have a beginning and a middle. So it is not partless.

Divide it. Its first part is already gone. Its last part is not yet here. Only the middle is present. But the middle lasts too. So divide it again.

Each time I look for the present, it splits. Into a past that is no longer, and a future that is not yet. And no present is found standing between them.

Then perhaps the true instant has no length at all. A mere point of now. But a point with no length is not long enough to be anything. Line up as many as you like, and they never add up to a second. No tone can ring in it.

So either way. A present with duration falls apart, into what is gone and what is to come. A present without duration cannot make up time. The partless moment is not found.

Nāgārjuna then turns to the three marks every conditioned thing was given. Arising, abiding, and ceasing. Looked for one by one, none of the three is found. And the chapter ends, not with a denial, but with an image. Like a magic show. Like a dream. Like a city of gandharvas in the clouds. So arising, so abiding, so ceasing have been taught.

One thing needs saying clearly. The dividing is a search, not a discovery of tiny pieces. It does not show that time is made of infinitely many smaller instants. And it does not reveal a timeless reality behind the clock. It shows only that no present is found standing by itself, at any size.

And nothing is missing. The tone still fades on time. The clock still keeps time. And I still arrive for the meeting.

Second, an atom.

Pick a petal from the red flower. Tear it, and tear it again. Under a lens, it is cells. Under a better one, molecules. Under the best, the smallest bits physics can name.

Surely, at the bottom, there is something solid. However far the flower comes apart, the pieces are really there. The smallest piece, the one with no parts, is the brick the whole world is built of.

Or, and again it is a big or, look at that brick.

The partless atom was the Abhidharma schools' idea. The Yogācāra master Vasubandhu argued against it, in his Twenty Verses, with a test I find hard to forget.

For anything to be built out of atoms, the atoms must join. So place one atom in the middle, with neighbours on all six sides. Above and below. In front and behind. Left and right.

If all six touch the same spot, they all occupy one place. Then however many atoms are heaped together, the heap is no bigger than one atom. A mountain would be the size of one atom.

If they touch different places, then the atom has a left and a right, a top and a bottom, a front and a back. Six sides are six parts. The partless atom has parts.

I press the question one step further. And this step is mine, not his. Where two atoms meet, is the edge part of this atom, or of that one? If the edge has any width, it has two sides of its own, and the question comes back. If it has no width, it is nowhere. And nothing meets there.

So either way, no partless piece of matter is found. Again, the search does not reveal a smaller dust underneath. It shows only that the brick the habit wanted does not turn up.

Here I part from Vasubandhu. He stopped there. He concluded that the outer world is not found, and kept the mind as what is really there. But as Part 1 showed, the mind is looked for the same way, and it is not found either. Losing the atom does not hand the floor to the mind. Mind and matter fail together. Neither is the floor under the other.

And nothing is missing. The petal is still soft. Bricks still make walls. And the wall still holds up the roof.

Third, space.

The habit has lost the moment and the atom. It has one more place to stand.

Surely, it says, space at least is real. One still, empty room, in which the bell, the flower and I all sit. It was there before anything was put in it. And it would still be there if everything were taken out.

Or, and it is the biggest or, look for that room.

The eighth century Indian master Śāntarakṣita built his whole Ornament of the Middle Way on one test.

Here is the rule. Whatever is really there, from its own side, must be either really one, or really many.

Really one means a single whole, with no parts. Really many means a collection of such single wholes. There is no third choice. And many is not a separate escape. Many only means several ones, counted together. So if no real one can be found, no real many can be found either.

The whole test comes down to one question. Can a real one be found?

Is space one? There is space here, around the bell. And space over there, around the gate. Is the space around the bell the very same space as the space around the gate?

If it is exactly the same, then the place of the bell is the place of the gate. The bell would be at the gate, and the whole courtyard would shrink to a single spot.

So the space here and the space there must be different. But then space has a part here and a part there. An east and a west. An above and a below. Something with parts is not one.

Is space many, then? A great number of small pieces of space, side by side? But many needs real ones to count. Take one piece. The space just inside the mouth of the bell. It too has a side towards the bronze, and a side towards the air. Divide it, and each half has two sides again. However small the piece, it is never one thing without parts. With no real one, there is nothing for many to be made of.

So space is neither really one nor really many. And so it holds no nature of its own.

That is why Śāntarakṣita reaches for a reflection. A face in a mirror appears clearly, as one face. And it works: I fix my hair by it. Looked for in the glass, there is no face there that is truly one, or truly many. Space is like that. Clearly there, as room. And not found, as a thing standing by itself.

What the test does not take away matters just as much. I can still say one courtyard, or three rooms. I can still count. Everyday one and many work perfectly well, because they are designated. One courtyard, because a wall marks it off. Three rooms, because doors divide them. What fails is only a oneness or a manyness held inside the thing itself.

Nāgārjuna comes at the same point from another side.

Every element has a mark, the sign by which it is known. Earth is known by hardness. Water by wetness. The mark of space is the absence of obstruction. Space is where nothing blocks.

So he asks a simple question. Could space be there first, and then have its mark? His answer: space does not exist in the slightest before its mark.

Picture it in the courtyard. When do I meet space? Only as: nothing blocks here. The gap between the bell and the wall. The open gate I can walk through. The air the bell can swing in.

Take away the bell, the wall, the gate, and the one who walks. What would nothing blocks even mean? There would be nothing to block, and nothing to be blocked.

The open room is singled out together with what bounds it, and with what moves in it. It is not a stage that was waiting first. Nor can the mark come first, and land on space afterwards. Space and its mark are designated together, like the bell and its ringing.

Then is space nothing? Nāgārjuna closes that door as well. Nothing only means the absence of a thing. If space is not found as a thing, it cannot be found as a no thing either. Space is neither existent nor non existent.

So two masters arrive at one place by two roads. One divides space, and finds it neither one nor many. The other asks what space is apart from its mark, and finds nothing there. Neither leaves a container behind. And neither leaves a void.

Two cautions belong here.

First, empty space is not emptiness. The words sound alike, and the mind joins them at once. Space is one thing that appears: the open room, known by nothing blocking. Emptiness is not one more thing that appears. It is how every appearance is. The bell, the wall, and space itself, each lacking a nature of its own. So space is empty too, just as the bell is.

The traditions often use the sky as an image. Open, unstained, without a centre. It is a good image. But an image is not a ground. Turn the sky into the place where everything sits, and the floor is back. Only bigger.

Second, why I borrow only Śāntarakṣita's test. He joined the Middle Way with Yogācāra. In everyday terms, he described things the Yogācāra way: what appears is mind, with no outer object behind it. He was fair to his test, and applied it to the mind as well. But where I part from him is that everyday picture. I follow Nāgārjuna and Candrakīrti. In ordinary life there are bells and courtyards, as well as seeing and hearing. Mind and its objects rise and fall together. So I use his test of space, which cuts cleanly, and leave the rest aside.

And nothing is missing. The bell still hangs in the courtyard. And I still walk through the gate.

Now, when the floor is not found, the mind reaches quickly for one of two pictures.

The first: so everything is made of infinitely many tinier moments, and tinier specks, all the way down. But that keeps the habit, and only shrinks it. Each tinier piece meets the same question as the bigger one, and fails the same way. The search was never a microscope finding smaller things. It is a looking that finds no thing standing by itself, at any size.

The second: so there is nothing at all. A blank behind the clock, the brick and the room. But that is Part 1's last mistake again. The absence is not a place. Not a container. Not a void left over when the floor is gone. Nothing is put where the floor was, because the floor was never there to be taken away.

Three searches, one result. Time breaks into before and after. Matter breaks into this side and that side. Space breaks into here and there. In all three, the floor the habit wanted is not found.

What the search leaves is exactly what was there before it. The tone fading. The petal soft. The room to walk through. All vivid. All working. None of it standing on its own.

So, which holds? By experience, by logic, and by how things work.

By experience. Listen to the bell's tone as it fades. Is there a moment, standing still, that I can point to? Look at a petal. Is there a partless speck I ever meet? Look at the courtyard. Do I ever meet an empty room by itself, before anything is in it? What is met is always the ringing, the softness, the room to move. Never a floor underneath.

By logic. Every candidate for the floor undoes itself the moment it is picked out. Because picking it out needs a before and an after. A this side and a that side. A here and a there. The very dependence it was meant to escape.

By working. The floor view says the world is orderly because something solid sits at the bottom. But a partless, unchanging piece could not arise, combine, or act. The view without a floor explains all of it. The tone fades because the bronze stops trembling. The wall stands because the bricks meet, the mortar sets, and the ground bears it. There is room because nothing obstructs. Order never needed a floor.

So the floor view is not the truer one. It is the default one, carried by habit and by language. The words moment, atom and space each quietly set up one thing, standing behind the word.

None of this makes my everyday words false. There are moments. There is matter. There is room. What fails is only the extra claim. A piece of reality, at the bottom, that is real in itself.

And something loosens when this is seen. The habit kept reaching down for somewhere to stand. When no floor is found anywhere, there is nothing to reach for. And nothing is lost by not reaching.

The ringing, the petal and the courtyard are all still here. Vivid. With nothing under them. Needing nothing under them.

This is Section 2, Part 2 of the Dependent Arising series.
