# Dependent Arising Section 5 Part 1: When This Is, That Is

A harsh word is said in the morning. By evening, a grievance has a life of its own. How did that happen?

I am John Tan, known as Thusness. These are my own notes, from my own practice and contemplation.

Four sections have looked at dependent arising as a view. Whether things are undeniably there, or formed. What a phenomenon is. Mind and matter. And the logic of dependency.

All of that widened the word, from the arising of suffering to everything that appears. But when the Buddha speaks of dependent arising, he mostly means something narrower, and more urgent. How suffering comes about. And how it ends.

He gives it as twelve links. If I leave them out, I have left out where the teaching starts. So this section turns to the twelve links, and stays with them as the tradition teaches them. The next section reads them with Nāgārjuna.

Before the twelve, there is one sentence. The Buddha gives it again and again.

When this is, that is. From the arising of this, that arises. When this is not, that is not. From the ceasing of this, that ceases.

It is short, and easy to pass over. But look at what it does not say. It does not say that this makes that. It does not say a power goes out from this into that. It only says, when. Two things are named together, in arising and in ceasing.

Nothing is passed across. And because nothing is passed across, the second half of the sentence is possible at all. If that had its own nature, it would not cease when this ceases.

Applied to suffering, the sentence becomes a chain.

Ignorance. Not knowing suffering, its arising, its ending, and the way to its ending.

Formations. Intentional shaping, by body, by speech, by mind.

Consciousness. Knowing, through the six senses.

Name and form. Feeling, perception, intention, attention, and the body.

The six senses. The fields where meeting happens.

Contact. Sense, object and consciousness, meeting.

Feeling. Pleasant, painful, or neither.

Craving. Thirst, for more of the pleasant, and an end to the painful.

Clinging. Taking hold. Of pleasures, of views, of rituals, of a self.

Becoming. Taking on a mode of existence.

Birth.

And ageing and death, with sorrow, grief and despair.

In forward order, each conditions the next. Such, the Buddha says, is the arising of this whole mass of suffering. In reverse order, each ends with the ending of the one before. Such is the ending of this whole mass of suffering.

As I read them, the forward order describes how suffering runs while ignorance is present. The reverse order describes how it ends when ignorance ends. A teaching that gave only the first would describe a trap. The twelve links are given so that the trap can be seen, and left.

It is natural to picture the twelve as a line of dominoes, each knocking down the next. The discourses do not keep to that picture.

In one long discourse, the chain stops at consciousness and name and form, and turns back on itself. Consciousness depends on name and form, and name and form on consciousness.

The image given is two sheaves of reeds, standing because they lean on each other. Pull one away, and the other falls.

A line of dominoes has a first domino, and the first one has to stand on its own. Reeds leaning on each other have no first. No first point of the round is found.

The tradition has read this chain at more than one scale.

The classical reading spreads it over three lives. Ignorance and formations in the past life. Consciousness through becoming in this one. Birth, ageing and death in the next. This keeps rebirth at the centre. It is how most of the Buddhist world has learned the twelve links.

Another school allowed four ways of taking the chain. In a single moment. Over a long span. As a connected series. And by stages, across lives.

And some modern teachers, the Thai monk Buddhadāsa best known among them, read the chain mainly as it runs in present experience. Others have criticised this as setting rebirth aside.

I do not think these need to compete. They describe the same pattern at two scales.

Emptiness removes one objection to rebirth, that it would need something to carry over. Nothing ever has a carrier. But that does not prove that rebirth happens. That is a matter for its own evidence. What I can say from practice is that the same chain can be seen in a single afternoon.

Someone says something harsh to me at work.

There is contact. The words, the ear, the knowing of them. There is feeling. Unpleasant, sharp. Then craving. For it to stop. For an apology. For the other to be shown wrong.

Then clinging. To a view: I was wronged. And to the one who was wronged. Then becoming. I take on a whole mode of being, the injured party, with its history and its case.

Then birth. By evening, the grievance has a life of its own, rehearsed in the car, retold at dinner. Then ageing and death. It hardens. It wears me out. In time it fades, leaving sorrow, and a little more readiness for the next one.

And behind it, ignorance and formations. The habit of taking the words, the speaker and myself as things standing on their own. And the old shaping that had me ready to react before the sentence was finished.

Nothing in that afternoon was made by one thing alone. And nothing in it was without conditions.

When Ānanda told the Buddha that dependent arising seemed plain to him, the Buddha stopped him. This dependent arising is deep, he said, and it appears deep.

The twelve links are not hard to memorise. What is hard is to see them run, in a harsh word, or a pleasant memory, without someone at the centre turning the wheel.

This is Section 5, Part 1 of the Dependent Arising series.

When this is, that is. It describes how the knot is tied, and it is also how the knot comes loose.
