# Knowing From Somewhere: The Six Higher Knowledges in a Non-Substantialist View

*NotebookLM source for the video of the bonus article "Knowing From Somewhere". It follows the article's argument in
the order the video should take, with every example and every source. Notes in italics marked "Precision note" say
what a summary must not say; treat them as binding.*

**The view in one paragraph.** This article follows the Middle Way (Prāsaṅgika-Madhyamaka) of Nāgārjuna and
Candrakīrti, as read by Tsongkhapa. Everything, mind included, is empty of its own nature (*svabhāva*). Emptiness is
a non-implicative negation: it removes a false assumption and installs nothing in its place. Appearances are not
denied. They are vivid and they work, and they are found nowhere standing on their own. The article applies this view
to one test case: the six higher knowledges of the early Buddhist texts.

## 1. A monk in deep meditation

Early Buddhist texts describe a meditator whose mind has become still, bright and steady, and who turns that mind
toward remarkable things. He knows what is in the mind of the person across from him. He hears sounds from far away,
human and heavenly. He remembers lives before this one. He sees beings dying and being reborn according to what they
have done. He passes through a wall as if it were open space.

Later tradition gathers these under one name: the **higher knowledges** (Sanskrit *abhijñā*, Pāli *abhiññā*; also
translated "direct knowledges"). There are six:

1. **Psychic powers** (*iddhi*): becoming many and becoming one, appearing and vanishing, passing through walls,
   walking on water, flying.
2. **The divine ear** (*dibba-sota*): hearing sounds human and heavenly, near or far.
3. **Knowing the minds of others** (*ceto-pariya-ñāṇa*).
4. **Recollecting past lives** (*pubbe-nivāsānussati*).
5. **The divine eye** (*dibba-cakkhu*): seeing beings pass away and be reborn according to their deeds.
6. **The ending of the defilements** (*āsava-kkhaya*): the end of the habits of craving and confusion that bind a
   mind. This is awakening itself.

The first five are abilities that come with deep concentration. The sixth is of a different kind.

*Precision note: the Pāli list is the early one; do not attribute the list of six to a single later school or say the
Buddha "taught six superpowers". Do not call the sixth a "power".*

## 2. Two reactions, one mistake

Modern listeners meet these claims in one of two ways. Some dismiss them at once: matter does not behave like that,
so they must be impossible. Others embrace them as proof: of a soul that travels, a mind that stands outside the body,
or a higher reality that the meditator finally sees as it truly is.

The two reactions look like opposites, but they share one mistake. Each assumes that something exists all by itself
and is the final measure of what can happen. For the first, it is matter. For the second, it is mind, or soul.

The Middle Way of Nāgārjuna refuses that assumption everywhere. It exempts nothing from the search. That is what
"non-substantialist" means here, and the higher knowledges are a perfect test of it, because they press on every
part of the view at once: what a mind is, what knowing another means, space and distance, memory and time, rebirth,
the many worlds of beings, and what freedom is.

*Precision note: the article is neither for nor against the higher knowledges. It says they cannot be ruled out or
proven by appeal to a substance on either side.*

## 3. Own nature and substance

The word the Middle Way takes apart is *svabhāva*, "own nature" or "intrinsic nature". A thing would have own nature
if it were what it is entirely from its own side: not made, not dependent on anything else, and not changing in its
essence. Nāgārjuna states this in the *Fundamental Verses on the Middle Way* (*Mūlamadhyamakakārikā*) 15.2: an
intrinsic nature would be "not made, and not dependent on another".

Anything taken to have such a nature is a **substance** in this article's sense. It need not be material stuff. It can
be:

- **matter**: atoms, particles, fields or energy as the one thing that is really real;
- **mind**: a consciousness, witness or awareness as the one thing that is really real;
- **a self or soul** that stays the same beneath every experience, and, on some views, across lives;
- **a space or container**: an empty box that would exist with nothing in it;
- **a ground**: a hidden bedrock, source or field from which everything comes.

They look very different but play the same role. Each is a candidate for the floor that everything else stands on,
while it stands on nothing.

The habit of positing such a floor is not a philosopher's invention. It is how ordinary experience organizes itself:
the word "cup" seems to name a thing that was there before we looked; the feeling "I" seems to point to someone who
has it. Buddhist texts call this habit grasping or fixing. Nothing here asks anyone to stop saying "cup" or "I". The
only question is whether either word names something there on its own.

## 4. The search, and nothing exempt

The Middle Way does not refute substance by announcing that there is none. It looks. If something exists by its own
nature, a careful search should find it, standing apart from its parts, its causes and its name.

**The chariot.** In *The Questions of King Milinda* (*Milindapañha*), the monk Nāgasena asks King Milinda which part
is the chariot: the pole, the axle, the wheels? None of them. All the parts in a heap? Not that either. Something apart
from the parts? Nothing is found there. And yet the king came by chariot. "Chariot" is a name that works perfectly in
dependence on the parts. In the same way, Nāgasena says, his own name is used in dependence on body and mind, and no
person is found in addition to them.

Candrakīrti (*Entering the Middle Way*, *Madhyamakāvatāra*, chapter 6) sharpens this into a sevenfold search: the
chariot is not the same as its parts, not different from them, does not possess them, is not in them, does not have
them in it, is not their mere collection and is not their shape. In none of these ways is it found. Yet it is not
nothing: it is designated, and as designated it carries people.

**Nothing exempt.** The search is only as strong as its willingness to apply itself everywhere. The usual escape is to
exempt one favourite thing. The materialist searches the self, finds nothing, and keeps matter. The idealist searches
matter, finds nothing standing by itself, and keeps mind. The spiritual seeker searches the ordinary self and keeps a
pure awareness behind it as the true self. Each stops one step early. The Middle Way does not stop: matter, mind,
self, space, awareness, each meets the same search, and none is found standing on its own.

*Precision note: never present the view as "only mind is real", "everything is consciousness" or "everything is
energy/relations". The search does not stop at any of them.*

## 5. Empty is not unreal

If nothing is found under analysis, what are cups, people and chariots? The Middle Way answers with **two truths**.
Conventional truth is how things appear and work: the cup holds tea, the chariot carries the king, a person keeps a
promise; within it there are real distinctions between accurate and mistaken. Ultimate truth is what is found when we
search for how things exist in themselves: nothing standing by itself. This is emptiness (*śūnyatā*).

The two truths are not two levels or two worlds. They are two aspects of one appearance. The cup that holds tea is the
cup that is empty of a nature of its own.

Emptiness is not unreality. Fire still burns, a lie still harms, a kind act still helps. What is denied is only that
anything works by a power it owns from its own side. A thing with its nature fixed from its own side could not
respond, combine or change. Nāgārjuna's line in *Fundamental Verses* 24.14, "For whom emptiness works, everything
works", is his reply to critics who said emptiness would make the path and its results impossible: it is their
fixed natures, not emptiness, that would freeze everything.

*Precision note: emptiness is never a cause, a power or a condition of possibility. Never say "because things are
empty, they can arise", "emptiness enables things to happen" or "emptiness is what makes things work". Say instead
that something with a fixed nature could never change, and that nothing has one.*

## 6. Put nothing in its place

Indian and Tibetan logic distinguish two kinds of negation. An **implicative negation** denies one thing and implies
another ("he is not a monk" suggests he is a layperson). A **non-implicative negation** (*prasajya-pratiṣedha*) simply
denies, and implies nothing in its place. Tsongkhapa reads emptiness as the second kind.

This is the most important rule for everything that follows. When a substance is removed, put nothing in its place.

- No material bedrock does not mean that mind is the bedrock.
- No travelling soul does not mean that a subtler consciousness travels instead.
- No ordinary view from nowhere does not mean that the meditator's higher vision is the view from nowhere.

Each time a substance is cleared away, the temptation is to install a new one in the empty space. That is how
substance comes back, under a new name. Not even emptiness can be the new ground. Nāgārjuna (*Fundamental Verses*
13.8) says the victorious ones taught emptiness as the relinquishing of all views, and that those for whom emptiness
becomes a view are incurable. Emptiness, too, is empty.

*Precision note: never describe emptiness as a "space", "field", "openness", "source" or "radiant reality" from which
things come, and never say the meditator finally "sees emptiness itself" as if it were an object.*

## 7. Three questions for any claim

What is left when own nature is removed is dependent arising (*pratītyasamutpāda*): things arise in dependence on
causes and conditions, on their parts, and on how they are conceived and named. Nāgārjuna joins the two
(*Fundamental Verses* 24.18): whatever arises dependently is emptiness; that is a dependent designation; that is the
middle way. A thing and its designation arise together: there is no unlabeled thing beneath the label, and no labeler
standing outside the labeling. Dependent arising is regular (rice from rice seed; kind acts tend toward happiness)
without needing a hidden power that makes it hold.

For every claim about the higher knowledges, the article asks three questions:

1. Does the claim, as usually understood, need a substance?
2. Can it be understood without one?
3. Does the logic prove that the claim is true?

The answer to the third is always no. The Middle Way can show that an objection rests on a substance, and so the
objection falls. But **removing a bad argument against something is not the same as supplying a good argument for
it.** So the article keeps three things apart throughout: what the logic shows, what the tradition affirms, and what
only evidence can decide.

## 8. The knower is not a substance

Mind is the most tempting candidate for the last substance. When everything outside has been shown to depend on
perception, perception itself seems simply given. The Middle Way does not accept this. Candrakīrti, in his critique of
Mind-Only, argues that if the object is not found standing by itself, the mind that knows it is not found standing by
itself either. Knower and known arise together, as long and short do. Consciousness is always of something, through
something: not a standing light, a container or a witness behind experience.

The early texts already say this. In the *Longer Discourse on the Ending of Craving* (*Mahātaṇhāsaṅkhaya Sutta*,
Majjhima Nikāya 38), a monk named Sāti claims that "it is this very same consciousness that roams and transmigrates,
not another." The Buddha rebukes him sharply: he has said "in many ways" that "consciousness is dependently
originated," and that "without a cause, consciousness does not come to be." Consciousness is named after the
condition it arises from, as a fire burning logs is called a log fire and a fire burning grass a grass fire.

*Precision note: do not replace the self with a "stream of consciousness", "subtle mind" or "pure awareness" that
continues on its own. The point of the fire simile is that consciousness has no existence apart from its conditions.*

## 9. Worlds are made by channels of contact

A **world** here is the whole field of appearances that opens when a kind of being meets its surroundings through
particular senses, habits and channels of contact. Buddhist texts call it a *loka*; the biologist Jakob von Uexküll
called it an *Umwelt*.

Uexküll's tick responds to three signals: the smell of butyric acid in mammal sweat, the warmth of a body, the touch
of hair. Those signals are almost its whole world. A bat lives in a world of returning echoes. A honeybee lives among
ultraviolet patterns on flowers that humans cannot see. A human lives among colours, words, mid-sized objects and
clocks.

These worlds are not separated by walls or distance. They differ because each being is coupled to its surroundings
through different channels. Every world has three sides, the being, the environment as it is met, and the channels
joining them, and all three are designated together; none comes first. Worlds overlap by degrees: a human and a dog
share a great deal, a human and a tick very little. And no world is the master copy of the others: the human world is
reliable within its channels and has every practical priority for us, but not ultimate priority. That is what
"multi-dimensional" means here: not the extra dimensions of physics, but many worlds, none of them the foundation of
the rest.

*Precision note: never picture the worlds as stacked in one shared space, layered on a common ground, or as "filters"
over one real world.*

## 10. No view from nowhere, and wider is not from nowhere

One thing never crosses between worlds: a **view from nowhere**. No being can step outside every world and compare
them all from a neutral height, because any comparison is made from somewhere, through some channel. A human can learn
a great deal about a bat, but always as a human, building a human picture of the bat. A view from nowhere would be a
knowing that depended on no position, no channel and no knower: a knowing with an intrinsic nature, exactly what the
search fails to find.

Now notice what this does **not** rule out. It does not rule out knowing more, knowing further, or knowing along
channels that ordinary humans lack. A telescope already does that. Science widens the human world enormously through
spectrometers, microscopes and detectors, without lifting anyone out of it.

**A view can grow wider without limit and still be a view from somewhere. Wider is not the same as from nowhere.**
This one distinction does most of the work in the rest of the article. Every higher knowledge the texts describe is a
widening. None, read carefully, is described as a view from nowhere. And the sixth is not a wider view at all.

*Precision note: this is the hinge of the whole video. Never say the argument shows the higher knowledges are
impossible, and never say the meditator gains a "God's-eye view" or sees "reality as it is from outside every
perspective".*

## 11. Distance without a container, continuity without a carrier

**Distance.** We picture space as an empty box that would exist with nothing in it. Nāgārjuna (*Fundamental Verses*,
chapter 5) shows that space cannot exist before its defining mark (the absence of obstruction): space and what is in
it are designated together. So near and far are relations within a world, through its channels, not fixed
measurements inside a box. A telephone makes a voice on another continent near in one respect and far in another.
Other worlds are "not elsewhere"; but that does not mean they are "in the same place", because there is no neutral
place for them to share.

**Continuity.** Even within one life, nothing carries yesterday across the night. A lamp lit from another lamp is
neither the same flame nor a different one (*Questions of King Milinda*): nothing passes over, yet the second flame is
not unconnected with the first. Nāgārjuna (*Fundamental Verses* 21.18–21) finds no link at the joint between lives:
if the last moment had ceased, nothing would connect; if not, two lives would run at once; if one began exactly as the
other ended, they would still be two. Continuity is not denied as a convention; it is not found as a thing that flows.

**Karma and rebirth.** In chapter 17 of the *Fundamental Verses*, a karma with its own nature could never have been
made, never change and never end; agent, action and result are empty, and karma is fully working without a ledger or
store. The tradition affirms rebirth, but not as a journey: what is denied is the traveller, and also its shadow, the
self that would be wiped out at death. Emptiness removes one objection to rebirth, that it needs a carrier, since
nothing ever has one. It does not show that rebirth happens. Rebirth is affirmed by the tradition and held
conventionally; whether a given report of a past life is accurate is a matter of evidence.

*Precision note: other worlds are "not elsewhere", never "in the same location" or "right here in the same space".
Never say a soul, subtle body or stream of consciousness travels from life to life. Never say emptiness proves
rebirth.*

## 12. What the early texts describe

The fullest early account is the *Discourse on the Fruits of the Ascetic Life* (*Sāmaññaphala Sutta*, Dīgha Nikāya
2). King Ajātasattu of Magadha asks the Buddha what visible fruit the life of a renunciate brings here and now. The
Buddha answers step by step: first simple fruits (even a bondservant or a farmer who goes forth is honoured by the
king), then ethics, restraint of the senses, contentment, and the four absorptions (*jhāna*) of deep meditation.

Then he describes a mind "immersed in samādhi" (deep concentration), always in the same words: "purified, bright,
flawless, rid of corruptions, pliable, workable, steady, and imperturbable". Such a mind, the sutta says, can be
"projected and extended" toward a series of knowledges. It lists eight: insight into body and consciousness, a
mind-made body, and the six higher knowledges. Each comes with a simple image.

The Buddha's own awakening, in the *Discourse on Fear and Dread* (*Bhayabherava Sutta*, Majjhima Nikāya 4), follows the
same pattern: in the first watch of the night he recollects past lives; in the middle watch he sees beings pass away
and be reborn; in the last watch he realizes the ending of the defilements. Only with the third is his mind freed.

**Five worldly, one liberating.** The first five are worldly (*lokiya*). The early texts credit them even to people
outside the Buddha's teaching. In the *Brahmajāla Sutta* (*The Divine Net*, Dīgha Nikāya 1), ascetics who, through
effort and concentration, recollect many past lives conclude that "the self and the cosmos are eternal … steady as a
mountain peak, standing firm like a pillar." Recollecting past lives does not make anyone wise; it can even support a
wrong view. The sixth is supramundane (*lokuttara*). It is not one more ability.

*Quotations from the early discourses follow Bhikkhu Sujato's translations (SuttaCentral).*

## 13. The first knowledge: psychic powers

**The text.** "Multiplying themselves and becoming one again; materializing and dematerializing; going unobstructed
through a wall, a rampart, or a mountain as if through space; diving in and out of the earth as if it were water;
walking on water as if it were earth; flying cross-legged through the sky like a bird; touching the sun and moon with
the hand; controlling the body as far as the realm of divinity." The image: a skilled potter with well-prepared clay,
who can make any kind of pot they like. Just before it, DN 2 describes creating a mind-made body, drawn out of this
body as a reed is drawn from its sheath.

**The substantialist reading.** A wall solid by nature, a body solid by nature, and the meditator forcing one through
the other, overriding a bedrock. On this picture psychic power must be either impossible or a miracle that breaks the
rules of reality. And the mind-made body looks like the soul stepping out of its shell.

**Without a substance.** Solidity is not something a wall owns by itself. It is a resistance that appears when two
things sharing the relevant kind of coupling meet. Dark matter, as far as observations show, passes through the Earth
without collision, yet the Earth's gravity still bends its path. A neutrino crosses vast thicknesses of lead with
almost no chance of striking an atom. In the traditional accounts, spirits with subtle bodies pass through mountains.
Read this way, passing through a wall "as if through space" would not override a solidity that exists by itself,
because there is none to override. It would be a change in the meeting. The wall stays a wall for everyone else.

The sutta's own image points the same way: the clay is "well-prepared", the mind is "pliable, workable". The power is
described as workability, a skill with a material that responds, not force against a nature that resists. "One" and
"many" are designated together, so becoming many is not one substance dividing. And the mind-made body is *created*
from a concentrated mind, a body designated in dependence on that concentration; the sutta never says it is what the
person really is.

*Precision note: this is not an explanation and gives no mechanism. Dark matter and neutrinos illustrate a pattern;
they are not evidence that a human body can pass through walls. Never say "quantum physics explains psychic powers".
Never call the mind-made body the soul or the true self.*

## 14. The second knowledge: the divine ear

**The text.** "With clairaudience that is purified and superhuman, they hear both kinds of sounds, human and heavenly,
whether near or far." The image: a person travelling along a road who hears drums, horns and kettledrums, and knows
each for what it is.

**The substantialist reading.** An extremely long ear, reaching across a fixed box of space to sound sources located
far away in it; "heavenly" sounds coming from a higher floor of the same building.

**Without a substance.** Distance is designated within a world, not laid down in a container. The divine ear would be
a change in the channel of hearing, so that what is far by one measure becomes near by another. Heavenly sounds would
be sounds of another world, met through a channel ordinary hearing does not share, not sounds from a higher region of
one shared space. And the sutta's own image places the hearer on a road, recognizing what is heard. Even a superhuman
hearing, as described, is still a hearing from somewhere.

## 15. The third knowledge: knowing the minds of others

**The text.** "They understand the minds of other beings and individuals, having encompassed them with their own
mind." What is known: a mind with greed as "mind with greed" and without greed as "without greed"; likewise for hate
and delusion; constricted or scattered; expansive or not; supreme or not; immersed in concentration or not; freed or
unfreed. The image: a young person checking their reflection in a clean bright mirror or a clear bowl of water, and
knowing whether or not there is a spot on their face. Elsewhere (*Kevaddha Sutta*, DN 11; *Saṅgārava Sutta*, Aṅguttara
Nikāya 3.60) the "demonstration of revealing" includes revealing another's thoughts.

**The substantialist reading.** Each mind is a sealed private room only its owner can enter; telepathy is a break-in,
one private substance gaining access to another, or merging with it. Behind this lies the assumption that my own mind
is simply given, while yours is hidden. Dharmakīrti argued that we infer other minds from purposeful action;
Ratnakīrti, from the Mind-Only side, that other minds can never finally be proven. Both treat one's own mind as the
unquestioned starting point.

**Without a substance.** Under analysis, my own mind is no more findable as an independent thing than yours. Other
minds are designated together with oneself, as long with short. There are no sealed rooms on either side. Ordinary
empathy is already a real channel between minds: we read a face, hear a voice, and know a friend is afraid. Knowing
others' minds would be a finer channel of the same kind, opened by concentration. Three details of the sutta fit: it
is done "with their own mind" (the knower does not merge into the other or step outside both); what is known are
conditioned states, not a hidden essence; and the image is a mirror, which shows a face accurately without holding it.

Two minds remain two by convention, as two lamps remain two even when one lights the other.

*Precision note: never say telepathy shows "all minds are one", a "universal consciousness" or a "field of mind". A
single cosmic mind would be a new substance.*

## 16. The fourth knowledge: recollecting past lives

**The text.** "They recollect many kinds of past lives: one, two, three … a hundred thousand rebirths; many eons of
the world contracting and expanding." With details: "There, I was named this, my clan was that, I looked like this,
and that was my food. This was how I felt pleasure and pain, and that was how my life came to an end." The image: a
person who goes from their home village to another, then a third, and returns, remembering where they went and what
they did.

**The substantialist reading.** This knowledge seems to demand a substance more than any other: something present
then and present now, carrying the memories across.

The early texts know this reading as the root of a wrong view: the ascetics of DN 1 conclude from real recollection
that the self is eternal, "firm like a pillar"; Sāti in MN 38 makes consciousness the traveller. The tradition rejects
both conclusions while keeping the recollection.

**Without a substance.** When I remember yesterday, nothing has carried yesterday across the night. There is no box
where memories wait between rememberings, as there is no place where a melody known by heart waits between
performances. Recollecting a past life would be the same kind of relation over a longer stretch, with no traveller
passing between. "I was named this" is how the earlier life is designated from within the present one, in the
ordinary language of a self. The village image is about the reliability of the memory, not a doctrine that one
unchanging person walked the road. The experience can stand; turning it into proof of a self is the mistake.

*Precision note: never say past-life memory "proves the soul" or "proves rebirth". Never describe memories stored in a
subtle consciousness that survives death.*

## 17. The fifth knowledge: the divine eye

**The text.** "With clairvoyance that is purified and superhuman, they see sentient beings passing away and being
reborn: inferior and superior, beautiful and ugly, in a good place or a bad place. They understand how sentient beings
pass on according to their deeds." The image: a person with clear eyes standing in a raised longhouse at the central
square, watching people enter and leave a house, walk along the streets and sit down in the square.

**The substantialist reading.** This seems to be the view from nowhere itself: an overview of all beings in all realms,
from above, as they really are. If there is no view from nowhere, the divine eye seems ruled out.

**Without a substance.** Look at the sutta's own image. The seer stands in a raised house at the central square. That
is a place: a very good place to see from, a height, but a place. The image is of an eye that looks from a height, not
from nowhere. Wider, not from nowhere. What it sees, "inferior and superior, beautiful and ugly", are designations
made from a point of view. And what it sees is a process of dependence: beings pass on according to their deeds. It
does not see a soul travelling from body to body; it sees conditions in one life followed by results in another.

Seeing beings in other realms would be contact through a channel opened by training, with those beings appearing as
seen by the meditator, within the meditator's field. Nothing in the text says the meditator sees a hungry ghost's
world as the hungry ghost does.

*Precision note: never call the divine eye a "God's-eye view" or "seeing reality from outside all perspectives".*

## 18. The sixth knowledge: the ending of the defilements

**The text.** With the mind immersed in samādhi in the same words, the meditator "truly understands: 'These are
defilements … This is the origin of defilements … This is the cessation of defilements … This is the practice that
leads to the cessation of defilements.'" And "knowing and seeing like this", the mind is freed from the defilements
of sensuality, desire to be reborn, and ignorance. The image: a person with clear eyes on the bank of a transparent
mountain lake, seeing the clams and mussels, the pebbles and gravel, and the fish swimming about or staying still.

**The substantialist reading.** The greatest power of all: the final channel, the one that at last sees all the way
down to the bedrock.

**Without a substance.** The text does not say this. The sixth knowledge does not look at a hidden object. It knows the
very process by which the mind grasps and fixes, and it knows that process ending. Its result is not that the
meditator acquires something but that something stops. Nothing is added to the lake; what was always there is seen.

Freedom, on this view, is not produced by causes, and it is not an uncaused ground uncovered at the bottom of things.
It is the recognition that what the mind kept fixing on was never there to be fixed. Nirvāṇa is not another world
reached by a further channel; it is this same appearing with the fixing ended.

So the five worldly knowledges widen the field of what appears, and a mind with them can still cling to what it sees,
and to itself as the one who sees. The sixth does not widen the field. It ends the clinging, whatever the field.

*Precision note: never call the sixth knowledge "seeing emptiness", "seeing the ground", "union with pure awareness"
or "the highest power". Never say freedom is "produced" by the meditation, or that it uncovers a hidden luminous mind.
Do not end the section on "what remains".*

## 19. The monk who went to the gods

The *Kevaddha Sutta* (Dīgha Nikāya 11) ends with a story that gathers everything together.

A monk wonders where earth, water, fire and air finally cease without remainder. In deep concentration, a path to the
gods appears. He asks the gods of the four great kings; they do not know and send him to their superiors. He asks the
gods of the thirty-three, and Sakka their lord, and the gods of the higher heavens, realm after realm, each sending him
on. At last he reaches the Great Brahmā, who answers only by repeating his titles: "I am the Divinity, the Great
Divinity, the Vanquisher, the Unvanquished, the Universal Seer … the Maker, the Creator." Asked a third time, Brahmā
takes the monk aside: these gods think there is nothing I do not know, which is why I did not answer in front of them;
but I too do not know. Go and ask the Buddha.

The monk returns. The Buddha compares him to a land-spotting bird released from a ship at sea: it flies in every
direction, and finding no land, comes back to the ship. Then the Buddha says the question was asked the wrong way. Not
"where do these four elements cease?", but:

> Where do water and earth, fire and air have no footing; where long and short, tiny and huge, beautiful and ugly?
> Where do name and form cease with no residue left behind?

Three lessons: psychic power took the monk through every heaven and did not find a bedrock; the highest god, called the
Universal Seer, was a being in a world, seeing from somewhere; and the answer is not a location at the top of the
cosmos. The Buddha turns a question about a place into a question about where all the pairs we lay down together find
no footing.

The verse's answer begins "Consciousness where no form appears, infinite, radiant all-round." Some readers take this as
a luminous consciousness that exists by itself and underlies everything. The article does not: such a consciousness
would be one more substance. The verse itself ends, "with the cessation of consciousness, here they cease." Read
without a ground, the radiance names the clarity of a mind that no longer fixes, not a hidden light behind appearances.

*Precision note: never present "consciousness … radiant all-round" as a cosmic consciousness, a luminous ground or the
true self. If the verse is quoted, include its last line about the cessation of consciousness.*

## 20. How the tradition treats them

**Not for display.** In DN 11 the householder Kevaḍḍha asks the Buddha to have a monk perform a miracle so that the
prosperous town of Nāḷandā will become more devoted. The Buddha refuses: "I do not teach Dhamma to the mendicants like
this." There are three kinds of demonstration: of psychic power, of revealing (another's thoughts), and of
instruction. A sceptic would explain the first two away: "There's a spell named Gandhārī", "a spell named Māṇikā".
"Seeing this drawback," the Buddha says, "I'm horrified, repelled, and disgusted by demonstrations of psychic power,"
and likewise by demonstrations of revealing. The demonstration he offers is instruction: "Think like this, not like
that. Focus your mind like this, not like that. Give up this, and live having achieved that."

**Experienced only by the one who performs it.** In AN 3.60 the brahmin Saṅgārava judges that a demonstration of
psychic power "is experienced only by the one who performs it, occurring only to them. This seems to me like an
illusion", and the same of revealing another's mind; instruction, he says, is the finest of the three. Anyone who
follows instruction can test it.

**A rule of restraint.** Pācittiya rule 8 of the monastic code: a monk who truthfully tells someone not fully ordained
of a superhuman quality commits an offence entailing confession, even though the claim is true. The origin story: in a
famine, monks praised one another's attainments to householders to be fed, and the Buddha rebuked them for doing it
"for the sake of your stomachs."

**Grounded in ordinary virtue.** In the *Discourse on One Might Wish* (*Ākaṅkheyya Sutta*, Majjhima Nikāya 6), for
every wish, including each of the higher knowledges, the instruction is the same: "let them fulfill their precepts, be
committed to inner serenity of the heart, not neglect absorption, be endowed with discernment." No secret technique.

This caution is what the view predicts. If the five worldly knowledges are widenings of a view from somewhere, they
cannot settle anything for anyone else. The sixth cannot be displayed, because there is nothing to display.

## 21. Three kinds of claim

| Knowledge | What the logic shows | What the tradition affirms | What only evidence can decide |
|---|---|---|---|
| Psychic powers | Solidity is a relation; no intrinsic solidity is overridden | Trained meditators have them; they are not to be displayed | Whether a given report is accurate |
| Divine ear | Distance is designated within a world; no container needed | The concentrated mind hears sounds human and heavenly | Whether a person hears what they report |
| Others' minds | No sealed rooms; no break-in or merger needed | The mind knows others' states "with its own mind" | Whether a given claim is correct |
| Past lives | Memory needs no carrier; no traveller implied | Past lives can be recollected; this does not prove an eternal self | Whether a recollection fits a real earlier life |
| Divine eye | A wide view is still from somewhere | Beings are seen passing on according to their deeds | Whether a vision is accurate |
| Ending of defilements | Not a channel or ground; the ending of fixing | The goal; the one liberating knowledge | Known by the one freed ("when they're freed, they know they're freed") and shown in how they live |

The first column never proves the second. The second is held conventionally, as everything is held. Neither settles
the third.

## 22. Two opposite mistakes, and the middle

**Dismissal from a substance.** Dismissing the higher knowledges because they "break the laws of matter" sounds
hard-headed, but quietly makes matter the bedrock, the one thing exempt from the search. Physics, as practised, makes
no such claim: it gives some of the most reliable descriptions we have, within the conditions it studies. Doubting a
particular report, asking for evidence, noticing how easily people deceive themselves: all sound. Thinking the
question is settled in advance by the nature of matter is not.

**Credulity from a substance.** Taking the knowledges as proof of a soul that travels, a mind independent of
everything, or a seer beyond every perspective is the implicative reading again. The early texts warn against this
mistake more often: the ascetics who declared the self eternal (DN 1), the monk who made consciousness the traveller
(MN 38), the god called the Universal Seer who did not know (DN 11).

**The middle.** Not a compromise. The question cannot be settled by a substance on either side. The tradition affirms
the higher knowledges, held conventionally, as working and empty. Whether a particular report is true is a matter of
evidence. The logic removes an objection; it proves nothing.

## 23. What this view does not claim

1. It does not prove that the higher knowledges exist.
2. Physics does not confirm them; dark matter, neutrinos, telephones and telescopes are illustrations only.
3. It offers no mechanism; the tradition's own account is the training: ethics, calm, concentration, insight.
4. It makes no knower exempt from emptiness: even if the knowledges occur, they reveal no hidden bedrock and establish
   no self-existing awareness or radiant ground.
5. It is one reading within Buddhism (the Middle Way of Nāgārjuna and Candrakīrti as read by Tsongkhapa, applied to the
   early discourses); Abhidharma and Mahāyāna texts treat the knowledges in their own ways.
6. It does not tell anyone to seek them; the texts almost unanimously say the five worldly knowledges are not the goal.

## 24. Practice, and knowing from somewhere

Meditators sometimes have unusual experiences: sensing what someone feels, vivid images, vastness, a memory that seems
to come from beyond this life. The experience can be real as an experience, neither mistaken nor fabricated merely
because it is unusual. The mistake is to build a self on it, as the ascetics of DN 1 did. That applies most of all to
experiences of vastness, clarity or pure awareness, the most tempting to turn into a new self.

The finest demonstration is instruction, which anyone can test, and which leads to the sixth knowledge. And the
ordinary channel of empathy is not a weak copy of telepathy: it is a real knowing of another, available now, and where
compassion starts.

Every knowing, ordinary or extraordinary, is a knowing from somewhere: by someone, of something, through a channel,
dependently arisen. The higher knowledges, as the early texts describe them, would widen that somewhere, perhaps
enormously. They would not abolish it. What the tradition values most is not reaching further. It is no longer
grasping what is reached, or the one who reaches it.

*Precision note: end on this line, not on "what remains", "pure awareness", "the ground of being" or emptiness as a
final reality.*
