# Knowing From Somewhere

A monk sits in deep meditation. His mind becomes still, bright, and steady. And then, the old texts say, it can be turned toward remarkable things.

He knows what is in the mind of the person sitting across from him. He hears sounds from far away, human and heavenly. He remembers lives before this one. He sees beings dying and being reborn, each according to what they have done. He passes through a wall as if it were open air.

Buddhist tradition calls these the higher knowledges. In Sanskrit, abhijñā. There are six. Five are powers that come with deep concentration. The sixth is different. It is the ending of the defilements, the habits of craving and confusion that bind a mind. It is awakening itself.

Modern listeners tend to meet these claims in one of two ways. Some dismiss them at once. Matter does not behave like that, so they must be impossible. Others embrace them as proof. Proof of a soul that travels, or a mind that stands outside the body, or a higher reality that the meditator finally sees as it truly is.

These two reactions look like opposites. But they share one mistake. Each assumes that something exists all by itself, and is the final measure of what can happen. For one it is matter. For the other it is mind, or soul.

The Middle Way of Nāgārjuna refuses that assumption everywhere. It exempts nothing from the search. And the higher knowledges turn out to be a perfect test of what that means.

Start with the word that the Middle Way takes apart. In Sanskrit it is svabhāva, own nature. A thing would have own nature if it were what it is entirely from its own side. Not made. Not dependent on anything else. Not changing in its essence.

Anything taken to have such a nature can be called a substance. It need not be a material stuff. Matter can be taken as the one thing that is really real. So can mind, or awareness. So can a self that stays the same beneath every experience. So can a space, an empty box that would exist with nothing in it. So can a hidden ground from which everything comes.

They look very different. But they all play the same role. Each is a candidate for the floor that everything else stands on, while it stands on nothing.

The Middle Way does not refute substance by announcing that there is none. It looks. If a thing exists by its own nature, then a careful search should find it, standing apart from its parts, its causes, and its name.

The classic example is a chariot. The monk Nāgasena asks a king which part is the chariot. The pole? The axle? The wheels? None of them. All the parts in a heap? Not that either. Something apart from the parts? Nothing is found there. And yet the king came by chariot. The name works perfectly, in dependence on the parts. No chariot is found in addition to them.

The search is only as strong as its willingness to apply itself everywhere. And the usual way to escape it is to exempt one favourite thing. The materialist searches the self, finds nothing, and keeps matter. The idealist searches matter, finds nothing standing by itself, and keeps mind. The spiritual seeker searches the ordinary self, and keeps a pure awareness behind it as the true self.

Each of these stops one step early. The Middle Way does not stop. Matter, mind, self, space, awareness. Each meets the same search, and none is found standing on its own.

That does not make things unreal. The cup still holds tea. Fire still burns. A kind act still helps. What is denied is only that anything works by a power it owns from its own side. And a thing with a nature fixed from its own side could never respond, or combine, or change.

There is one rule that matters more than any other here. When a substance is removed, put nothing in its place.

Indian logic has a name for this. It is a negation that simply denies, and implies nothing in its place. No material bedrock does not mean that mind is the bedrock. No travelling soul does not mean that a subtler consciousness travels instead. And no ordinary view from nowhere does not mean that the meditator's higher vision is the view from nowhere.

Every time a substance is cleared away, the temptation is to install a new one in the empty space. That is how the substance comes back, under a new name. Even emptiness cannot be the new ground. Emptiness, too, is empty.

So for every claim about the higher knowledges, there are three questions. Does the claim, as usually understood, need a substance? Can it be understood without one? And does the logic prove that the claim is true?

The answer to the last question is always no. The Middle Way can show that an objection rests on a substance, and so the objection falls. But removing a bad argument against something is not the same as supplying a good argument for it.

Now consider knowing itself.

The mind is the most tempting candidate for the last substance. When everything outside has been shown to depend on perception, perception itself seems simply given. The Middle Way does not accept this. If the object is not found standing by itself, the mind that knows it is not found standing by itself either. Knower and known arise together, as long and short do.

The early texts already say this. A monk named Sāti once claimed that the very same consciousness wanders from life to life. The Buddha rebuked him sharply. Consciousness, he said, is dependently arisen. It is named after its conditions, the way a fire burning logs is called a log fire, and a fire burning grass is called a grass fire.

Then consider worlds. A tick lives in a world of three signals: the smell of sweat, the warmth of a body, the touch of hair. A bat lives in a world of returning echoes. A bee lives among ultraviolet patterns on flowers that we cannot see. A human lives among colours, words, and clocks.

These worlds are not separated by walls or by distance. They differ because each being is joined to its surroundings through different channels of contact. And none of these worlds is the master copy of the others.

One thing never crosses between worlds. A view from nowhere. No being can step outside every world and look at all of them from a neutral height. Any comparison is made from somewhere, through some channel. A human can learn a great deal about a bat, but always as a human.

But notice carefully what this does not rule out. It does not rule out knowing more, or knowing further, or knowing along channels that ordinary humans lack. A telescope already does that. Science widens the human world enormously, without lifting anyone out of it.

A view can grow wider without limit, and still be a view from somewhere. Wider is not the same as from nowhere. Hold on to that distinction. It does most of the work from here on.

There are two more pieces. Distance does not need a container. Near and far are relations within a world, not fixed measurements inside an empty box. And continuity does not need a carrier. Even within one life, nothing carries yesterday across the night. A lamp lit from another lamp is neither the same flame nor a different one. Nothing passes over. Yet the second flame is not unconnected with the first.

Now the six knowledges themselves.

The fullest early account comes in a discourse called The Fruits of the Ascetic Life. A king asks the Buddha what visible good the life of a renunciate brings. The Buddha answers step by step. Ethics. Restraint. The deep absorptions of meditation. Then a mind that is bright, flawless, pliable, workable, steady. Such a mind, the text says, can be turned toward each of the knowledges in turn. And for each, it gives a simple image.

The first is psychic power. Becoming many and becoming one. Appearing and vanishing. Going through a wall as if through space. Walking on water as if it were earth.

On the usual reading, this is a violation. There is a wall, solid by nature. There is a body, solid by nature. And somehow one is forced through the other.

But solidity is not something a wall owns by itself. It is a resistance that appears when two things that share the right kind of coupling meet. Dark matter, as far as we can tell, passes straight through the Earth, and yet the Earth's gravity still bends its path. In the traditional stories, spirits with subtle bodies pass through mountains.

Read this way, passing through a wall would not override a solidity that exists by itself, because there is none to override. It would be a change in the meeting. The wall stays a wall for everyone else.

The text's own image points the same way. It is a skilled potter with well-prepared clay, who can shape any pot they like. The power is described as workability, a skill with a material that responds. Not force against a nature that resists. This is no mechanism, and physics confirms nothing here. It shows only that the claim does not need a solid bedrock to be broken.

The second is the divine ear. Hearing sounds, human and heavenly, near or far. The image is of a traveller on a road, who hears drums and horns, and knows each for what it is.

On the usual reading, it is an extremely long ear, reaching across a fixed box of space. But near and far are relations within a world. A telephone already makes a voice on another continent near in one way and far in another. And notice the image. The hearer is on a road. The hearer is somewhere. Even a superhuman hearing, as described, is still a hearing from somewhere.

The third is knowing the minds of others. The text says the meditator understands other minds, having encompassed them with their own mind.

On the usual reading, each mind is a sealed private room, and telepathy is a break in. But under analysis, my own mind is no more findable as an independent thing than yours. There are no sealed rooms on either side. Ordinary empathy is already a real channel between minds. We read a face and hear a voice, and we know a friend is afraid.

Knowing others' minds would be a finer channel of the same kind, opened by concentration. And look at what the text says is known. A mind with greed, or without it. A mind with anger, or without it. Scattered or focused. Freed or not freed. These are conditioned states, not a hidden essence. And the knowing is done with one's own mind. The knower does not merge into the other. Two lamps remain two lamps, even when one lights the other.

The fourth is remembering past lives. One life, two, a hundred, a hundred thousand. There I had this name, this family, this food. This is how that life ended.

This one seems to demand a substance more than any other. If I remember being someone else, surely something was there then and is here now, carrying the memories across.

The early texts know this reading well, because it is the root of a wrong view. One discourse describes ascetics who really do recall past lives, and conclude from it that the self is eternal, steady as a mountain peak, standing firm like a pillar. The tradition rejects that conclusion, while keeping the memory.

When I remember yesterday, nothing has carried yesterday across the night. There is no box where memories wait between rememberings, as there is no place where a melody you know by heart waits between performances. Remembering a past life would be the same kind of link over a longer stretch, with no traveller passing between. The experience can stand. Turning it into proof of a self is the mistake.

The fifth is the divine eye. Seeing beings pass away and be reborn, each according to their deeds.

This seems at first like the view from nowhere itself. An overview of all beings, in all realms, from above. But look at the text's own image. A person with clear eyes stands in a raised house at the central square, watching people enter and leave, walk the streets, and sit down in the square.

That is a place. A very good place to see from. A height. But a place. The image is not of an eye that looks from nowhere. It is of an eye that looks from a height. Wider, not from nowhere.

And what it sees is a process of dependence. Beings pass on according to their deeds. It does not see a soul travelling from body to body. It sees conditions in one life followed by results in another.

The sixth knowledge is the ending of the defilements. The meditator truly understands the defilements, where they come from, their ending, and the way to their ending. And knowing this, the mind is freed. The image is a clear mountain lake. A person on the bank sees the shells, the pebbles, the fish moving or still.

On a substantialist reading, this would be the greatest power of all. The final channel that at last sees all the way down to the bedrock.

But that is not what the text says. The sixth knowledge does not look at a hidden object. It knows the very process by which the mind grasps and fixes, and it knows that process ending. Nothing is added to the lake. What was always there is simply seen.

Freedom, on this view, is not produced by causes, and it is not a hidden ground uncovered at the bottom of things. It is the recognition that what the mind kept fixing on was never there to be fixed.

So the five worldly knowledges widen the field of what appears. A mind with them can still cling to what it sees, and to itself as the one who sees. The sixth does not widen the field at all. It ends the clinging, whatever the field.

There is a story that gathers all of this together.

A monk wonders where earth, water, fire, and air finally cease, without remainder. In deep meditation, a path to the gods opens before him. He asks the gods of the first heaven. They do not know. They send him higher. He asks the next gods, and the next, realm after realm, each sending him on.

At last he reaches the great god Brahmā, who is called the all-seeing, the maker, the creator. Brahmā answers only by repeating his own titles. Asked a third time, he takes the monk aside. These gods believe I know everything, he says. That is why I did not answer in front of them. But I do not know either. Go and ask the Buddha.

The monk returns. The Buddha compares him to a bird released from a ship at sea. It flies in every direction looking for land, and finding none, it comes back to the ship.

Then the Buddha says the question was asked the wrong way. Not where do the elements cease, as if the answer were a place at the top of the universe. But where do they find no footing. Where do long and short, beautiful and ugly, name and form, all the pairs we lay down together, cease.

Psychic power took the monk through every heaven. It did not find a bedrock. Even the highest god, the one called all-seeing, was a being in a world, seeing from somewhere. And the answer was not a location at all.

How, then, does the tradition handle these powers? With striking caution.

A householder once asked the Buddha to have a monk perform a miracle, so that a prosperous town would become more devoted. The Buddha refused. A sceptic, he said, would simply explain it away. There is a spell that can do that. And so, he said, he was repelled by displays of psychic power and of mind reading.

The demonstration he offered instead was instruction. Think like this, not like that. Focus your mind like this, not like that. Give up this, and live having achieved that.

A brahmin who heard the same three kinds of demonstration put it this way. A display of power, he said, is experienced only by the one who performs it. To him it seemed like an illusion. Instruction, he said, was the finest of the three. Anyone who follows it can test it for themselves.

The monastic rules go further. A monk who tells someone not fully ordained about a superhuman quality commits an offence, even when what he says is true. And the path to these knowledges is no secret technique. It is the ordinary training. Keep your precepts. Cultivate inner calm. Do not neglect meditation. Develop wisdom.

This caution is exactly what the view would predict. If the five worldly knowledges are widenings of a view from somewhere, they cannot settle anything for anyone else. And the sixth cannot be displayed at all, because there is nothing to display.

So there are two opposite mistakes.

The first dismisses the higher knowledges because they seem to break the laws of matter. That sounds hard headed, but it quietly makes matter the bedrock, the one thing exempt from the search. Physics, as practised, makes no such claim. It gives some of the most reliable descriptions we have, within the conditions it studies. Doubting a particular report is sound. Thinking the question is settled in advance by the nature of matter is not.

The second mistake takes the knowledges as proof of a substance. A soul that travels. A mind that is independent of everything. A seer who has finally seen reality from beyond every perspective. The old texts warn against this one more often. The ascetics who remembered past lives and declared the self eternal. The monk who made consciousness the traveller. The god called all-seeing, who did not know.

The middle way between these is not a compromise. It says the question cannot be settled by a substance on either side. The tradition affirms the higher knowledges, and those who follow it hold them as everything is held, conventionally, as working and as empty. Whether a particular report is true is a matter of evidence. The logic removes an objection. It proves nothing.

And for practice, the lesson is simple. Unusual experiences in meditation can be real as experiences. The mistake is to build a self on them. That applies most of all to experiences of vastness, clarity, or pure awareness, which are the most tempting to turn into a new self.

Every knowing, ordinary or extraordinary, is a knowing from somewhere. By someone. Of something. Through a channel. The higher knowledges, as the early texts describe them, would widen that somewhere, perhaps enormously. They would not abolish it.

What the tradition values most is not reaching further. It is no longer grasping what is reached, or the one who reaches it.
