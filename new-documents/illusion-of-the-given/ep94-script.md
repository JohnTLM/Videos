# The Illusion of the Given

Picture a single bowl of liquid, set down where many kinds of beings can see it.

A human sees clear, refreshing water. A hungry ghost, a being driven by deep habits of craving, sees foul pus and blood. A fish finds the air it breathes and the home it swims in. A god tastes heavenly nectar. And an awakened being sees the pure wisdom of water, the female Buddha Māmakī.

It is one of the oldest examples in Indian and Tibetan Buddhist thought. And it asks a simple question. What is really in the bowl?

Common sense has a ready answer. Something must be sitting there first, some raw and neutral stuff. Each creature looks at it through its own lens. Two layers: the hidden thing, and the many views of it.

The Middle Way of Nāgārjuna, and the Great Perfection, Dzogchen, read without a foundation, both take this picture apart. Agreement does not need a hidden bedrock. The thing seen, the one seeing, and the way they meet are identified together, each depending on the others. And the relations between them are just as impossible to pin down as the things themselves.

Start with a word we slipped in without noticing. The liquid. To say that beings see the same liquid differently already assumes an object waiting there, with its own identity, before anyone meets it. This is the old idea that Kant made famous: a thing in itself, hidden behind the senses.

So ask what this hidden thing is like. If it has definite qualities, if it really is thirst-quenching water, it cannot explain why the ghost meets burning pus. If it has no qualities at all, a featureless blank cannot set off any experience. Either way, it does no work.

Someone will push back. Why not simply wetness, the quality that water and pus share? But wetness in general is an idea made by thought. In real life it only ever shows up as this drinkable water, this foul pus, this water a fish lives in. And if wetness is only an agreed point of comparison, it is no longer a hidden thing at all. It is a useful label.

What Nāgārjuna's analysis targets is called svabhāva, own nature. It means a nature a thing would have all by itself, depending on nothing. A neutral substance beneath appearances is that same imagined nature, in a modest disguise.

Then a real question remains. If there is no hidden object, why do humans agree so reliably that the liquid is water? Why doesn't the shared world fall apart into private fantasies?

The Indian master Candrakīrti answers plainly. Agreement does not prove a hidden object. Shared, reliable agreement is simply what everyday truth is.

He also refuses the opposite move. The Mind-Only school tried to solve the puzzle by removing outer objects and making consciousness the real foundation. But a seeing cannot be identified without what is seen. And an object cannot be identified without the seeing that picks it out. Neither mind nor matter comes first. Making the mind the container of everything is as mistaken as making matter the container.

Within everyday truth, mistakes are still caught. A person with jaundice sees a white conch shell as yellow, and people with healthy eyes correct them. But notice where that judgement is made. It is made within one kind of being. The hungry ghost is not a jaundiced human. By the standards of its own kind, its senses work normally. For the ghost, the liquid really does function as pus.

So what of the bowl itself? What is denied is a shared base that exists by itself, there before anyone perceives it. What is kept is a working reference point. It is a label, made from within one frame, for the place where different ways of appearing meet and can be compared. Its job is to coordinate, not to sit underneath.

The bowl example did not begin in the Middle Way. Vasubandhu, in his Twenty Verses, used the hungry ghosts to defend Mind-Only. Ghosts with the same karma all see a river of pus together, he said. Shared karma, ripening in consciousness, explains shared experience. No river outside the mind is needed.

That answers the question by making consciousness the floor. Candrakīrti turned the example around. If the river of pus is not established by itself, neither is the mind that sees it. The river of pus and the seeing of it stand or fall together. So the bowl does not show that the world is inside the mind. It shows that neither the world nor the mind is the floor.

Agreement also needs others to agree with. Indian logicians debated whether other minds could be proven. Both sides assumed that my own experience is certain, and yours only inferred. The Middle Way refuses that imbalance. Looked for closely, my stream of experience is no more findable than yours, and both work in daily life. Other observers are identified together with this one, as long is identified together with short.

A critic can press further. Senses count as healthy when they agree with others of their kind, and the kind is defined by sharing those senses. Isn't that circular? It is, and the circle is not a flaw. It would be fatal only if agreement had to rest on a final judge, outside every point of view. There is no such judge. Wittgenstein reached a similar point from another direction: we agree, not in opinions, but in a shared form of life. And inside the circle, correction is still real. A second look corrects a misreading.

For humans, much of this agreement is carried by language. A child learns the word water because it works. Say it, and a glass arrives. Yet language is also where the habit of making things solid is strongest. The noun, the bowl, seems to point at one solid thing behind every view of it.

Dzogchen describes the same freedom from the side of experience.

In the Dzogchen of Longchenpa and Mipham, the basis is primordially pure. From the very beginning, appearing was never fixed, so nothing ever needed cleaning. The basis is not a hidden block of stuff, a cosmic mind, or a starting point of the world. Longchenpa says it is called a ground only from a relative point of view. In the way things are, it is empty of being any ground at all.

Appearances are called display. And display is not the display of something standing behind it. It is dependent arising itself, vivid and unobstructed, with nothing behind it. Even the feeling of being a subject inside, looking out at a world, is part of the display. It arises together with the world it seems to look at.

Knowing awareness, rigpa, is not a projector throwing out a world. It is not a subtle stuff behind appearing, and it is not the basis. It is the knowing of the basis: knowing appearing as unfixed, dependent and empty. The clarity belongs to the appearing itself. And like the appearing, it is empty.

Does awareness know itself? Candrakīrti refused a self-knowing consciousness: a blade, he said, cannot cut itself. In Dzogchen, the self-knowing of awareness is no second act, and no knower watching from behind. It is simply clarity, recognised as belonging to no one, and empty like everything else.

Mipham also separates ordinary vision from pure vision. Within ordinary vision, no kind of being has the last word over another. Even the gods' nectar belongs here. In pure vision, the liquid appears as Māmakī. But pure vision is not a window onto a bedrock. Māmakī has no nature of her own. Pure vision is clearer because it meets appearing without the habit that splits it into a solid self and solid things.

Modern physics offers echoes of all this. Only echoes, not proofs.

For the physicist and philosopher Karen Barad, things do not exist first and then interact. They emerge through what she calls intra-action. So there is no liquid waiting before the meeting. The human drinker and the water appear together. The suffering ghost and the pus appear together.

In Carlo Rovelli's relational quantum mechanics, physical quantities have values only relative to another system. How, then, do two observers agree? With Emily Adlam, Rovelli answered: when a second observer interacts with the first and with what was measured, their results match. Agreement comes from checking across perspectives, not from a hidden state underneath.

But neither theory should be read as saying that relations are what is really there. That would only build another floor. Relations and the things related are identified together, and neither is the ground.

If appearances depend on channels of contact, what happens when a being shares almost no channel with something? Contact comes in degrees.

A tick responds mainly to three signals: the smell of mammal sweat, the warmth of a body, and hair. A pool of clear spring water has none of these, so it never appears in the tick's world. Yet the water is not nothing to the tick. If the tick falls in, the surface holds it and the current carries it away. Dark matter passes through the Earth without collision, yet gravity still bends its path. A neutrino can cross vast thicknesses of lead and almost never touch an atom. For it, the lead is neither a wall nor nothing. It is a region of very weak contact.

So what appears in a meeting, nourishment, pus, a barrier or an open passage, depends on which channels are active in it.

The hardest test is contact between worlds. In offerings to hungry ghosts, water is blessed with a mantra and given, and the tradition holds that it reaches them as nectar. Not by a substance that travels. Nothing passes from the human bowl into the ghost's mouth, just as nothing passes from the evening flame of a lamp into its midnight flame. The giving belongs to the human world. The relief belongs to the ghost's. They are linked as condition and result, with no common object underneath.

That the offering helps is a claim of tradition. This argument does not prove it. It only shows that such a link would need no hidden substance. And physics gives it no support, because in physics agreement needs real contact between observers.

Why do we cling so hard to an object underneath? Because of the container myth. We picture space as an empty box. We place a solid object in the middle, and put observers around it who must either see the real thing or be fooled. In Einstein's theory, spacetime is not an empty stage. It is the gravitational field itself. And Nāgārjuna shows that space cannot exist before its own defining mark, the absence of obstruction. When the box goes, the puzzle of one object seen many ways goes with it.

One more question. If there is no ultimate ground, why is the world so orderly? Barley seeds grow barley, not rice. Fire burns and does not freeze. Being empty of a fixed nature does not make anything arbitrary.

But emptiness is not what makes cause and effect work, either. Emptiness enables nothing and explains nothing. Things never needed a fixed essence in order to work. And something with a fixed essence could never arise, change or end.

Ask why there is a web of causes at all, and the question treats the whole web as one thing that needs an outside cause. But the whole is a label too, and dependent arising has no first beginning. The question does not uncover a hidden answer. It loses its object.

So far, one bowl. Now widen the view.

If each kind of being meets the bowl in its own way, then each lives in a whole world of its own. Its own textures, distances, dangers and nourishment. Buddhist texts call such a world a loka. The biologist Jakob von Uexküll called it an Umwelt.

Worlds are made by channels, not by walls. The tick lives in a world of three signals. The bat finds its way by echoes, in a darkness that is no obstacle to it. The honeybee sees ultraviolet light, so flowers that look plain to us carry bright landing paths for it. And we live among colours, words and clocks.

This needs no belief in ghosts or gods. The animals alone carry the argument. Those who accept the traditional realms can extend it to them.

Every world has three sides. A being, with its senses and habits. An environment, as that being meets it. And the channels joining them. None comes first. Even the shared environment, in the old Buddhist account, is shaped by the karma of those who live in it. It is not a box that would exist with no one inside.

Worlds also overlap by degrees. A human and a dog share sight, hearing and touch, so they can live and play together. A human and a tick meet briefly, at the skin. In the traditional account, a human and a hungry ghost share almost nothing.

Then where are the other worlds? The tempting answer is: right here, in the same space as ours. It sounds open-minded. But it quietly brings back the box. It assumes one neutral space underneath both worlds, holding them side by side.

Indian and Tibetan logic knows two kinds of negation. One denies something and implies something else. The other simply denies, and puts nothing in its place. Emptiness is the second kind. So we can say the other worlds are not elsewhere. Not far away, not behind a wall. And we stop there. We do not add, so they are in the same place. Each world has its own here, its own near and far.

Buddhist traditions have drawn two kinds of map. The old Abhidharma map stacks the realms in a vast vertical cosmos, with a great mountain at its centre, gods above and hells below. Other traditions stress that the realms, and even the pure lands, are not far away at all. Read without a container, both are maps, each useful for its own purpose.

Worlds touch where channels are shared. A tick reaches a human through warmth and skin. A dog and its owner meet in a glance. What never crosses is a view from nowhere. No being can step outside every world and compare them all from above. A human can learn a great deal about the bat's world, but always as a human.

So the human world is not the master copy. It has every practical priority for us, but no final authority over the bat, the bee or the ghost. Science does not escape this. It extends our senses, with instruments that reach the bee's ultraviolet and the neutrino. It is our most reliable map within its domains. But every instrument is built and read by humans.

And this is not relativism. Within every world there are still mistakes, like the jaundiced eye. And between confused seeing and pure vision, the difference is real.

Physics has its own many worlds. In Hugh Everett's interpretation of quantum mechanics, a single universal quantum state evolves without ever collapsing. Through a process called decoherence, it divides into branches that no longer affect each other, each with its own outcome. The branches are not places side by side. And the theory is fully deterministic.

Both views reject the picture of one world seen one way. But most defenders of Everett treat the universal wavefunction as the one thing that really exists. The Middle Way accepts no floor at all, not even a mathematical one. Everett's branches split over time. The worlds of the bowl arise together with those who live in them.

Many Buddhist traditions have explored these many worlds, and they do not all agree.

The Abhidharma says that the variety of the world is born of action. Yogācāra explains shared worlds as shared karma ripening in consciousness, though it makes consciousness the floor that Candrakīrti refused.

The Huayan school pictures Indra's Net: a net with a jewel at every knot, each jewel reflecting all the others, and none of them the original. A thing with a core of its own would exclude others. Nothing has one, and so nothing crowds anything out. The Tiantai school teaches that the ten realms, from hell to buddhahood, are present in each single moment of mind.

In the Vimalakīrti Sūtra, a disciple wonders why the Buddha's land looks so impure. The Buddha touches the ground with his toe, and the same land shines as a jewelled pure land. It was always so. The disciple had not seen it. Vajrayāna trains this as pure vision. And Dzogchen calls the openness of appearing unimpeded and transparent.

These traditions share three things. Worlds are shaped by those who live in them. Worlds need not obstruct one another. And transformation is a change in seeing, not a journey. What they do not share is the answer about a foundation. The reading here chooses no foundation, for the reasons we have followed.

It is worth saying plainly what has not been shown. None of this proves that other realms exist. Physics does not confirm it. It is not a theory that predicts anything. It is not idealism, because worlds are not inside minds. And it is one reading within a living tradition. What it removes is one confusion: the idea of a single hidden original beneath all the worlds.

What difference does it make? Humility, first. What we see is not the measure of all there is. Every creature we meet, from a bee to a stranger, lives in a world we cannot fully enter.

And compassion. If the ghost's pus is really pus for the ghost, its suffering is not a mistake to be explained away. The same holds, with no appeal to tradition, for an animal's fear, or the loneliness of a person whose world we do not share. No point of view owns the real world, so no being's world can be dismissed as only theirs. Emptiness takes nothing away from their suffering, or from the wish to relieve it.

Nāgārjuna answered his critics in one line. For whom emptiness works, everything works. A world of fixed natures could never change. Only where nothing is fixed can anything move at all.

And from the side of Dzogchen: when appearing is recognised as display, with no solid self grasping a solid world, it is met as it always was. Vivid, unproduced, and found nowhere.

Many worlds, then, and no floor beneath them. Everyday life never needed one.
