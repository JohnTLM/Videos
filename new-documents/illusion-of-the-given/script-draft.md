# The Illusion of the Given

Picture a single bowl of liquid, set down where many kinds of beings can see it.

A human sees clear, refreshing water. A hungry ghost, driven by deep habits of craving, sees foul pus and blood. A fish finds the air it breathes and the home it lives in. A god tastes delicious nectar. And an awakened being sees the pure wisdom form of water, the female Buddha Māmakī.

It is one of the oldest examples in Indian and Tibetan Buddhist thought, and it asks a simple question. What is really in the bowl?

Common sense has a ready answer. Something must be sitting there first, some raw, neutral stuff, and each creature looks at it through its own lens. Two layers: the hidden thing, and the many views of it.

The Middle Way of Nāgārjuna, and the Great Perfection, Dzogchen, read without a foundation, both take this picture apart. Agreement does not need a hidden bedrock. The thing seen, the one seeing, and the way they meet are identified together, each depending on the others. And the relations between them are just as impossible to pin down as the things themselves.

Start with a word we slipped in without noticing. The liquid. To say that beings see the same liquid differently already assumes an object waiting there, with its own identity, before anyone meets it. This is the old idea that Kant made famous: a thing in itself, hidden behind the senses.

So ask what this hidden thing is like. If it has definite qualities, if it really is thirst-quenching water, it cannot explain why the ghost meets burning pus. If it has no qualities at all, a featureless blank cannot cause any experience whatsoever. Either way, it does no work.

Someone will push back. Why not simply wetness, the quality that water and pus share? But if wetness is claimed to exist by itself, it fails. Wetness in general is an abstraction made by thought. In real life it only ever shows up as this drinkable water, this foul pus, this water a fish lives in. And if wetness is only an agreed reference point, it is no longer a hidden thing at all. It is a useful label for comparing views.

What Nāgārjuna's analysis targets is called svabhāva, own nature: a nature a thing would have all by itself, depending on nothing. A neutral substance beneath appearances is just that imagined nature in a modest disguise.

Then a real question remains. If there is no hidden object, why do humans agree so reliably that the liquid is water? Why doesn't the shared world fall apart into private fantasies?

The Indian master Candrakīrti turns the question around. Agreement does not prove a hidden object. Shared, reliable agreement is simply what everyday truth is.

He also refuses the opposite move. The Mind-Only view tried to solve the puzzle by removing outer objects and making a truly real consciousness the foundation. But a seeing cannot be identified without what is seen, and an object cannot be identified without the senses that pick it out. Neither mind nor matter comes first. Making the mind the container of everything is as mistaken as making matter the container.

Within everyday truth, mistakes are still caught. A person with jaundice sees a white conch shell as yellow, and other people, with healthy eyes, correct them. But notice where that judgement is made. It is made within one kind of being. The hungry ghost is not a jaundiced human. By the standards of its own kind, its senses work normally. It is not suffering an illusion of water. For the ghost, the liquid really does function as pus.

So what of the bowl itself? In this article's own terms, what is denied is a shared base that exists by itself, there before anyone perceives it. What is kept is a working reference point: a label, made from within one frame, for the place where different ways of appearing meet and can be compared. Its job is to coordinate, not to sit underneath.

The bowl example did not begin in the Middle Way. Vasubandhu, in his Twenty Verses, used the hungry ghosts to defend Mind-Only. Ghosts with the same karma all see a river of pus together, he said. Shared karma, ripening in consciousness, explains shared experience, and no river outside the mind is needed.

That answers the question by making consciousness the floor. Candrakīrti turned the example around. Just as the object does not exist on its own, he concluded, neither does the mind. The river of pus and the seeing of it stand or fall together. So the bowl does not show that the world is inside the mind. It shows that neither the world nor the mind is the floor.

Agreement also needs others to agree with. Indian logicians debated whether other minds could be proven, and both sides assumed that my own experience is certain, and yours only inferred. The Middle Way refuses that imbalance. Looked for closely, my stream of mind is no more findable than yours, and both work in daily life. Other observers are identified together with this one, as long is identified together with short.

A critic can press further. Senses count as healthy when they agree with others of their kind, and the kind is defined by sharing those senses. Isn't that circular? It is, and the circle is not a flaw. It would be fatal only if agreement had to rest on a final judge outside every point of view. There is no such judge. Nothing is being built on a foundation, so no foundation is missing. Wittgenstein reached a similar point: we agree, not in opinions, but in form of life. And inside the circle, correction is still real.

For humans, much of this agreement is carried by language. A child learns the word water because it works. Say it, and a glass arrives. Yet language is also where the habit of making things solid is strongest. The noun, the bowl, seems to point at one solid thing behind every view of it.

Dzogchen describes the same freedom from the side of experience.

In the Dzogchen of Longchenpa and Mipham, the basis is primordially pure. It is not a hidden block of stuff, a cosmic mind, or a starting point of the world. Longchenpa says it is called a ground only from a relative point of view. In the way things are, it is empty of being any ground at all.

Appearances are called display. And display is not the display of something standing behind it. It is dependent arising itself, vivid and unobstructed, with nothing behind it. Even the feeling of being a subject inside, looking out at a world, is part of the display, arising together with the object outside.

Knowing awareness, rigpa, is not a projector throwing out a world. It is not a subtle stuff behind appearing. It is the knowing of the basis: knowing appearing as unfixed, dependent and empty. The clarity belongs to the appearing itself, and like the appearing, it is empty.

The traditions divide on whether awareness knows itself. Candrakīrti rejected self-knowing awareness, and Śāntideva made the argument famous with a sword that cannot cut itself. In the author's own reading, this article follows Mipham. Ultimately, self-knowing cannot be found, like everything else. Conventionally, it stays a useful everyday term, to explain memory and the immediacy of experience, without an endless chain of minds watching minds.

Mipham also separates ordinary vision from pure vision. Within ordinary vision, the vision of beings in the cycle of existence, no kind of being has the last word over another. Even the gods' nectar belongs here. Pure vision is clearer: there, the liquid appears as Māmakī. But pure vision is not a window onto a bedrock. It is itself empty, and Māmakī has no nature of her own. It is clearer because it sees without the distortion that splits the world into solid subjects and solid things.

Modern physics offers echoes of this. Only echoes, not proofs.

For Karen Barad, things do not exist first and then interact. They emerge through what she calls intra-action. So there is no liquid sitting there before the measurement. The human drinker and the water appear together, and the suffering ghost and the pus appear together.

In Carlo Rovelli's relational quantum mechanics, physical quantities have values only relative to another system. How, then, do two observers agree? With Emily Adlam, Rovelli answered with cross-perspective links. If one observer records a value and a second later checks the record, the two will match. Agreement comes from checking across perspectives, much as healthy senses confirm one another, not from a hidden state underneath.

But neither theory should be read as saying that relations are what is really there. That would only build another floor. Nāgārjuna examined contact itself and found it cannot be found either. Relations and the things related are identified together, and neither is the ground.

If appearances depend on channels of contact, what happens when a being shares almost no channel with something? Contact comes in degrees.

A tick responds mainly to three signals: the smell of mammal sweat, the warmth of a body, and hair. A pool of clear spring water has none of these, so it never appears in the tick's world. Yet the tick can still drown in it. Dark matter passes through the Earth as if the planet were not there, yet gravity still bends its path. A neutrino can pass through lead on the order of a light year thick. The lead is neither a wall nor nothing. It is a region of very weak contact.

The bowl is not erased just because some being fails to register it. Everyday existence does not wait for someone to be naming it. But what appears there, nourishment, pus, a barrier or an open passage, depends on which channels are active in the meeting.

The hardest test is contact between worlds. In offerings to hungry ghosts, water is blessed with a mantra and given, and the tradition holds that it reaches them as nectar. Not by a substance that travels. Nothing passes from the human bowl into the ghost's mouth, just as nothing passes from the evening flame into the midnight flame. The giving belongs to the human world. The relief belongs to the ghost's. They are linked as condition and result, with no common object underneath.

Two cautions belong here. This does not prove that such practices work. It only asks what the claim would mean without a hidden substance. And the mantra is not a power living inside sounds, any more than the power to open lives in a key, apart from the lock, the hand and the door.

Why do we cling so hard to an object underneath? Because of the container myth. We picture space as an empty box, place a solid object in the middle, and put observers around it who must either see the real thing or be fooled. In Einstein's theory, spacetime is not an empty stage. It is the gravitational field itself. And Nāgārjuna shows that space cannot exist before its own defining mark, the absence of obstruction. When the box goes, the puzzle of one object seen many ways goes with it.

One last question. If there is no ultimate ground, why is the world so orderly? Barley seeds grow barley, not rice. Fire burns and does not freeze. Being empty of a fixed nature does not make anything arbitrary.

But emptiness is not what makes cause and effect work either. Emptiness enables nothing and explains nothing. Things never needed a fixed essence in order to work. Something with a fixed essence could never arise, change or end.

Press further, and ask why there is a web of causes at all, and the question treats the whole web as one thing that needs an outside cause. But the whole is a label too, and dependent arising has no first beginning. The question does not uncover a hidden answer. It runs out of steam.

So this is not nihilism. It is not idealism, because the mind is as empty as the things it meets. And it is not anything goes, because everyday truth works with reliable regularity. What is removed is only the anxious demand for a foundation, a dead, unmoving ballast beneath the living display.

It also matters beyond philosophy. If the ghost's pus is really pus for the ghost, its suffering is not a mistake to be explained away. No point of view owns the real world, so no being's world can be dismissed as only theirs. That is why compassion makes sense without a self behind it. Emptiness takes nothing away from others' suffering, or from the wish to relieve it.

Nāgārjuna put it in one line, where the key word means fits, or works. For whom emptiness works, everything works.

And from the side of Dzogchen: when appearances are recognised as display, with no solid subject grasping a solid object, appearing is met as it always was. Vivid, unproduced, and not found anywhere.

Everyday life does not carry on in spite of having no foundation. And it does not appear because it has none. It never needed one.
