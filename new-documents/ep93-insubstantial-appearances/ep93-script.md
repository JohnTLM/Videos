# Insubstantial Appearances

Hold a coin in your palm. It is cool, and it has weight. It seems obvious that it is simply there.

We carry two mistakes about appearances, and they mirror each other. The first is the familiar one. We take what we see and touch to have a fixed nature of its own, waiting to be found. This habit is called reification. It rarely feels like a view. It feels like the plain fact that things are there.

The second mistake is quieter. Having heard that things lack a fixed nature, we picture them as faint and ghostly, like shadows that could be brushed aside. But the coin is not faint. It is vivid and solid in its working.

Both mistakes share one assumption: that being solid and being empty cannot go together. Four points loosen it.

The first is mutual dependence. Nothing is long or short by itself. A stick is long beside a pebble and short beside a tree. A person is a parent only because there is a child. Your car is its wheels, its engine, the road, its purpose and its name. Gather those relations, and no car is found lying underneath them. And this is not a new ground made of relations. The relations are as empty as the things they relate.

The second point is that empty appearances are not flimsy. Nor are they harmless. A car has no essence that makes it a car from its own side. Yet when it swerves into a crowd, the injuries are real. Its emptiness does not reduce the consequences. It is part of why they follow. In a dream, a falling stone is as heavy as any stone. Emptiness is not a dimming of appearance. It is the way appearance is.

The third point is that things work without a hidden substance. A bridge carries traffic, though no single part of it is the bridge. Steel, deck, supports and name work together, and a truck can cross. The conditions that hold it up are patterns too, named in the same way. Nāgārjuna presses this point in the first chapter of his Middle Way verses. Search the conditions for a fixed ground and none is found, yet the bridge still holds.

The fourth point is habit, what the tradition calls karma. What appears to each mind is shaped by the tendencies it has gathered. People see water as something to drink. Fish see it as home. In dim light, a coiled rope becomes a snake, and your heart pounds. Look again, and the snake leaves nothing behind. The fear was built from habit, yet it was real in its effects. Habit is not a hidden cause behind appearances. It belongs to the same web.

So two errors fall away together. Nothing stands behind appearances, holding them in place. And appearances without such a ground are not unreal or disposable.

The coin is still cool and heavy in the hand. It is vivid, workable and empty, and it can be held without grasping.
