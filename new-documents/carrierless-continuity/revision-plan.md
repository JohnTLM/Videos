## B. New narration

**G1. Innate grasping.** After 1:29 ("…an enduring object to experience it"). About 30 s.

> But language is only the surface. Buddhist analysis distinguishes two layers of grasping. One is learned: the theories of soul and substance we inherit from philosophy and grammar. The other is innate: a pre-verbal sense of a solid "me", already present in an infant, that no argument installed. Refuting the theories removes only the first layer. The innate habit is undone not by argument alone, but by seeing through it directly, again and again.

**G2. Neither one nor two.** Replaces E1. About 12 s.

> The flame at dusk and the flame at midnight are not one flame, yet they are not two unrelated flames either. The later arises in dependence on the earlier, and no underlying fluid passes between them.

**G3. Fire and fuel (MMK chapter 10).** After 6:13, the end of the lamp passage. About 60 s.

> Nāgārjuna takes the lamp one step further. In chapter ten of the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā he asks whether fire is the same as its fuel or different from it. If the same, the burner and the burned would be one. If different, fire could exist without fuel. Neither holds, so the fire itself cannot be found, and he applies the same analysis to the self and its aggregates. This matters. It is not that real, discrete moments exist and the mind merely strings a label across them. The moments are designated too. Not that words are always running: designation here means that nothing appears established from its own side, even when no concept is operating. "Dusk flame" and "midnight flame" are as dependent and unfindable as "the candle". There is no layer of solid events beneath designation, and no layer of designation beneath the events either. The vividness of the flame and its naming arise together, like long and short. As chapter twenty-four puts it: dependent arising is emptiness; being a dependent designation, it is itself the middle way.

**G4. Sleep without annihilation.** Replaces E3, after "…carrying those changes" (7:35). About 30 s.

> But the stream does not simply break. The body goes on breathing, habits and memories remain, and the Tibetan traditions speak of a subtle clarity that continues even in dreamless sleep. What is absent is a self enduring through it, and the tracking of duration. Nāgārjuna's rule holds here as everywhere: what arises in dependence on something is neither that thing nor other than it. Neither permanence nor annihilation.

**G5. The Dōgen question.** Replaces E4. About 7 s.

> Yet if no moment carries over into the next, why does experience feel so fluid and seamless?

**G6. Death is not annihilation.** After 9:40 ("…supported a particular display"). About 25 s.

> This is not annihilation. In chapter twenty-seven, Nāgārjuna rejects both views of the future: that I will exist, and that I will not. Both assume a findable "I" to continue or be destroyed. The stream of causes and effects goes on (the traditions speak of rebirth, and Dzogchen of the bardo and the clear light of death), but no owner travels through it.

**G7. The mere I.** Replaces E5; the existing line "The Mere I is a nominal designation…" follows. About 20 s.

> To keep moral coherence, Candrakīrti and the Tibetan master Tsongkhapa speak of the "mere I". Unlike Western reductionism, it does not identify the self with the body, the mind, or their stream. The self is not found among them, yet it is not nothing.

**G8. Ending.** After the main video's last line, "Experience remains perfectly continuous, vivid and complete in each moment, unowned and entirely free from permanence." About 55 s.

> One last clarification. Every negation here removes something, the fixing of a carrier, and installs nothing in its place. No timeless ground, pure witness or deeper reality is left behind beneath appearances. So what is this vivid appearing? It is like a reflection with no mirror: vivid, dependent and unfindable. Its clarity is not lent by a mirror or a witness. Appearing is already clear, and that clarity is not something it possesses but simply what appearing is. In Dzogchen terms, the empty essence, the luminous nature and the unceasing display are not three things, and not one thing standing behind the others. And seeing this is not an achievement to hold. It is recognition: clear, alert knowing that keeps releasing the habit of fixing, moment by moment, with no one doing the releasing.
