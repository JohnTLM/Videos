# Carrierless Continuity: Identity Without a Bearer

A source text for a video overview. It draws on Nāgārjuna's Mūlamadhyamakakārikā (MMK), Candrakīrti and Tsongkhapa, Dōgen, and a non-foundational reading of Dzogchen. Every section is meant to be covered, in this order. The precision notes in each section are part of the content: they mark where a careless summary would turn the view into a different one.

---

## 1. The problem: everything changes, yet life feels continuous

Over a lifetime, the cells of the body turn over almost completely. Memories fade and rewrite themselves. Moods, beliefs and situations never hold still. Yet experience feels continuous, and we assume that one and the same person runs from birth to death.

For millennia, philosophy and theology have explained this with **substantialism**: the assumption that continuity through time needs an **invariant bearer**. This is an unchanging anchor that carries identity across change. It is pictured as an immortal soul, a Cartesian ego, an enduring psychological self, or a conserved physical or neural substrate. Changing processes such as thinking, acting and aging are pictured as resting on it, the way verbs rest on a noun.

This video argues that the bearer is not only unnecessary but incoherent, and builds an account of continuity with no carrier at all.

## 2. Two roots of the bearer: grammar, and something deeper

Language needs a noun to anchor a verb. We say "the fire burns" and "I think", and we project that rule onto reality, assuming every process needs an enduring thing that undergoes it. Nāgārjuna exploits exactly this in MMK chapter 2: the "goer" cannot be found apart from the going.

But language is only the surface. Buddhist analysis distinguishes two layers of grasping at a self:

- **Acquired grasping:** the theories of soul and substance we learn from philosophy, religion and grammar.
- **Innate grasping:** a pre-verbal sense of a solid "me", already present in an infant, that no argument installed.

Refuting the theories removes only the first layer. The innate habit is undone not by argument alone, but by seeing through it directly, again and again. In Dzogchen the root is called non-recognition (ma rigpa), not grammar.

*Precision note: do not present grammar as the root cause of self-grasping. It is one expression of a deeper, innate habit.*

## 3. The interaction paradox

Take the bearer seriously. If it is truly invariant and self-contained, it cannot be affected by anything. Changing conditions cannot touch it, register in it or modify it. But then it cannot learn, remember, perceive or suffer. An entity that change cannot reach cannot be the one who lives a changing life.

So the bearer fails at the very job it was invented for. Continuity does not require persistence. Nāgārjuna makes the general point in MMK 24.38: if things had their own fixed nature (svabhāva), the world would be unarisen, unceasing and frozen, with nothing to do and nothing to change.

## 4. Nāgārjuna's test: permanence or annihilation

Nāgārjuna's target is **svabhāva**, "own-nature" or intrinsic existence: the assumption that things exist from their own side, independently, with a self-contained essence.

Assume something has svabhāva, and only two outcomes are possible (MMK 15.10–11):

- **Permanence.** If it persists unchanged, it cannot give rise to anything new. An eternal essence produces no new state.
- **Annihilation.** If it ceases absolutely, the causal link is cut, and nothing connects it to what follows.

Any model built on intrinsic existence either freezes reality into a block or shatters it into disconnected pieces. "Exists" is the extreme of permanence. "Does not exist" is the extreme of annihilation. The middle way avoids both.

## 5. The momentary stream also fails

Some Buddhist schools (the Abhidharma) gave up the soul and replaced it with a stream of momentary, really existing events. Each moment arises, does its work, perishes and triggers the next. The person is a chain of real, discrete links.

Nāgārjuna tests the joint between two links (MMK chapter 20). If moment A has ceased before B arises, A no longer exists and cannot produce anything. If A has not ceased, cause and effect coexist, and there is no succession. The present cannot be found either: it is squeezed between a past that is gone and a future that has not come (MMK chapter 19). A string of really existing terminations is just a string of annihilations.

So reductionism fails exactly as substantialism does. Real moments are no better a foundation than a real soul.

*Precision note: this is essential for everything that follows. The solution must never quietly bring real, discrete moments back. Do not say that experience is "made of discrete, disconnected moments", that states are "completely distinct", or that real moments exist and the mind adds continuity to them. That is the view this section refutes.*

## 6. Dependent designation: neither one nor two

The way out is the Madhyamaka account of **dependent designation** (prajñaptir upādāya). Something that arises in dependence on something else is neither that thing nor other than it, and so it is neither permanent nor annihilated (MMK 18.10).

**The lamp.** A flame burns from dusk until midnight. The fuel and air are replaced at every instant, yet we reliably call it the same flame. The flame at dusk and the flame at midnight are not one flame, yet they are not two unrelated flames either. The later arises in dependence on the earlier, and no underlying fluid or traveller passes between them. This image comes from the early Buddhist dialogue the Questions of King Milinda, where the monk Nāgasena uses it.

**Fire and fuel.** Nāgārjuna takes the lamp one step further, in MMK chapter 10. Is fire the same as its fuel, or different from it? If the same, the burner and the burned would be one. If different, fire could exist without fuel. Neither holds, so the fire itself cannot be found. In MMK 10.15 he applies the same analysis to the self and the aggregates (body, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness).

**The moments are designated too.** It is not that real moments exist and the mind merely strings a label across them. "Dusk flame" and "midnight flame" are as dependent and unfindable as "the candle". There is no layer of solid events beneath designation. There is no layer of designation beneath the events either: the vividness of the flame and its naming arise together, like long and short, and neither is the ground of the other.

**Designation is not words.** "Designated" does not mean that words or concepts are always running. Designation here means that nothing appears established from its own side, even when no concept is operating, as in direct perception.

Continuity, then, is a reliable, functional designation applied across changing, dependent conditions. It works fully, and needs no carrier. MMK 24.18 says: whatever arises dependently is emptiness; being a dependent designation, it is itself the middle way.

## 7. Deep sleep and anesthesia

The substantialist pictures time as an objective river carrying a dormant subject along, even when nothing is experienced.

Look at general anesthesia. Three hours pass on the operating-room clock. In experience, no time passes at all. Upon waking, memory, the room and the clock are used to construct the sense of "having been under for three hours". The ticking clock shows that physical conditions changed. It does not show a hidden river of time carrying an unconscious passenger.

But the stream does not simply break. The body goes on breathing, habits and memories remain, and Tibetan traditions speak of a subtle clarity that continues even in dreamless sleep (the "clear light of sleep" in Dzogchen practice). What is absent is a self enduring through it, and the tracking of duration. Nāgārjuna's rule holds here as everywhere: what arises in dependence on something is neither that thing nor other than it. Neither permanence nor annihilation.

*Precision note: do not say that the timeline "breaks cleanly", that only "discrete, localized states" remain, or that the mind's naming is "the only continuity that exists". Each of these slides into annihilation or into real moments.*

## 8. Dōgen: firewood and ash

Yet if no moment carries over into the next, why does experience feel so fluid and seamless?

The Zen master Dōgen answers in the Genjōkōan. Firewood turns to ash and does not turn back into firewood. Yet, he says, we should not think of ash as the future of firewood and firewood as its past, as if one thing travelled forward in time and changed its form. Firewood abides in its own dharma position as firewood, which includes before and after, yet is cut off from before and after. Ash abides in its own dharma position as ash. In the same way, we do not say that life turns into death.

Experience is seamless not because something persists through it, but because nothing does. No passenger drags past moments into the present, so there is no friction. Each position is complete in itself.

*Precision note: Dōgen's dharma positions are not atoms of time. Do not describe them as "discrete, disconnected moments".*

## 9. Reification: the root of suffering

Suffering takes root in **reification**: treating a useful designation as an inherent property of the world. The substantialist picture makes life a possession: a central owner anxiously hoarding experiences and memories in a metaphysical vault.

Without a carrier, both the possessor and the possession drop out. Experience happens without a landlord. The warmth of a fire or a vivid memory appears complete, and unowned.

## 10. Death without an owner

Projected onto death, the substantialist picture produces deep dread: a permanent owner about to be robbed of everything gathered over a lifetime. If experiences were never held by an owner, there is no one to be robbed. Death is the ending of the particular physical and mental conditions that supported a particular display.

This is not annihilation. In MMK chapter 27, Nāgārjuna rejects both views of the future: that "I will exist" and that "I will not exist". Both assume a findable "I" to continue or be destroyed. The stream of causes and effects goes on. Buddhist traditions speak of rebirth, and Dzogchen of the bardo and the clear light of death, but no owner travels through it.

## 11. Who keeps the promise? The mere I

The main objection is ethical. If no one persists, who is responsible for past actions, and who keeps a promise made twenty years ago?

Candrakīrti, in chapter 6 of his Entering the Middle Way (Madhyamakāvatāra), and later the Tibetan master Je Tsongkhapa, answer with the **mere I**. The self is not identical with the body, the mind, or their stream, and it is not found among them. Yet it is not nothing. It is merely designated in dependence on a continuum of changing aggregates, and as a designation it functions fully. It makes promises and keeps them, incurs debts and repays them, and reaps the results of its actions.

The child in an old photograph shares no cells and no thoughts with the adult looking at it. Yet the mere I reliably links them.

*Precision note: the mere I is not Western reductionism such as Derek Parfit's, which identifies the person with a stream of physical and psychological events. Madhyamaka rejects that identification, as section 5 showed. Do not compare the mere I to Parfit.*

## 12. Freedom to change

An invariant soul would actually threaten freedom. If identity had a permanent essence, character flaws, traumas and habits would be carved into it as intrinsic properties, beyond change.

Because identity is designated across changing conditions, nothing is anchored to bedrock. Habits can soften. Character can be renewed. A long-standing grievance can end. Nāgārjuna makes this point in MMK chapter 24: only because things are empty of own-nature is a path, and any change at all, possible. Removing the bearer does not destroy accountability. It is what makes real transformation possible.

## 13. What, then, is the vivid appearing?

When the bearer, the real moments and the hidden river are all gone, experience does not go blank. The sound of rain, the warmth of the sun and the colour of a leaf are as vivid as ever, often more so. What is this vivid appearing?

It must not be answered by naming a substance, such as pure consciousness, energy, mind-stuff or a ground. Each of those would turn it into a thing that needs a carrier of its own. Appearing is described in three ways at once, none of them a layer beneath the others:

- **Clear:** it is vivid and known as it appears.
- **Dependent:** it arises only with its conditions.
- **Empty:** looked for apart from those conditions, it cannot be found.

**A reflection with no mirror.** Appearing is like a reflection with no mirror behind it: vivid, dependent and unfindable. Its clarity is not lent by a mirror or a witness. Appearing is already clear, and that clarity is not something it possesses but simply what appearing is. Nāgārjuna denies that a lamp lights itself (MMK 7.8–12), so the point is not that appearance illuminates itself in a second act. Appearing and its clarity are simply not two.

**Luminosity.** Dzogchen calls this clarity luminosity. Luminosity is not a light shining from behind appearances, and emptiness is not a luminous substance. In Dzogchen terms, the empty essence, the luminous nature and the unceasing display are not three things, and not one thing standing behind the others.

## 14. The closing clarification

Every negation in this account removes something, the fixing of a carrier, and installs nothing in its place. No timeless ground, pure witness or deeper reality is left behind beneath appearances.

Seeing this is not an achievement to hold. It is recognition: clear, alert knowing that keeps releasing the habit of fixing, moment by moment, with no one doing the releasing. Experience remains continuous, vivid and complete in each moment, unowned, and free from permanence and from annihilation.

*Precision note: do not end on "what remains" or "what is left behind". Do not call what is seen "timeless". Do not present recognition as attaining a state.*

## Names and terms (for correct pronunciation)

- **Nāgārjuna:** nah-GAR-ju-na. Indian Buddhist philosopher, about the 2nd century CE, founder of Madhyamaka.
- **Mūlamadhyamakakārikā (MMK):** MOO-la-MUD-hya-ma-ka-KAH-ri-kah. "Root Verses on the Middle Way".
- **Madhyamaka:** MUD-hya-ma-ka. The "Middle Way" school.
- **svabhāva:** swa-BHAH-va. Own-nature, intrinsic existence.
- **prajñaptir upādāya:** pruj-NYUP-tir oo-PAH-dah-ya. Dependent designation.
- **Abhidharma:** ah-bhi-DHAR-ma. Early Buddhist systematic philosophy.
- **Nāgasena:** NAH-ga-say-na. The monk in the Questions of King Milinda.
- **Candrakīrti:** CHUN-dra-KEER-tee. 7th-century commentator on Nāgārjuna.
- **Je Tsongkhapa:** jay TSONG-ka-pa. Tibetan master, 1357–1419.
- **Dōgen:** DOH-ghen. Japanese Zen master, 1200–1253. Genjōkōan: ghen-JOH-koh-ahn.
- **Dzogchen:** DZOG-chen. "Great Perfection".
- **rigpa / ma rigpa:** RIG-pa / MAH RIG-pa. Recognition / non-recognition.
- **bardo:** BAR-doh. The intermediate state after death.
