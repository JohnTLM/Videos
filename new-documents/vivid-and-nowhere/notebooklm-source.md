# Vivid and Nowhere: What a Phenomenon Is

A source text for a video overview of the bonus article "Vivid and Nowhere: What a Phenomenon Is" (*The Observer and the Taste of Presence*, Book 4). It draws on Nāgārjuna's Mūlamadhyamakakārikā (MMK), his *Precious Garland* and *Vigrahavyāvartanī*, Vasubandhu, Candrakīrti, and Longchenpa and Mipham read as a non-foundational Dzogchen. Every section is meant to be covered, in this order. The precision notes are part of the content: they mark where a careless summary would turn the view into a different one.

The short answer, which the whole video builds towards: a phenomenon is a vivid appearing that is not found when it is looked for. That is the same for a ball, a ghost and an excitation of a field.

---

## 1. The question

Most of our confusions come back to one question: what is a phenomenon? We meet phenomena all day. A cup is lifted, a thought passes, a friend's voice is heard on the phone. We rarely ask what they are, because we already assume we know. We take each to be something there, real in itself, whatever else is true of it.

## 2. The habit that wants a floor

The belief in real things is not a theory. Nobody reasons their way into it. It is a habit of seeing, in place before any thought about it. The tradition calls it **innate grasping**: the sense, prior to words, that what appears is there from its own side.

The habit is hard to dislodge because it retreats rather than gives up. Shown that the cup does not last, it agrees, and moves one step down. The cup changes, it says, but at each instant something must be really there. Even if things last only for a quantum-brief moment, the moment itself must be real.

This retreat has a long history in Buddhist thought. The Abhidharma schools took the person and the cup to be designations. Underneath, they looked for what was not designated: basic elements (*dharma*) each with its own mark, partless atoms (*paramāṇu*) and partless moments (*kṣaṇa*). These were the floor, and the everyday world was built on it.

## 3. Searching for the floor: arising, the moment and the atom

Nāgārjuna does not attack the floor with a new theory. He asks whether it can be found.

**Arising, abiding, ceasing (MMK chapter 7).** The Abhidharma gave every conditioned thing three marks: arising, abiding and ceasing. If arising is itself conditioned, it needs its own three marks, and so on without end; if it is not conditioned, it cannot be a mark of conditioned things (MMK 7.1). Looked for one by one, none of the three is found. The chapter ends not with a denial but with a description: "Like a magic show, like a dream, like a city of gandharvas in the clouds: so arising, so abiding, so ceasing have been taught" (MMK 7.34).

**The moment (*Precious Garland* 1.69).** In the *Precious Garland* (*Ratnāvalī*), Nāgārjuna points out that a moment has an end, so it must also have a beginning and a middle. Having three parts, it is not a partless moment, and the world does not abide even for that long.

**The atom (Vasubandhu, *Twenty Verses* 12–13).** Vasubandhu asked how a partless atom could join its neighbours on six sides without having six sides. If the six neighbours touch it at one place, they all occupy one place, and a mountain would be the size of an atom. If they touch it at six places, it has six sides, and so has parts. Vasubandhu stopped there and kept the mind as the real. Madhyamaka asks the same question of the mind (section 5).

**What has and has not been shown.** Time and matter have not been disproved. Moments and particles stay as useful as they ever were. Clocks keep time and bricks build walls. What is not found is the one thing the habit wanted: a piece of reality, however small, that is real in itself.

*Precision note: Vasubandhu argued **against** the partless atom; he did not propose it. Do not present him as its theorist. Do not add mathematical claims of your own, for example that points of zero duration "cannot add up to" a second: the argument is about whether a self-standing moment can be found, not about the mathematics of the continuum. And do not conclude that time or matter is unreal, or that a timeless reality lies behind the clock.*

## 4. Three ways of appearing, none privileged

Phenomena present in very different ways. Some appear solid and undeniable: a ball can be tossed and caught, an apple bitten, and a car can knock a person down. Some appear ghostly and dreamlike: a thought, a feeling, a memory, the taste of tea, mental events with no weight and no edges. Some are not met at all except as descriptions: physics speaks of probability clouds, wave functions and excitations of fields.

The habit ranks them. For one person the solid is the most real and the mental is a shadow of it. For another the mental is most real and matter is its projection. For a third the equations are the reality and the apple is just how they look to us.

This view ranks none of them. The solid is not more real than the dreamlike, and the mental is not more real than the physical. All three are ways of appearing and of being described, and all three meet the same search. That is why the view is neither realism nor mind-only. Making matter the floor and making mind the floor are the same mistake.

The scientific descriptions are among the most reliable designations we have, within their domains. But they are conventions too, built from measurements, models and agreements. They do not show that things are empty, and emptiness does not need them. A field is no better a candidate for "what is really there" than an apple.

## 5. Vivid, and not found anywhere

So what is a phenomenon? It is a vivid appearing that cannot be found, even while it is appearing vividly.

**The chariot.** "Cannot be found" is meant precisely. Candrakīrti's chariot is the classic test (*Entering the Middle Way*, *Madhyamakāvatāra* 6). The chariot is not its parts, and not something apart from them. It is not the collection of parts, nor their shape, nor something that owns or contains them. Searched for in each of these ways, it does not turn up. Yet the chariot still carries its passenger.

**A sound.** The same search can be turned on a sound. A bell rings, clear and bright. The sound is not in the bell, not in the air, not in the ear, and not sitting apart from them. It is not here, and not there. It is not anywhere.

**Not in the mind.** It is not inside one's own mind, and not inside someone else's. This step matters. It closes the way out that the mind-only schools offered: if the cup is not out there, perhaps it is in here, as a mental image. Candrakīrti refused that way out. The mind is looked for in the same way and is not found either. We find hearing, a passing thought, a feeling; we never find a container in which the sound is kept.

**Not even nowhere.** This is the easiest step to miss. Having failed to find the thing, the mind quietly makes its absence into something: a void, a silence behind the sound, a background that remains when the things are gone. But the absence is not a place and not a thing. Nāgārjuna warns that those who make emptiness into a view are incurable (MMK 13.8), and that even "empty" is said only for the sake of designation (MMK 22.11).

This is what a **non-implicative negation** means. The search removes the assumed thing and puts nothing in its place. It does not reveal a hidden emptiness, a silent ground, or an indescribable reality beyond words. When all the identifying stops, nothing unidentified is left over.

**And yet the bell rings.** What the search fails to find is one specific thing: a sound standing there by itself, with its own nature. The ringing never needed that. Not found does not mean does not happen. Vivid and unfindable are not two layers, a surface and a depth. They are true of the same appearing at once.

## 6. The conditions are just as empty

When the conceptual mind tries to say what an appearance is, it reaches for dependence. The red depends on the light, the eye, the surface and the attention. The sound depends on the bell, the air and the ear. An appearance, it seems, sits at the centre of a web of relations.

That is a better picture than a self-standing thing. But it has a trap in it: the web can quietly become the new floor. The apple is unfindable, we say, but surely the conditions are real; the relations are what is really there.

Nāgārjuna examined the conditions first. "The own-nature of things is not found in their conditions or the like" (MMK 1.3). Things without own-nature have no existence of their own, so "when this exists, that comes to be" cannot describe one real thing producing another (MMK 1.10). In chapter 14 he examines contact and connection themselves: "Coming into contact, having come into contact, and the one who brings them into contact are not found" (MMK 14.8).

So the conditions are as unfindable as what they condition. The bell is itself an appearing, with conditions of its own. The conditions are not a solid network hidden behind the appearance. They too are appearances, as dreamlike as the apple. Relation and the things related are designated together, like long and short: neither is prior, and neither is the ground.

*Precision note: do not say that things "function through" dependence, or that an "infinite" chain or web of dependence is what makes them work. Dependence is not a mechanism, an engine or a new foundation, and the point is not an infinite regress. Say instead that the conditions and relations are as unfindable as what they condition, and that nothing needed a floor in the first place.*

## 7. Appearing already is dependent arising

There is a subtler trap: first there is a raw appearance, and then the mind describes it as dependent. Dependence would then be a label laid over something given.

That is not how it is. Appearing does not wait for the description in order to be dependent. The red is dependent in the very moment it is red. There is no undependent layer underneath that the concept of dependence then covers.

The same is true of designation. A thing is always picked out as something, by perception before it is picked out by words. There is no undesignated layer on which labels are placed. Designation is not a label on top of a thing. It is the whole status the thing has.

This is why Nāgārjuna can say that dependent arising is what is called emptiness, that this is itself a designation, and that this is the middle way (MMK 24.18). Since there is no phenomenon that has not arisen dependently, there is no phenomenon that is not empty (MMK 24.19). Dependent arising and emptiness are not two facts that happen to coincide. They are one fact, seen from the side of convention and from the side of analysis.

## 8. Illusions that work

If phenomena are unfindable, are they unreal? The tradition answers with images: the magic show, the dream, the city of gandharvas in the clouds (MMK 7.34), and the mirage (MMK 17.33).

The images are chosen for their two sides. A magician's elephant walks across the stage and the audience gasps: it is seen, it frightens, and it is not an elephant. A dream can make the dreamer weep. A mirage is seen, and thirsty travellers turn toward it. None of these is nothing. What they lack is a nature of their own, found when looked for.

Nāgārjuna presses the point. Suppose the Buddha made a magical person, and that magical person made another (MMK 17.31). The second is made, and it acts. In the same way, actions, agents and their fruits are like magical creations, and they work (MMK 17.32–33). In the *Vigrahavyāvartanī* he answers the objection that an empty argument cannot refute anything: one magically created man can stop another (verse 23).

So the effects are fully real. The car with no essence still injures. The grief is still grief. The medicine still heals.

One way of putting this needs care. It is tempting to say that things can work *because* they are empty, as if emptiness were a power that makes function possible. Emptiness does not do anything. It is not a cause, a condition or a ground. Function never needed an essence in the first place. And the reasoning runs the other way: if a thing had a nature of its own, fixed from its own side, it could not arise, change or end, and nothing could be done with it (MMK 24.16–20). That is a reductio of own-nature, not a power granted by emptiness.

Longchenpa built a whole book on these images: *Finding Rest in Illusion*, the third of his *Trilogy of Rest*. Its eight similes come from the Perfection of Wisdom sūtras: a dream, a magical illusion, a trick of the eye, a mirage, an echo, a city of gandharvas, a reflection and an apparition. Each is vivid and each is not found. As the title says, the point is not to make the world seem thin. It is rest: nothing in it needs to be held.

*Precision note: never say that things work, appear or are vivid "because they are empty", or that emptiness "allows", "enables" or "makes possible" anything.*

## 9. Clarity without a knower

Every appearance is clear. The colour is seen as colour; the sound is heard as sound; the thought is known as thought. This clarity is not one more category of things alongside apples and cars. It runs through all appearing: the solid, the dreamlike and the described alike.

The habit reads this clarity as a light behind the appearances: a knower, a witness, a background awareness that receives and illuminates them. That is the observer returning in a quieter form. Looked for, it is not found. You will find a tree and a seeing; you will not find a viewer standing apart from both. The sound does not need a listener behind it to be clear. The clarity is the appearing's own, not something it borrows from a knower.

The Dzogchen word "luminosity" is read the same way: not a bright substance behind the display, but the knowing clarity of the display itself. And the clarity is empty too. Appearing, clarity and emptiness are not three things. Nor is one of them the thing that stands behind the other two.

*Precision note: do not call clarity or luminosity a ground, a pure consciousness, a witness, or "self-luminous" in the sense of lighting itself (Nāgārjuna denies that a lamp lights itself, MMK 7.8–12). It is the appearing's own clarity.*

## 10. The Dzogchen words, read without a ground

The Dzogchen tradition of Longchenpa and Mipham has its own words for this. Read carelessly, they can sound like a ground behind appearances. Read here, they say what the earlier sections have said.

- **Primordial purity (*ka dag*).** From the beginning, appearing was never fixed and never had a nature of its own. "Pure" names no cleaning that took place, only the absence of what was never there. It is the emptiness of appearing, named from the side of the basis.
- **Spontaneous presence (*lhun grub*).** Appearing is already complete; it owes its completeness to nothing hidden. This is not an uncaused source. Spontaneous presence and dependent arising describe the same appearing: dependent arising as it is analysed, spontaneous presence as it is met when the fixing stops. Neither is higher than the other.
- **Display (*rtsal*).** Thoughts and appearances are display. Display must not be heard as the display of something standing behind it. It means only this: dependent arising, vivid and unobstructed, with nothing behind it.
- **Self-release (*rang grol*).** A thought recognised as unfixed releases itself as it arises, like a drawing on water, which needs no eraser. Nothing outside is applied to it, and no one does the releasing.
- **Appearance-emptiness (*snang stong*).** Appearing and emptiness are inseparable. This is not a mixture of two things. It is the vivid appearance and its not being found: two aspects of one appearing.
- **Rigpa.** Some sources call rigpa the pristine awareness that is the basis itself. This reading takes it only in the other sense the tradition gives it: the knowing of the basis, recognising appearing as unfixed, dependent and empty. Rigpa is not a witness, not a self-standing knower and not a ground. The basis (*gzhi*) is the emptiness of appearing, not a ground behind it. Longchenpa says the ground is called a ground only from a relative point of view; in the true nature of things it is empty of being one.

**Three cautions.** First, reading Dzogchen together with Nāgārjuna's Middle Way is a synthesis. It is a defensible reading of Longchenpa and Mipham, not the only one in the Nyingma tradition; some lineages treat Dzogchen as its own vehicle, not reducible to Madhyamaka analysis. Primordial purity and Prāsaṅgika emptiness are read together here, not claimed to be the same term. Second, none of these words is exempt: singled out, each is designated and empty like everything else. Third, traditional Nyingma teaching says more than this reading does. It speaks of the clear nature of mind and sets ordinary mind (*sems*) apart from rigpa as wisdom (*ye shes*). In Longchenpa, spontaneous presence also includes qualities said to be present in the ground, and display is spoken of as the ground's own dynamism. Recognition usually comes through a teacher's direct introduction (*ngo sprod*), not at the end of a search. And compassion (*thugs rje*), the third aspect of the basis, is not taken up here. These narrowings are deliberate, so that no ground returns, and a reader from that tradition will rightly find more there.

*Precision note: cover all six terms and the three cautions. Do not say that everything is "the play of primordial awareness", that appearances are the "radiance of rigpa", or that rigpa is the ground or the basis.*

## 11. Recognition, not search

The search of sections 3 to 6 does real work. It catches the habit in the act. Each time the mind looks for the floor and finds nothing, the assumption that there must be one loosens a little.

But the search is not where it ends. Phenomena do not become unfindable when the search is finished. They were never findable. The search only shows what was already the case. And the searching itself can harden into a habit: a dividing mind that keeps one last place for itself, the place from which it searches.

What the Dzogchen tradition points to is recognition. When appearing is recognised as it is, nothing needs to be looked for. Each appearance is met as vivid, unfindable and releasing itself as it arises, with nothing to hold and no one holding.

Recognition is not an achievement. It is not a state built up by effort, and the moment it is held as an accomplishment, the fixing has returned. Nor is it passive. It still takes clear knowing, the same clear knowing that undoes the habit wherever the habit returns. It is recognised again and again, not reached once and kept.

*Precision note: recognition is neither an achievement nor passivity; say both.*

## 12. Wonder

And then the ordinary world is simply the ordinary world. The apple is red, the car is dangerous, the friend's voice is warm. Nothing is lost. What has gone is only the floor that was never there.

The tradition has a word for meeting appearances in this way: wonder (*ngo mtshar*). Colours, sounds, tastes, thoughts, cars, planes and birds appear, vivid and precise, whenever their conditions come together, and none of them is found standing anywhere. That is not a problem to be solved. It is magical, and miraculous.

*Precision note: end here, on wonder and the ordinary world. Do not end on "what remains" or "what is left", and do not call anything "timeless".*
