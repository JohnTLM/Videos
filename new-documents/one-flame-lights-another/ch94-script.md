# One Flame Lights Another

A lamp burns through the night. In the last watch, its flame is not the flame of the first watch. The oil is different. The wick is shorter. And yet there was never a moment when one flame stopped and another began.

Now light a second lamp from the first. Nothing passes over. No small piece of the first flame jumps across. And yet the second flame is not unconnected with the first. It was lit from it.

This is the oldest image the Buddhist tradition has for rebirth. To see why it is the right one, we have to start with a puzzle.

Every Buddhist tradition teaches that actions bear fruit, and that one life conditions the next. Every Buddhist tradition also teaches that there is no self. No soul. No inner owner. Nothing that stays the same beneath the changing body and mind.

Put side by side, these can look like a contradiction. If there is no one who dies, who is reborn? If nothing passes over, in what sense is the next life mine? And if it is not mine, why should I care?

The contradiction is only apparent. It rests on one assumption: that continuity needs a carrier. Remove that assumption, and rebirth is no harder to make sense of than waking up in the morning.

Most of us picture rebirth like this. Inside each person is a someone, a soul or a spark. The body is its house. At death the house falls, and the someone moves out, travels, and moves into a new house.

The skeptic uses the same picture. There is no someone inside, the skeptic says. The mind is what the brain does. When the brain stops, nothing is left to move out. The believer and the skeptic agree that rebirth would need a traveler. They disagree only about whether there is one.

But look at an ordinary day. You fall asleep at night and wake in the morning. You take it for granted that the same someone slept and woke. Yet in deep sleep, nothing at all is experienced. No witness keeps watch through the gap. The body of ten years ago has been replaced, cell by cell. Its moods and plans are not the ones it has now.

And still, the person who wakes is, by every ordinary standard, the person who fell asleep. They keep their promises. They pay their debts. They recognize their friends.

So the question of rebirth is only the sharpest form of a question that comes every morning. If continuity within a life needs no traveler, continuity between lives does not need one either.

From the start, the Buddha taught against two opposite views. The first is eternalism: a self that persists unchanged, so that the same one who acted in a past life now meets the result. The second is annihilationism: a self that is wiped out at death, or a later being who has nothing to do with the earlier one.

The tradition treats annihilationism as the shadow of eternalism, not its cure. To say a self is destroyed, you must first assume there was a self to destroy. And to say the later life has nothing to do with the earlier ignores the plain dependence between them. The rice that grows from a seed has something to do with the seed.

Between these two, the Buddha taught dependent arising. This being, that comes to be. Life conditions life. Deed conditions fruit. Nothing carries the one into the other. And nothing cuts them apart.

The Middle Way of Nāgārjuna tests every candidate for a carrier in the same way. It asks: if this thing had a nature of its own, where would that nature be found? Then it looks.

The Sanskrit word for that nature is svabhāva, own nature. Something with own nature would be what it is from its own side. Not made. Not depending on anything else. Not changing.

A soul that never changes could not learn, or suffer, or be freed. A stream of subtle consciousness is either the same at every moment, and then it does not flow, or different at every moment, and then it is not one thing carrying anything. A store of seeds either holds them unchanged, and then they never ripen, or changes with them, and then it is one more changing process.

Each candidate, examined, turns out to be one more dependent appearance. It needs a carrier of its own. And so no carrier is found.

But notice what is not lost. The person still wakes. Still keeps promises. Still meets the fruit of what they have done.

One rule matters here more than any other. When the carrier is removed, put nothing in its place. Not a subtler soul. Not a stream. Not an energy. Not even emptiness itself. Many attempts to explain rebirth deny the soul, and then quietly install a replacement. The Middle Way catches each replacement as it appears.

It also helps to keep three kinds of claim apart. The logic shows that rebirth does not need a carrier. The tradition affirms that rebirth happens, and holds that affirmation in the ordinary way it holds everything. And whether a particular memory of a past life is accurate is a question for evidence.

The first does not prove the second. Removing a bad argument against something is not the same as giving a good argument for it. Emptiness does not prove rebirth. It removes the one objection that seemed to stand against it.

Rebirth is not a later addition to the Buddha's teaching. On the night of his awakening, the texts say, he recollected his own past lives, one, two, a hundred, a thousand. Then he saw beings dying and being reborn according to their deeds. Then he knew the ending of the defilements, and was freed.

In none of these does he see a self passing from life to life. He sees lives, and the dependence of lives on deeds. A pattern of conditioning. Not a passenger.

The clearest correction came to a monk named Sāti. Sāti believed that it is this very same consciousness that roams and transmigrates, not another. He was not crude. He did not posit a soul. He posited consciousness, and he was trying to explain rebirth.

The Buddha rebuked him sharply. Consciousness, he said, arises dependently. Without a condition, there is no consciousness. A fire is named by what it burns. A fire on logs is a log fire. A fire on grass is a grass fire. In the same way, consciousness is named by its condition. Eye consciousness. Ear consciousness. Mind consciousness.

Consciousness is not one thing moving from fuel to fuel. Ask which fire is the same fire that moved from the logs to the grass, and you have already imagined a fire apart from its fuel.

So a modern reader who rejects rebirth because there is no soul to be reborn is in an odd position. They agree with Sāti about what rebirth would require. And they disagree with the Buddha.

A naked ascetic named Kassapa once asked the Buddha whether suffering is made by oneself or by another. The Buddha declined both. To say the one who acts is the one who experiences the result, he said, is eternalism. To say one acts and another experiences is annihilationism. Avoiding both, he taught by the middle: the chain of conditions, link by link.

This is not evasion. It is an exact answer to a better question. Not, is it the same one? But, on what does this depend?

Another wanderer asked what a being depends on between laying down this body and taking up the next. The Buddha answered with an image. When a flame is tossed by the wind and carried some distance, it is fueled by the wind. In the same way, a being between lives is fueled by craving.

Even in the gap, there is dependence, not a carrier. And what keeps the round turning is named. Not a self that wishes to survive, but the thirst for being. That is why the ending of craving is the ending of rebirth, and why that ending destroys no one.

The Buddha also left some questions unanswered. Does an awakened one exist after death? He turned the question back. If a fire went out in front of you, he asked, which direction did it go? East, west, north, south? The question does not apply. It burned on its fuel, and when the fuel ran out, it went out.

The ending of rebirth is like that. Not the annihilation of a self, but the ending of the fuel.

The early teaching left a problem for the schools that followed. An intention arises and passes in a moment. Its fruit may come lives later. What connects them?

The schools gave many answers. One said there is a person who is neither the same as body and mind, nor different from them. Another said the past deed still exists. Another said each deed plants a seed in the mental stream. Another described a storehouse consciousness that holds every seed until it ripens. The Theravāda said the last moment of one life is followed at once by a linking consciousness in the next, which does not come over from the past, but arises in dependence on it, like an echo, or the impression of a seal.

Each bridge names something. Taken as a thing with a nature of its own, each becomes a soul by another name. Taken as a way of speaking about the dependence, each is harmless. The echo, especially, is exact.

Nāgārjuna asked a more radical question. Is any bridge needed at all?

If an action had a nature of its own, he argued, it would be permanent. A permanent action could never have been done. And it would ripen again and again, without end. A karma that existed by itself could not do the work karma is supposed to do.

So the agent, the action and the fruit are all empty, and this is exactly why they work. The link between deed and fruit is real and reliable, as the link between a rice seed and rice is real. But it is not carried by something traveling through the moments. The gap the bridges were built to span is a gap only for things with a nature of their own.

Nāgārjuna compares karma to a magician's creation. A magician conjures a person, and that person conjures another. They really act. A mirage is seen, and travelers turn toward it. A dream can make the dreamer weep. Karma is like this. Fully working, and nowhere found standing by itself.

He also looked at the very moment of rebirth. If the last moment of one life had already ceased, nothing would connect it to the next. If it had not ceased, two lives would run at once. No link is found at the joint. The continuity is not denied. It is simply not found as a thing that flows.

Later, Candrakīrti drew the conclusion. No storehouse is needed. A deed that never existed by itself never ceased by itself either. Its fruit can arise from it, even long after, with nothing to keep it in. Tsongkhapa added that the very ceasing of an action is itself a condition. And the person who is reborn is the mere I: the I designated on changing body and mind. Not found when looked for. And yet acting, remembering, keeping promises, and meeting the results of what it has done.

Tibetan Buddhism describes the interval between death and rebirth in detail. It is called the bardo, the in-between. As a person dies, the elements dissolve one into another, until what the texts call the clear light of death dawns.

This clear light can be read in two ways. On one reading, it is a luminous awareness beneath all experience, uncovered at death. That would be the traveler at last. On the reading these books follow, luminosity is the knowing clarity of appearing itself. It is not a light behind things, and it is empty like everything else. What dawns at death is not a hidden ground, but appearing recognized as unfixed.

That is why the Tibetan teachers say the best preparation for death is practice in life. Every moment is in between. If appearing is recognized as it is now, there is nothing new to learn at death.

Some teachers speak of a very subtle mind that continues from life to life. Here the danger returns in its most refined form. Read as a thing with its own nature, it is Sāti's view again. Read as one more empty, changing condition on which a person is designated, it is no more a carrier than the ordinary mind. The step from one reading to the other is short.

In Japan, the Zen master Dōgen rejected the view that the body perishes while a true self lives on and moves elsewhere, like someone leaving a burning house. That, he said, is not the Buddha's teaching.

His best known image is firewood and ash. Firewood becomes ash. Yet we should not see the ash as after and the firewood as before. Each abides fully in its own position. Life does not become death, as winter does not become spring.

This does not deny continuity. It denies that one thing passes from the firewood into the ash. Each moment is complete, and needs no substance running through it.

There is also the story of Baizhang's fox. An old man told Baizhang that long ago he had been an abbot. A student asked him whether a person of great practice still falls into cause and effect. He answered: does not fall into cause and effect. For that answer he was reborn as a fox for five hundred lives.

He asked Baizhang the same question. Baizhang answered: does not obscure cause and effect. At these words, the old man was freed.

Not falling into cause and effect treats freedom as an escape from conditions. Not obscuring cause and effect is freedom as clarity about them. Emptiness gives no exemption from karma. Because nothing has a nature of its own, karma works.

A modern listener may still object. The mind depends on the brain. When the brain dies, the mind stops.

The tradition agrees that the mind depends on the body. But the objection usually assumes that rebirth needs a mind that floats free and moves to another body. That is exactly the carrier the Buddha refused. And the objection has a substance of its own. It takes matter as the one thing that is really real. The Middle Way does not grant that either. None of this proves rebirth. It shows only that the usual argument against it depends on a picture the tradition never held.

What about memory? Most people remember nothing of a past life. But memory was never the carrier, even within one life. You remember almost nothing of your first three years, and nothing of deep sleep. Yet you are held to the promises you made. What makes a later person continuous with an earlier one is dependence, not a stored memory. Neither the same, nor another.

Is karma unfair, if the one who suffers is not the one who acted? Karma is not a court, and no one administers it. It describes how intention tends to shape what follows. Not everything that happens is the fruit of past action. And to look at a suffering child and think, this is deserved, is to misuse the teaching. It was given to free beings, not to judge them.

Is there evidence? Researchers such as Ian Stevenson and Jim Tucker have studied young children who report memories of a previous life. Critics have raised serious questions. The view does not need to settle the debate. But notice this. Even an accurate memory would not show that a soul traveled. It would show a dependence between two lives. It would fit the lamp, not the passenger.

So what does this change for the living, the dying and the grieving?

For the living, the place where karma is made is now. This act. This word. This turn of mind. Responsibility does not weaken without a self. The mere I acts, and the mere I meets the result. What falls away is only the anxious sense of a ledger held against a self.

For the dying, the view removes one dread: the image of an experience held, a holder taken away, a holding left unfinished. It does not remove the fear of pain, which can be met with care. It does not promise that the person goes on as the same one. What it affirms, with the tradition, is continuity without a carrier.

For the grieving, grief is not a mistake. Love is real, and its ending is a real loss. But the view removes two extra weights. The dead were not wiped out, with nothing following. And they were not carried away, to be found again as they were. Between the two is the lamp. A flame that was real, that has gone out, and that lit others.

And the end of rebirth? It is not the destruction of someone. It is the fire that went in no direction. What ends is the fuel, the craving and grasping on which the burning depended. Nirvāṇa is not another place. It is this same appearing, with the fixing ended.

The round turns on not-knowing: taking what is dependent as fixed. It ends with knowing: seeing that what appears was never fixed. That seeing does not wait for another life. The whole chain can be seen in a single afternoon. And it can be seen through in one.

This is all the continuity there is. The child becomes the adult. The one who sleeps becomes the one who wakes. The deed becomes its fruit. One life becomes the condition of the next.

It needs no traveler. It leaves no one cut off. One flame lights another, and the lamp is burning now.
