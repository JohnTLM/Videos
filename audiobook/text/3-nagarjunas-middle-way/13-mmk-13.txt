Nah-gar-joo-nah's Middle Way. Chapter 13. Examination of Compounded Phenomena.

A mirage looks like water. Walk towards it and the water is not there, yet the shimmer is real enough to photograph. It deceives not by being nothing, but by seeming to be more than it is. The Buddha said that all conditioned things (saṃskāra) are like this: they have a deceptive nature, and so they are false.

This chapter asks what that saying means. Does it mean that things exist but keep changing their nature? Or that everything is empty, and emptiness is the true nature behind them? Nah-gar-joo-nah rejects both readings. Change, examined, does not need a nature that changes, and emptiness is not a new thing to believe in. The chapter ends with one of the most quoted verses of Mahd-yah-mah-kah: emptiness is the way out of all views, and whoever turns emptiness into a view is beyond help. It picks up the conditioned things of Chapter 7.

# The verses.

The translation is new, made from the Sanskrit for this site. The Sanskrit is given above each verse; it follows the GRETIL e-text (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0).

# Verse 13.1.

The Blessed One said: whatever has a deceptive nature is false. All conditioned things have a deceptive nature; therefore they are false.

# Verse 13.2.

If whatever has a deceptive nature is false, what is it that deceives? The Blessed One said this to bring emptiness to light.

# Verse 13.3.

Things lack own-nature, because they are seen to become otherwise. Yet there is no thing without own-nature, since emptiness belongs to things.

# Verse 13.4.

If there is no own-nature, what is it that becomes otherwise? If there is own-nature, how could it become otherwise?

# Verse 13.5.

Becoming otherwise makes no sense for the thing itself, nor for something else. For the young do not age, and the aged do not age either.

# Verse 13.6.

If the thing itself became otherwise, milk itself would be curd. And as for something other than milk, what could ever become curd?

# Verse 13.7.

If there were anything that is not empty, there might be something called empty. But there is nothing that is not empty; how then could there be anything empty?

# Verse 13.8.

The victorious ones taught emptiness as the way out of all views. But those for whom emptiness is a view, they called incurable.

# What does 'deceptive' mean?

The chapter starts from a saying in the discourses (13.1): whatever has a deceptive nature (moṣadharma) is false, and all conditioned things have that nature. Verse 13.2 then raises a fair question. If everything deceptive is false, what is it that deceives? A deception seems to need something real behind it, as a trick needs a magician.

The same verse gives the answer: the Buddha said this to bring emptiness to light. Conditioned things deceive by appearing to stand on their own, when they arise only in dependence. The deception is not a hidden deceiver; it is the gap between how things appear and how they are found when looked for. Nothing behind the scenes is needed, just as a mirage needs no secret lake.

Verses 13.1–2. Conditioned things deceive by seeming to have a nature of their own. The deception lies in the seeming, not in a hidden agent.

# Change without a nature that changes.

Verse 13.3 gives the opponent's next position, and it is subtle. Things lack own-nature, he agrees, because they are seen to change. But, he adds, there is no thing without own-nature at all, since emptiness belongs to things, and a property needs something to belong to. So for him, emptiness becomes a quality of things, and those things must really be there to have it. Commentators read the second line in slightly different ways, but this is its point: emptiness is being turned into a property, with a real bearer underneath.

Nah-gar-joo-nah answers with a dilemma (13.4). If there is no own-nature, what is it that changes? There is no fixed something to go from one state to another. And if there is an own-nature, how could it change? A nature that could become something else would not be its own nature. So change does not prove a substrate of any kind, not even a substrate called emptiness.

Verses 13.5–6 make this concrete. Does the young person age? While young, no: youth is not old age. Does the old person age? No: already old. Does milk become curd? If the milk itself became curd, milk and curd would be the same, and you could pour curd into your tea. If something other than milk became curd, why not water?

Verses 13.4–6. Change, analysed as a fixed thing taking on a new state, fails both ways. What remains is change itself, described within before and after, with no changer underneath.

This is not a denial that milk turns sour. It is a denial that a milk-thing persists through the souring, carrying a nature from one state to the next. The books call this change without a changer.

# Empty of what?

Verse 13.7 removes the last foothold. 'Empty' only makes sense in contrast with 'not empty'. If there were something not empty, something with a nature of its own, then other things could be called empty by comparison. But nothing is found that is not empty. So emptiness is not a special quality some things have and others lack, and it is not a stuff that all things are made of. It is the absence of own-nature, nothing more. Without anything non-empty, there is no empty thing standing in its place either.

# Emptiness is not a view.

That leads to the famous last verse (13.8). The Buddhas taught emptiness as the way out of all views (dṛṣṭi). But those who make emptiness itself into a view are incurable.

The traditional image, used by Chun-drah-keer-tee, is a purgative medicine. A doctor gives it to clear a blockage. If it works, it clears the blockage and then leaves the body too. If the medicine stays in the body, it becomes a new illness, and there is no further medicine to remove it. Emptiness clears away views that grasp at own-nature: 'it is', 'it is not'. If it is then grasped as the final truth, the true nature of reality, the last remedy has itself become the disease.

Verse 13.8. Emptiness is a remedy, not a position. It removes grasping at own-nature and is not itself something to grasp. Held as 'the truth', it becomes the one illness no further teaching can cure.

This is why the books keep warning against a 'substrate of emptiness': a hidden openness, potential or awareness behind appearances, to which emptiness is quietly promoted. The view of emptiness is the most refined form of the habit the whole text is dismantling. Emptiness is itself empty.

# What is left standing.

Milk still turns to curd, and children still grow old. 'It has changed' is still true and useful. Things are still called empty, correctly, as a way of saying that nothing has own-nature. What goes is a fixed something that changes, and a fixed emptiness that is true of things the way colour is true of them.

The teaching of emptiness is still taught, studied and practised. It works exactly as a medicine works: by being used, not by being held. The test of having understood it is not having a new view, but being freed from the need to hold one.

# Words to know.

Conditioned things (saṃskāra): everything that arises from causes and conditions.

Deceptive nature (moṣadharma): appearing to be more than it is, as a mirage appears to be water.

Becoming otherwise (anyathābhāva): change, the alteration of a thing.

Emptiness (shoonya-tah): the absence of own-nature in anything; not a stuff, not a ground.

View (dṛṣṭi): a position held as true, especially about what things are in themselves.

Incurable (asādhya): beyond remedy, because the remedy itself has been made a view.
