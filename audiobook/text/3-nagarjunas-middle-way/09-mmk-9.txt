Nah-gar-joo-nah's Middle Way. Chapter 9. Examination of the Prior Entity.

You see a bird, you hear it sing, you feel the morning air. It seems obvious that someone is doing all this: one person, the same one, who sees and hears and feels. That someone seems to come first. The seeing and hearing belong to them, the way rooms belong to a house. Take away a particular sight or sound and the person is still there, waiting for the next one.

This chapter examines that someone. The opponent calls it 'the prior entity': a self that exists before, and apart from, the activities it owns. It is the natural sequel to Chapter 3, where seeing was found not to stand on its own, and to Chapter 8, where agent and action were found to be established only together. If the seer were already there before any seeing, the result of Chapter 8 would be wrong. So Nah-gar-joo-nah looks for the seer before the seeing.

# The verses.

The translation is new, made from the Sanskrit for this site. The Sanskrit is given above each verse; it follows the GRETIL e-text (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0).

# Verse 9.1.

Some say: the one to whom seeing, hearing and the rest, and feeling and the rest, belong exists before them.

# Verse 9.2.

'For how could seeing and the rest belong to something that does not exist? So that entity is there before them, already in place.'

# Verse 9.3.

But that entity which is in place before seeing, hearing and the rest, and before feeling and the rest: by what is it designated?

# Verse 9.4.

And if it is in place even without seeing and the rest, then without doubt they too will exist without it.

# Verse 9.5.

Someone is made known by something; something is made known by someone. How could there be someone without something, or something without someone?

# Verse 9.6.

'No one exists before all of seeing and the rest taken together. It is made known by one of seeing and the rest at one time, and by another at another time.'

# Verse 9.7.

If no one exists before all of seeing and the rest, how can anyone exist before each one of them on its own?

# Verse 9.8.

If the seer is the very same as the hearer, and the same as the one who feels, it would exist before each of them. But that makes no sense.

# Verse 9.9.

If the seer is one, the hearer another and the one who feels yet another, then there would be a hearer while there is a seer, and there would be many selves.

# Verse 9.10.

Nor is it found in the elements from which seeing, hearing and the rest, and feeling and the rest, arise.

# Verse 9.11.

If the one to whom seeing, hearing and the rest, and feeling and the rest, belong is not found, then these are not found either.

# Verse 9.12.

For what is not found before seeing and the rest, nor at the same time, nor after, the ideas 'it exists' and 'it does not exist' fall away.

# The case for a prior self.

The chapter opens with the opponent's view (9.1–2), and it is a reasonable one. Seeing, hearing and the other senses, and feeling and the other mental events, must belong to someone. A seeing that belongs to no one makes no sense. And whatever they belong to must already be there, or there would be nothing for them to belong to. So there is an owner, in place before them: a self that has experiences, the way a house has rooms.

Several schools of Nah-gar-joo-nah's time held something like this. Some Buddhist schools, the Pudgalavādins, spoke of a 'person' that is neither the same as the aggregates nor different from them. Non-Buddhist schools spoke of an ātman, a self that is the witness of experience. Most of us hold it without a name. It is the sense that 'I' am here first, and experiences come to me.

# Made known by what?

Nah-gar-joo-nah's first question is simple (9.3). If this self is in place before seeing, hearing and feeling, by what is it designated? What shows that it is there, so that we can point to it and name it?

It cannot be shown by seeing, hearing or feeling. Those come after it, by the opponent's own account, and the self is supposed to be there without them. But we have no other way of pointing to a seer than by seeing, to a hearer than by hearing. A self before all experience is a self that nothing reveals. There is nothing to designate it by.

The next step turns the argument around (9.4). Suppose the self really can be in place without seeing and the rest. Then it is independent of them. But independence goes both ways. If the self does not need seeing, seeing does not need the self, and seeing could happen with no one to whom it belongs. That is exactly what the opponent said was impossible in 9.2. The view undoes itself.

Verses 9.1–4. A self that comes before all experience has nothing to reveal it. And if it can stand without experience, experience can stand without it, which is the very thing the opponent denied.

# Someone and something.

Verse 9.5 states the positive point in a few words. Someone is made known by something: a seer by a seeing, a listener by a sound heard. Something is made known by someone: a seeing is someone's seeing. Neither is found without the other.

Verse 9.5. The same pattern as agent and action in Chapter 8: seer and seeing are designated together, and neither is the base on which the other rests.

This is not the claim that there is a seer after all. It is the claim that 'seer' and 'seeing' are designated together, like agent and action, and neither can be found first. Those who have seen through the self in practice sometimes find Nah-gar-joo-nah's wording puzzling here: if there is no seer, why insist that seeing needs one? The answer is that he is arguing on the opponent's ground. If you insist that seeing exists on its own, you must also find the seer it belongs to; and the seer is not found. So seeing, taken as existing on its own, is not found either.

# One at a time?

The opponent retreats (9.6). Perhaps the self is not before all the senses together, as a separate thing behind them. Perhaps at one moment it shows itself through seeing, at another through hearing, at another through feeling. It moves from window to window, like a person in a house looking out of one window and then another.

Nah-gar-joo-nah's reply is short (9.7). If the self is not found before all of them together, it cannot be found before each one separately. What is not in the whole set is not in any member of it. A self that is there before this seeing, but not before experience as a whole, is not a coherent idea.

# One self or many?

Then a dilemma about the person in the house (9.8–9). Is the seer the same as the hearer and the one who feels, or different?

Verses 9.8–9. A single self behind all the senses would have to exist before each of them, which 9.7 has ruled out. Separate selves for each sense would multiply the self and keep a hearer waiting during every sight.

If they are one and the same (9.8), then this one self exists before each sense: it is there as the hearer even while it is seeing, ready and waiting. That puts it back before each particular experience, standing on its own, which 9.7 has just ruled out; and 9.3–4 showed that such a self has nothing to reveal it.

If they are different (9.9), the seer is one self, the hearer another, the feeler another. Then whenever there is a seer there is also, separately, a hearer, even when nothing is heard; and one person would contain many selves. That is not what anyone meant by 'I'.

# Not in the elements either.

Perhaps the self is in the material basis of experience: the elements, earth, water, fire and air, from which the body and its senses are made (9.10). But the same search applies. The elements are not found as an owner of seeing standing before seeing. And the elements themselves are examined and found empty in their own chapter (Chapter 5), so they cannot serve as a solid floor.

Then the result runs back the other way (9.11). If the owner of seeing and the rest is not found, then seeing and the rest, taken as things that belong to an owner, are not found either. This does not mean nothing is seen. It means that seeing is not found as a self-standing event with an owner attached, any more than the owner is found apart from it.

# Before, at the same time, after.

The last verse widens the search to every time (9.12). The self is not found before seeing and the rest, as the opponent claimed. It is not found at the same time, alongside them, as a second thing. It is not found after them, as something they leave behind. And for what is not found at any time, the ideas 'it exists' and 'it does not exist' simply fall away.

Verse 9.12. A self sought in every time and not found is not thereby proved to be non-existent. Both 'it exists' and 'it does not exist' presuppose something to have that status, and both fall away together.

The wording is careful. Nah-gar-joo-nah does not conclude 'so the self does not exist'. That would be another view about the self, a claim that there is something and it is absent. He says the conceptions (kalpanā) of existing and not existing stop. This is the non-implicative negation again: the false picture is removed and nothing is put in its place.

# What is left standing.

You still see the bird and hear it sing, and you can say 'I saw it' and be right. Your friend can ask what you heard, and you can tell them. The word 'I' keeps its everyday use: it picks out this person, designated in dependence on this body, these experiences and this history. Tsong-kah-pah calls this the mere I. It acts, remembers and keeps promises.

What goes is the owner behind the windows: a self already there before any experience, waiting for sights and sounds to arrive. Look for it and you find seeing, hearing and feeling, and the 'I' designated together with them; you do not find a witness standing apart. Many people meet this first in practice, as the discovery that there is seeing without a seer behind it. The chapter adds the second half: the seeing, too, is not found on its own.

# Words to know.

Prior entity (prāg vyavasthito bhāvaḥ, 'the entity in place before'): a self held to exist before, and apart from, the experiences it owns.

Self (ātman): in this chapter, an owner and witness of experience, existing from its own side.

Seeing, hearing and the rest (darśana, śravaṇa): the activities of the senses.

Feeling and the rest (vedanā): pleasant, unpleasant and neutral feeling, and the other mental events.

Made known (ajyate, prajñapyate): pointed out, designated; the self is designated only by what it is said to own.

Conceptions (kalpanā): the ideas 'it exists' and 'it does not exist', which fall away when nothing is found to have either status.
