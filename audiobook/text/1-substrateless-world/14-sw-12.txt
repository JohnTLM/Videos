The Logical Necessity of a Substrateless World. Chapter 12. Dissolution of Internal versus External Worlds.

# The Cartesian Split: Internal Mind versus External World.

Perhaps the most deeply ingrained substantialist dichotomy in Western thought is the Cartesian split between an 'internal mind' and an 'external world'. Under this framing, the external world is a cold, non-conscious container filled with self-existing objects, forces and spatial boundaries that exist whether or not anyone observes them; and the internal mind is a private room—what Daniel Dennett called the 'Cartesian theatre' (Consciousness Explained, 1991)—sitting behind the eyes, receiving signals, forming representations and gazing out at the world.

This spatial split treats 'inside' and 'outside' as hard, ontological divisions built directly into the fabric of reality.

However, as Chapters 2 and 4 established, when we examine any physical or mental boundary independently of context, contrast, and conceptual framing, no self-established wall between an 'inner subject' and an 'outer object' can be found.

# Inside versus Outside as Relational Designations.

If 'inside' and 'outside' are not solid, self-established boundaries in nature, how do we account for our everyday experience of having an inner mental life and an outer physical world?

Inside and outside are not fundamental ontological territories; they are functional, relational designations. The 'field' within which such lines are drawn is no more findable than the lines themselves; it too is a designation.

Consider a house. 'Inside the house' and 'outside the house' are perfectly clear: one is dry in a storm and the other is not. Yet no wall is found that separates the house from space by itself; the boundary is drawn around wood, stone, doors and shelter for the purposes of living, and the open doorway belongs to both sides at once. Calling thoughts, emotions and bodily sensations 'internal' and tables and trees 'external' is the same kind of framing—drawn by contrast and for use, and entirely reliable within the framework in which it is drawn.

The boundary between self and world does not mark an absolute divide between two different kinds of substance; it marks a functional distinction, drawn conventionally, with no seamless web beneath it. Chapter 4 explained why removing the wall does not leave everything one.

# A Lived Example: Your Name Across a Crowded Room.

At a crowded party the room is a wash of voices, and you hear none of them in particular. Then, from across the room, someone says your name, and you hear it at once, clearly, over all the rest. Psychologists call this the cocktail-party effect. Where did that sound happen? It came from over there, from a particular speaker; yet it was singled out by something in you—by years of being called by that name—before you had decided to listen.

Asked whether the sound was inside or outside, the honest answer is that the question does not fit. It was out there, in the room, and it was yours, salient only to you. Neither description is wrong; each draws the line for a purpose. The experience is not an outer sound copied into an inner room, nor an inner event projected outwards. It is one vivid appearance, designated as 'a voice over there calling me', with speaker, sound, hearer and name identified together.

# A Lived Example: A Window at Dusk.

Stand at a window as evening falls, with the lights on in the room behind you. For a while the glass shows two scenes at once: the street outside, with its trees and passing cars, and the room behind you, with its lamp and its bookshelf and your own face, laid over the street in faint reflection. As the light outside fades, the room grows brighter in the glass and the street dims, until the window becomes a mirror.

Which part of what you see is inside, and which is outside? The reflected lamp appears out among the trees; the tree appears to stand in the room. The question can be answered, conventionally, with a little physics and a step to one side. But in the seeing itself there is one appearance, vivid and continuous, in which inner and outer are drawn by attention and knowledge rather than given in the scene. The dusk window is an ordinary emblem of this chapter. The line between the room and the street is real and useful, and one does not try to walk through the glass. But it is drawn in the seeing, by attention and purpose, and it is not a wall between an experience and a world.

# Collapsing the Container Model of Mind.

The Cartesian split relies heavily on the container model of mind—the belief that consciousness is a localized vessel into which sensory impressions enter and from which intentional actions exit.

The preceding chapters have already removed its three supports. Perception is not entry: subject, sense organ and object co-arise (Chapter 10). Cognition is not trapped inside: it extends across body, tools and environment (Chapter 11). And there is no unconditioned spectator inside the head watching experience unfold (Chapters 7 and 11).

# One World Without Walls.

When the Cartesian split is removed, experience and world are no longer set against each other.

Appearance is not a cover. The experienced world is not a screen masking a non-conscious reality underneath; there is nothing behind it of which it is the surface. Nor do distinctions become arbitrary. My thoughts are still mine and not yours to read; a wall still stops a hand. These distinctions hold because, within the framework in which they are drawn, they hold up under further experience and can be corrected when they fail. And mind and world are not two things joined by a bridge, nor two sides of a single process, which would be one more substrate. They are designated together, each identified only through the other.

# Forgetting the Self.

Doh-gen puts the dissolution of the inner room in a sentence that has become famous: 'To study the buddha way is to study the self. To study the self is to forget the self. To forget the self is to be actualized by myriad things' (Gen-joh-koh-ahn, translated by Tanahashi). The self is not destroyed, and the world does not swallow it. What is forgotten is the self as a spectator standing apart, and with it the sense of a world standing over against that spectator.

The sentence is easily read as describing a merger: the self dissolving into a larger whole, which is then the true reality. That would be the container model again, enlarged to the size of the universe. Read with the rest of the Gen-joh-koh-ahn, it says something simpler. Self and myriad things are designated together; neither is the ground in which the other is found. When the spectator is no longer assumed, things are met where they are—the cold in the hand, the voice across the room—without an inside from which they are observed.

# A Lived Example: The Boundary of the Body.

The line between self and world seems most secure at the skin. Here, surely, is a boundary nature drew. Yet even this line moves. In the rubber hand illusion, a person watches a rubber hand being stroked while their own hidden hand is stroked in time with it; within minutes many feel the touch in the rubber hand and experience it as their own (Botvinick and Cohen, Nature, 1998). A blind person's cane, a carpenter's chisel and a driver's car are felt, in use, as extensions of the body.

The boundary of the body is real and functional; it is what lets us dodge a branch or feel a burn. But it is drawn in experience, and redrawn as circumstances change. It is a designation of what belongs to 'me', made in dependence on sight, touch, movement and habit—not a wall found in nature between an inner subject and an outer world.

# Objection: Isn't Experience Private?

If there is no inner theatre, why can no one else feel my toothache? Privacy seems to prove that there is an inside to which only I have access.

What privacy shows is an asymmetry of access, not a hidden room. My toothache depends on this tooth, these nerves and this attention; another person's experience depends on theirs. The asymmetry is real and conventionally important—it is why we must ask others how they feel. But it is accounted for by the different conditions on which each experience depends, not by a sealed inner space in which each one occurs. Two people at different windows see different views of the same street without either view being locked inside a box.

# Objection: Is the World Then Only Mind?

If the wall between inner and outer is a designation, perhaps everything is on the inner side after all. The Yoh-gah-chah-rah, or Mind-Only, school of Buddhism argued something like this: what appear as outer objects are aspects of consciousness, as in a dream, where a whole world appears without anything outside the dreaming mind.

Chun-drah-keer-tee answered the Mind-Only school directly, and his answer is the position of this book. Conventionally, he argued, outer objects exist just as much as the mind that knows them, and to affirm the one while denying the other is a conventional mistake. Ultimately, neither mind nor object is found; to deny objects while keeping mind is to make mind the last substance (Mahd-yah-mah-kah-vah-tah-rah 6.45–97). The dream example cuts both ways. In a dream, the dreamed mountain and the dreaming mind appear together and are equally unfound on waking. The dream does not show that mind is real and objects are not, but that both are designated in dependence on each other. Removing the wall does not leave everything inside. It leaves no inside and no outside established from their own side—and an ordinary world in which the distinction is still drawn, and still useful.
