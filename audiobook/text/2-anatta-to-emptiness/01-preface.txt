The Transition from Anatta to Emptiness. Preface.

I first posted the seven phases of enlightenment around twenty-five years ago; they later appeared on the Awakening to Reality blog. The contemplative journey behind that framework, and behind everything in this book, spans roughly forty years. For most of that time, consolidating it properly into a book was something I wanted to do and never found the time for. Work commitments simply did not leave room for the kind of sustained writing a project like this demands.

What changed is the arrival of AI capable enough to work through this material with me directly — not writing it for me in any sense that replaces my own thinking, but making it possible for me to think out loud, be pushed back on, refined, corrected where I overreached, and helped to organize four decades of scattered insight into something coherent enough to finally put down. I am not a strong writer. Without this help, this book would not exist, full stop — not a better version of it, not a slower version of it, but no version at all. I want to say that plainly and gratefully, rather than leave it unstated.

I am John Tan, known also as Thusness, co-owner of the blog Awakening to Reality (www.awakeningtoreality.com).

# Why Deconstruct the View? A Word to the Sceptical Reader.

A fair question, and one worth answering directly rather than leaving for the reader to wonder about on their own: why go through all of this? Why the sustained dismantling, chapter after chapter, of self, causality, substrate, medium, and the very demand for an explanation — when the taste of anatta, or of total exertion, can arrive on its own, directly, without a page of philosophy anywhere in sight?

I can only answer this from what actually happened to me. The idea that grasping is directly the result of being blinded by 'inherent' thought was something I had heard, in one form or another, for years before it happened. It never landed until the eureka moment of anatta itself. That moment did not simply confirm something I already understood intellectually. It opened something: a freedom, a full opening of radiant presence, an opening at the level of energy centres in the body, all of it directly and unmistakably tied to the reification of mental constructs, not as metaphor but as mechanism. Grasping was not a separate problem sitting alongside inherent thought. It was inherent thought, felt directly, for the first time, as what it actually is.

Even that was not the end of understanding it. It took much longer to see how deeply 'inherent' thought pervades the entire body and mind — not as an abstraction confined to the head, but as something with attachments reaching down to the level of individual cells, a view so total and so early-installed that it operates more like a spell than a belief. This is the real difficulty with inherent view: it is not that we hold a mistaken opinion we could simply reconsider. It is that the view itself constitutes the matrix we look through, and a matrix cannot be seen past from inside it. That is why insight into this had to come the way it came — not as an argument that persuaded me, but as a seeing that broke the spell from within, one layer at a time.

As the deconstruction continued — seeing through cause and effect, seeing through phenomena, seeing through the split between subject, action, and object — something happened that I had not expected going in. The mind and the body's energy began opening automatically, without effort. Less attachment. Less compulsive thought. More direct radiance, arising on its own. I have come to think of deconstruction as functioning much like the opening of energy centres described in older contemplative systems, except considerably more effective, because it goes directly at the root holding those centres closed in the first place. Being hypnotized by inherent view, as deeply and totally as we are, costs the body enormous energy — energy spent maintaining a construction that never needed to be maintained. Seeing through the construction does not just clarify the mind. It releases what was being spent to keep the construction standing. In my own experience, this has been more effective than long hours of formal sitting practice, and in a specific sense safer, because it does not depend on forcing an altered state; it simply removes what was obstructing what was already there.

There is, however, a real trap hiding inside this approach, and I want to name it honestly rather than let the method sound safer than it is. Deconstruction can quietly degrade into something that looks identical to itself but is not: ceaseless thinking, mental gymnastics performed under the name of inquiry, the mind endlessly turning ideas over without ever actually touching the unknowing attachment those ideas were supposed to expose. This is the same failure the rest of this book calls relentless negation — a mind that mistakes the activity of examining for the event of seeing, and keeps examining long after seeing was what was actually called for. The difference between real deconstruction and this counterfeit is not always obvious from the inside, which is exactly why it deserves to be named plainly here rather than assumed away: genuine deconstruction frees energy and quiets thought; its counterfeit consumes energy and multiplies thought, while sounding, to the person doing it, exactly like progress.

This is also where the path's other failure mode does its damage, from the opposite direction. Spiritual bypass uses a correct view of emptiness or non-duality to excuse the actual work of recognition, treating 'it's all empty anyway' as a reason to stop looking rather than as a description of what looking finds. Between these two failures — endless conceptual gymnastics on one side, premature closure on the other — the corrective in both cases is the same: not a bigger experience, but a view precise enough to notice which of the two is happening, in the moment it starts happening.

There is a further reason, more practical than either of these, for writing the view down as carefully as this book tries to. An experience that cannot be understood cannot be stabilized, taught, or trusted under pressure. It remains a peak, visited and then, in some quiet way, lost or doubted later, because the mind that had it never developed a framework robust enough to hold it against the return of ordinary difficulty, grief, or fear. View is what lets an opening become a place to live rather than a place once visited. This is why the desynchronization this book keeps returning to matters: it is not a philosophical nicety, but the actual point at which realization either becomes stable and load-bearing, or quietly erodes back into the substantialist habits it briefly interrupted.

So the deconstruction is not offered as a substitute for direct experience, and it cannot manufacture what direct experience alone provides. I did not arrive at freedom by reasoning my way there, and no reader will either. But I would not have been able to trust what arrived, protect it from being quietly re-narrated back into substance, or free the energy that had been spent maintaining the spell for as long as it was maintained, without following the seeing all the way through, view by view, attachment by attachment. It is offered because view and experience were always meant to close the same gap from two sides, and a book that offered only one side would be handing the reader half of what they actually need.
