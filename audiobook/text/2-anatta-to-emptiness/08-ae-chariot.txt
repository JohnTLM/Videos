The Transition from Anatta to Emptiness. The Chariot Comes Alive—Dependent Designation and the Birth of Conventional Reality.

# A Note Before the Search Begins.

Before following Nah-gah-say-nah through the search for the chariot, it is worth pausing to say plainly what this chapter actually is. It is not the third insight in a sequence, arriving after anatta and total exertion and preparing the way for Chun-drah-keer-tee and Nah-gar-joo-nah. It is something closer to the seed from which every other insight in this book quietly grows.

I did not see this clearly when I first encountered the dialogue with King Milinda. Like most readers, I took it as an elegant argument about a vehicle — clever, instructive, but bounded to its own example. It took years of returning to it, and of watching the same structure resurface in entirely unrelated places, before I understood that the chariot was never only about chariots.

Everything this book will go on to describe is already contained here, in miniature. When the wheels are removed from the chariot, nothing 'chariot' clings to them afterwards — no residue, no trace, nothing carried over. Later, this same absence will appear again when we look closely at the difference between sight and sound and taste, and find nothing shared between them either. When the axle is removed, there is no diminished chariot, no partial chariot — there is no chariot at all. This same completeness will appear again when we examine causality, and find that an effect does not loosely depend on its conditions but is designated together with them, entirely and exhaustively. The wheels are never wheels first and parts of a chariot second; the chariot is never assembled after its parts and named afterwards. This same absence of sequence will appear again in the collapse of the self, in the taste of total exertion, and even in the relationship between direct experience and the view that comes to understand it. And through all of this, the chariot never once stops carrying its rider. This same undiminished functioning, arising from nothing found, will appear again and again, in every chapter that follows, under different names, in different registers, always the same discovery wearing a new disguise.

I raise this now, before the search itself begins, so that the search can be read for what it is. What follows is not one example among several. It is the whole shape of the argument, given once, cleanly, in the simplest possible case — a case with nothing at stake, nothing to defend, nothing the mind flinches from examining honestly. Once seen here, it does not need to be seen again for the first time. It only needs to be recognized, again and again, in every place the mind had quietly assumed something had to be standing underneath.

# What the Chariot Did Not Do, and What It Did.

Yet it is important to be exact about what the chariot supplied, and what it did not. It did not deepen the taste of anatta. It did not intensify total exertion. Both of those had already arrived in their fullness, vivid and undeniable, long before Nah-gah-say-nah's dialogue meant anything to me. The self had already gone missing under investigation. Walking had already revealed itself as complete participation, owing nothing to a walker standing behind it. Nothing about the chariot added to that.

What the chariot supplied was something the taste alone could never provide: a way for the world to be understood as what it had already been tasted to be. This distinction is easy to overlook and difficult to overstate. Before the encounter with the chariot, I had directly experienced a world without an observer and without separation — and yet I continued, unknowingly, to interpret that same world through a framework built entirely on independently existing things quietly interacting with one another. The taste was non-substantialist. The view remained substantialist. I did not notice this contradiction, because the two were operating on entirely different registers, and nothing had yet shown me that they were supposed to agree.

The chariot is what finally broke this. Not by adding a new experience, but by giving the mind, for the first time, a working demonstration of how something can function completely while being nowhere findable — a demonstration simple enough, and safe enough, to be checked directly rather than merely believed. Once seen clearly in so small and harmless a case as a cart, the same seeing became available everywhere else it had been quietly withheld: from causality, from qualia, from presence itself. Every hindrance that remained after anatta and total exertion had already done their work was a hindrance of exactly this kind — not a failure to taste freedom, but a failure of the inherited framework to recognize the freedom that had already been tasted. The chariot did not teach me something new about the world. It taught me, at last, how to understand what I had already seen.

The decisive breakthrough in understanding emptiness does not occur when one first reads Nah-gah-say-nah's famous dialogue with King Milinda. Nor does it occur when one intellectually accepts that the chariot cannot be found among its wheels, axle, frame, or yoke. At that stage, the dialogue remains an elegant philosophical illustration, admired for its cleverness but still external to lived experience. The breakthrough occurs when a recognition dawns: Nah-gah-say-nah was never merely explaining a vehicle. He was explaining the structure of conventional reality itself.

At that moment, the chariot ceases to be an example and becomes the grammar of every ordinary experience. Walking is the chariot. Hearing is the chariot. Thinking, the physical body, society, and language itself—all are the chariot. Every convention possesses the same structure. One suddenly realizes that the world has always functioned exactly like Nah-gah-say-nah's chariot, a recognition that fundamentally changes everything.

The ordinary mind unconsciously assumes that designation operates through a simplistic, linear process, beginning with an independently existing object that subsequently receives a linguistic name. This framework of conceptual designation dictates that a tree, a car, or a person exists first, and only then are the labels 'tree', 'car', or 'John' assigned. While this picture appears obvious, it quietly assumes what Mahd-yah-mah-kah philosophy sets out to dismantle. It takes for granted that there is already an independently existing entity waiting to receive a conceptual label. Nah-gah-say-nah refuses this foundational assumption from the very beginning.

Instead of asking what an object should be called, Nah-gah-say-nah asks where exactly the object is located. He systematically questions whether the chariot is identical to its wheels, its axle, its frame, the collection of its parts, or something apart from that collection. As every possibility collapses, the conclusion is frequently misunderstood. Many readers assume Nah-gah-say-nah is claiming that the chariot does not really exist and that humanity simply pretends it does. This interpretation is mistaken. Nah-gah-say-nah is not replacing realism with fictionalism; rather, he is explaining the precise mechanisms of how conventional reality functions.

# From Nah-gah-say-nah to Chun-drah-keer-tee: The Sevenfold Search.

Nah-gah-say-nah's search in the Milindapañha is brief: the chariot is not the pole, not the axle, not the wheels, not the frame, not their sum, and not something apart from them. Some centuries later Chun-drah-keer-tee, Nah-gar-joo-nah's great commentator, made the search exhaustive. In his Introduction to the Middle Way (Mahd-yah-mah-kah-vah-tah-rah 6.151 and following) he examines seven possible relations between the chariot and its parts: it is not identical with them; it is not different from them; it does not possess them; it is not in them; they are not in it; it is not their mere collection; and it is not their shape.

The added cases close the escape routes that Nah-gah-say-nah's version leaves open. A mind that concedes the chariot is not any single part may still picture it as the owner of the parts, as a container they sit in, or as their arrangement. Chun-drah-keer-tee checks each and finds nothing. Yet he insists, as firmly as Nah-gah-say-nah, that the chariot is not thereby denied: it is established by designation in dependence on its parts, and the world's way of speaking of it is left intact. Wherever this book speaks of the sevenfold search, it is Chun-drah-keer-tee's version that is meant. Nah-gah-say-nah supplied the question; Chun-drah-keer-tee made the answer complete.

# Beyond the Search: Exhaustive Conditionality.

The sevenfold search answers one question: where is the chariot located. Nowhere among the wheels, nowhere apart from them, nowhere possessing or possessed by them, nowhere in their mere collection, nowhere in their shape. But a second question remains, and it deserves to be asked just as carefully, because without it a subtle escape route stays open: what does it take for this event to appear as exactly this event, and not some other, or none at all?

Remove the axle, and there is no diminished chariot. There is no chariot. Remove the rigidity of the wood, and no amount of the other parts, however perfectly arranged, produces a chariot in its place. This is not loose dependency, the way a house depends loosely on the colour it happens to be painted. It is exhaustive dependency — every condition required, in full, for the event to be what it is at all. Change a single condition and what appears is not a variation on the same thing. It is a different thing, or nothing.

This is the same structure I would later find in sound, in sight, in every quale that ever appeared to me. Remove the ear, and there is no sound — not a diminished sound, but no sound whatsoever. Remove the narrow band of frequency a human ear happens to register, and the same vibrating air simply is not the event called hearing. Nothing loosely contributes to these appearances. Everything they depend on is required, completely, for them to be themselves. This is why sight and sound can be so absolutely different from one another, sharing not the faintest residue: each is designated together with its own complete and non-overlapping conditions, none of them prior to it. There was never a shared ingredient standing behind them, waiting to be common to both. Exhaustive conditionality shows only that no shared residue is found; it does not install the conditions as a new explanatory ground.

Once the notion of inherent existence has been exhausted, an insight becomes apparent. The convention does not arise because an independently existing thing receives a name. Nor does dependent functioning first produce an independently existing object that is subsequently designated. Rather, the convention arises together with the dependent functioning itself. There is no object standing behind the convention waiting to be named. The convention is the conceptual articulation of the functioning event itself, not the conceptual representation of an independently existing thing. The chariot is not hidden somewhere beneath its parts, nor is it merely a convenient fiction. It is the conventional expression of a functioning relational configuration. Convention is therefore licensed not by substance but by dependent functioning. Dependent functioning does not refer to an underlying process or hidden mechanism. It simply refers to the conventional coordination through which phenomena mutually establish one another without any participant possessing independent existence. Dependent functioning is itself conventionally designated, not another hidden ontological layer.

This distinction is crucial. Dependent designation does not create conventional reality out of nothing. Nor does it merely attach labels to an already completed world. Rather, dependent designation expresses the very manner in which empty conventional phenomena are conventionally established through their dependent functioning. The designation and the functioning arise together; neither possesses priority over the other. Dependent designation is therefore not fundamentally a theory of naming; language is merely its expression. It is, more fundamentally, the mode by which empty conventional phenomena are conventionally established and articulated.

This distinction transforms the entire meaning of conventional truth. The validity of convention no longer depends upon discovering independently existing entities. Functioning therefore becomes the criterion of conventional validity. Conventional validity does not depend upon correspondence with independently existing objects. It depends upon successful functioning within dependent relations. Convention succeeds because it works, not because it mirrors an underlying ontology. A designation is justified because it successfully participates in the functioning of conventional life. The designation 'medicine' is valid because medicine heals. The designation 'money' is valid because money functions within exchange. The designation 'teacher' is valid because teaching occurs. None of these realities require intrinsic existence. Indeed, they function flawlessly precisely because they are not fixed into self-existing substances. This is the ultimate lesson of Nah-gah-say-nah's dialogue. He is not merely explaining language; he is explaining why an empty world functions perfectly.

Once this dynamic becomes clear, total exertion suddenly acquires a conceptual grammar. Previously, one might have experienced intimacy with reality—where walking simply walked, hearing simply heard, and breathing simply breathed—yet still imagined there must somehow exist independent participants entering into those relationships. Nah-gah-say-nah removes this final assumption. The participants cannot be found prior to the participation. There is no independently existing walker behind walking, no hearer behind hearing, and no thinker behind thinking. Every event possesses exactly the structure of the chariot. In this realization, the taste of total exertion suddenly recognizes itself.

Ordinary thought imagines that relationships connect independently existing entities, assuming that relata exist first and relationships subsequently arise between them. Nah-gah-say-nah quietly overturns this entire architecture, demonstrating that there are no independently existing relata awaiting connection. Relation and relata are designated together; neither is prior. A bell does not first exist independently before participating in sounding; the sounding is already the dependent event. Likewise, the body, the air, the ear, attention, movement, and vibration never first exist as isolated substances before becoming related. They arise conventionally as one dependent functioning. This reveals why intimacy no longer means closeness, but rather the absolute impossibility of independent separation.

This insight is not confined to Nah-gah-say-nah's analysis. It also provides the conceptual grammar for the phenomenology of total exertion described by Doh-gen. Doh-gen's famous description of riding a boat now reveals its full philosophical significance. The rower is not merely inside the boat, nor does the boat merely transport the rower. The rowing, the current, the water, the wind, the oars, the body, and the voyage all mutually establish one another. Nothing exists first; everything is conventionally established through participation. Doh-gen thereby reveals phenomenologically what Nah-gah-say-nah demonstrates analytically.

At this juncture, a reversal occurs. Where one previously believed conventional truth referred to independently existing objects, one now recognizes that conventional truth refers to dependent functioning itself. Convention is therefore neither an illusion nor a rigid ontology. It is neither arbitrary naming nor a hidden substance. It is the functioning dependent event expressed conceptually. This is precisely why conventional truth survives ultimate analysis completely intact. Ultimate analysis removes intrinsic existence, but it does not remove functioning.

The practitioner finally recognizes that Nah-gah-say-nah's purpose was never to prove that the chariot does not exist. His true purpose was to explain why the chariot functions without ever possessing inherent existence. This insight changes the direction of philosophical inquiry completely. The fundamental question is no longer an inquiry into what really exists, or even what the chariot is. The new question becomes: how does an entirely unfindable world function so perfectly?

# No Residue: The Testimony of the Senses.

Long after the chariot had shown me that nothing needed to be found beneath its parts, I noticed the same demonstration running constantly, silently, beneath ordinary perception itself — not something to be reasoned towards, but something already fully present, waiting only to be looked at directly.

Sight, sound, taste, and sensation are not variations on some shared underlying stuff, the way a lump of clay remains clay whether shaped as a ball or a cube. Hearing carries no trace of sight in it — no faint spatial extension, no colour, nothing borrowed. Taste carries nothing of sound. Each is total, complete, exhausted entirely in being exactly what it is, the instant it appears, and gone the instant it is not. If some minute residue of a common substance persisted beneath them — call it awareness, or consciousness, or presence, as I once quietly assumed — it would have to show up somewhere: some shared flavor threading through the otherwise complete difference between what it is like to hear and what it is like to see. I went looking for that thread directly, the way I had once gone looking for the chariot among its wheels. I found nothing. Not a subtle glow, not a background hum, not the faintest carried-over trace.

This absence is not a failure of the search. It is the search succeeding completely, in the most immediate territory available — not a memory of practice, not a state to be entered, but whatever is present in this very moment, simultaneously, in undeniable diversity. Anatta had already shown that no one stands behind any of these appearances, watching them. What this shows, as its quiet completion, is that nothing stands beneath them either, holding them together as expressions of one continuous thing. The observer was already gone. Here, even the substance the observer was once thought to observe turns out to have never been there.

And this absence of residue is precisely what allows the shift between one quale and the next to be so seamless and so total at once, which looks like a contradiction until it is seen clearly. Ordinary change — water growing warmer — is seamless because something persists through it, unbroken, carrying the change along. What happens between hearing and seeing is seamless for the opposite reason. Nothing persists, so there is nothing left to catch, nothing to create friction, nothing to mark a boundary being crossed. The seamlessness is not in spite of there being nothing underneath. It is made of there being nothing underneath.

# The Apple That Cannot Be Found.

Look at an apple, and see what is actually there. Colour, texture, taste, smell, the sense of weight in the hand, and the imputation 'apple' arriving over all of it — not one after another, but so entirely together that no seam can be found between them. There is no moment where colour is present without yet being an apple, no interval in which the taste arrives before the shape does. It is simply there, whole, the instant it is there at all.

Even 'here' and 'now' are not the stage this appearing happens upon. It is tempting to treat space and time as a pre-given container, already waiting, into which the apple's colour and shape are subsequently placed. But 'here' is not available prior to the apple appearing exactly there and nowhere else; 'now' is not available prior to the appearing being immediate rather than remembered. The apple does not appear into a here and a now that were already standing by. Here and now arrive with it, in the same instant, as inseparable from the appearing as the colour is.

Search for what all of this belongs to, and nothing is found. It is not the colour alone, since texture and taste are not colour. It is not the sum of colour, texture, taste, and smell added together, since a mere heap of qualities is not yet anything that could be called an owner of them — something would still be missing to hold them as a single thing rather than a scattering. And nothing beyond these qualities has ever been located either, standing apart from them, waiting to be discovered once the qualities are set aside. The search fails completely, the same way it failed with the chariot, and for the same reason: there was never a hidden bearer to find.

And yet nothing about the apple hesitates while this is true. It does not appear faintly, or provisionally, while some deeper apple stays hidden behind it. It is exactly as vivid, exactly as immediate, exactly as complete as anything could be — and remains, at the very same time, entirely unfindable the moment it is searched for. This is the peculiar character worth sitting with directly, without rushing past it: presence does not wait to be assembled before it appears. It does not build itself up in stages from ingredients gathered beforehand. Conditions and appearing are designated together: the appearing is already there, complete, with nothing prior to it and nothing behind it doing the work of bringing it about. There is no interval in which something is being produced. There is only the enacting itself — sudden, total, without an author.

This is why the old comparisons to a mirage, or the moon's reflection in still water, are so exact. The point of those images was never that the apple is secretly false, a surface hiding some plainer, truer apple underneath if only it could be found. The mirage is not appearance covering a deception. It is what full, vivid appearing looks like when nothing is holding it up from beneath. The apple's vividness and its unfindability are not two separate facts to be reconciled. They are one fact, seen twice — and what makes it feel, quite rightly, as though it borders on magic, is only that it has never needed anything more than the conditions being met to be exactly, completely, undeniably here.

Ask why this enacting happens exactly here, exactly now, and the question cannot be answered — not by a substantialist, and not by one who has already seen dependent arising clearly, though for entirely different reasons. The substantialist cannot answer it because any answer offered — the conditions caused it, the apple's own nature produced the appearance — already assumes a producing power that, as later chapters show, exists nowhere. Their explanation fails because it rests on a premise that does not hold. But even once that premise is gone, the question does not become answerable. It becomes unnecessary. 'Why this, why now' assumes a gap standing between the conditions and the appearing, a gap an explanation could occupy, the same way, as the next sections show, 'where does appearance come from' assumes a location an origin could occupy. Once condition and appearance are seen as arising together rather than one producing the other in sequence, there is no gap left for a why to fill. The question is not left unanswered. It is retired, for lack of anywhere left to stand.

# How Can the Physical Give Rise to the Felt?

For a long time, I carried an unexamined assumption that I never thought to question directly: that the physical and the experiential were two different kinds of thing, so unlike one another that something had to reach across the distance between them for one to give rise to the other. A bell is struck. Air moves. Somehow, impossibly, a hearing occurs. I pictured this as a crossing — as though the vibration had to travel some unimaginable distance, from the register of matter into the register of experience, and I could never locate what carried it across. The gap felt not just difficult to explain, but categorically uncrossable, as though physical and non-physical stood on opposite banks of a canyon a zillion miles wide, with nothing built to span it.

The chariot dismantled this, though it took time before I recognized that it had. The difficulty was never in finding the bridge. The difficulty was in the framework that first placed two separate, self-standing banks on either side of a canyon that was never there. A chariot and its wheels are not two independently complete things later joined by some connecting mechanism; 'wheel' and 'chariot' arise together, as a single dependently designated event, seen from two angles. There is no gap between them for anything to cross, because there were never two finished, independently existing things to begin with. I had been asking how to build a bridge across a canyon that only existed because I had first assumed both banks into being.

The same error, I came to see, had been shaping how I understood the arising of experience itself. The zillion-mile gap between the physical and the felt is not a feature of reality. It is a feature of the inherent framework I was still unconsciously using to look at reality — a framework that insists physical process and felt experience must each first exist complete and separate, in their own right, before any relation between them could even be asked about. Once that framework is seen through, once it becomes clear that nothing anywhere possesses this kind of independent, self-contained existence, the question of how the physical connects to the non-physical stops needing an answer, not because the answer is hidden, but because the two separately existing things the question depends on were never there to be connected. What remains is not a mystery bridged. It is one empty, dependently arisen occurrence, describable in the language of matter from one angle and in the language of feeling from another — with no medium required, because a medium is only ever needed to join what was mistakenly assumed to be apart.

I want to be careful here not to claim more than this insight actually delivers. It does not explain why there is something it is like to hear at all, rather than nothing. What it dissolves is narrower, and I think more fundamental: the demand for a connector, a carrier, a medium spanning an impossible distance. That demand was the confusion. Once it falls away, the appearance of a gap falls away with it — not because it was crossed, but because it was never there to cross.

# Where Does Not Apply.

It would be easy to end this thought by saying that appearance comes from nowhere — and for a long time, that was as far as I could take it. It has the right shape. It sounds sufficiently empty, sufficiently free of substance. But I eventually saw that 'nowhere' was still quietly answering the question of where this comes from, only with a blank instead of a place. The question itself had not been touched. I had negated the source and left the asking of where fully intact, expecting it to be satisfied by an empty answer rather than seeing that the question no longer applied at all.

This is harder to say than it is to see. Appearance does not originate from somewhere, and it does not originate from nowhere either, because both answers still assume that 'originating from a where' is a coherent thing for appearance to do — that there is some location, occupied or vacant, prior to the appearing, from which the appearing sets out. But nothing is prior. Appearance and its arising are not two events, one a location and the other a departure from it. There is no first place — not even an empty one — for anything to leave. 'Where' is a question built for things that exist independently of their designation and then get situated somewhere relative to other things. It was never built to be asked of dependent arising itself, and dependent arising does not owe it an answer, empty or otherwise.

Yet one question immediately arises. If dependent designation explains how conventions are established, why does dependent functioning itself not become another hidden ontology? Why does dependent arising not simply replace substance with process? This is where Nah-gar-joo-nah carries the analysis beyond Nah-gah-say-nah. His project is not merely to demonstrate that entities are empty, but to show that dependent arising itself is free from every ontological commitment. Dependent designation explains why convention functions without substance; Nah-gar-joo-nah will now demonstrate why dependent functioning itself can never become another hidden foundation. The Middle Way therefore culminates not merely in the deconstruction of entities, but in the deconstruction of ontological predication itself. Only then does the complete rehabilitation of the conventional world become possible.

Nah-gar-joo-nah therefore does not overturn Nah-gah-say-nah. He completes him. Having restored the conventional world, he now asks whether dependent arising itself possesses any intrinsic nature. His answer is uncompromising. Even dependent arising cannot stand from its own side. Its very functioning demonstrates that it is empty.
