The Transition from Anatta to Emptiness. A Note on Method.

This work is divided into four parts and an Appendix. Part One is the main text: the sustained argument running from the collapse of the observer through dependent designation, causality, and the resonances and open questions gathered near its close. Part Two, Three Formations, carries that same logic through three contemplative exercises — the mountain, the flower, and space — applying it directly rather than continuing to argue for it, then turns the same search on designation itself through Doh-gen's painted rice cake, and finally on life and death and on the taste the whole view leaves behind. Part Three, Science and the Dharma, is a short survey that sets this argument alongside developments in modern physics and cognitive science, distinguishing resonance from proof throughout; the physics is treated more fully in The Logical Necessity of a Substrateless World. Part Four, Nothing to Find, Nothing Missing, is a shorter, more accessible companion, walking a newer reader through the central argument from the beginning; anyone who prefers to start there can read it first. The Appendix offers formal and linguistic tools for the project — why ordinary grammar keeps reconstructing substance, one proposed notation for avoiding it, and an axiomatic specification of that notation, offered as an experimental shorthand rather than a proof. The logical case on which the whole book rests—that nothing can be coherently posited as existing from its own side—is argued in full in The Logical Necessity of a Substrateless World; this book traces how that case came to be lived, and does not repeat the argument step by step.

This book draws extensively on Nah-gar-joo-nah, Nah-gah-say-nah, Chun-drah-keer-tee, Doh-gen, and the Zog-chen tradition. It is not a translation, a commentary, or a scholarly exposition of any one of them. It is a personal philosophical synthesis, developed in continuity with Mahd-yah-mah-kah and Zog-chen, offered for its own coherence and usefulness rather than as a claim to represent orthodox doctrine.

The view I hold is a rangtong reading of Nah-gar-joo-nah's Moo-lah-mahd-yah-mah-kah-kah-ree-kah: everything, mind included, is empty of its own nature, and that emptiness is a non-implicative negation, which removes what was never there and puts nothing in its place. On luminous clarity I follow Nyingma Zog-chen, which takes the Moo-lah-mahd-yah-mah-kah-kah-ree-kah as its view. Luminosity, in this book, is the vividness of appearing itself, as empty as everything else. It is never an ultimate that is empty of other things but not of its own nature, as the shentong ("empty of other") reading holds.

Five terms and distinctions used throughout this book are my own, not classical divisions found in the source texts, and it matters that this is stated plainly rather than left for the reader to assume otherwise:

Conceptual designation versus dependent designation. Classical Mahd-yah-mah-kah does not separate these into two distinct categories. Mahd-yah-mah-kah's own position is that all designation is dependent designation — there is no legitimate 'conceptual designation' standing apart from it as a rival account of how labels attach to things. The distinction drawn throughout this book, between a 'thing first, label second' sequence and a 'thing and label arising together' structure, is a pedagogical separation of my own, built to make the core insight easier to approach for a reader encountering it for the first time. It is not a division Nah-gar-joo-nah or Chun-drah-keer-tee would recognize as their own, even though the conclusion it leads towards — that all designation is dependent — is entirely theirs.

Designation by relation. The distinction between designation-by-thought and designation-by-relation, and the claim that structural, non-conceptual individuation operates even prior to explicit conceptual labelling, does not appear as a formal division in classical Mahd-yah-mah-kah sources. It is a personal synthesis in continuity with the tradition, not orthodox doctrine.

The logic of the single stroke. The framing of Nah-gah-say-nah's chariot as containing every subsequent insight of this book in miniature — no residue, exhaustive conditionality, no hierarchy or sequence, mutual dependence, full function without essence — is my own organizing device. It is a way of reading the chariot dialogue that I have found illuminating, not a claim about how the dialogue was originally intended or has traditionally been read.

Spontaneous presence equals dependent arising. This book argues, in its later chapters, that Zog-chen's spontaneous presence (lhun grub) and Mahd-yah-mah-kah's dependent arising are the same nature under two descriptions, and that Zog-chen's direct introduction can be understood as the experiential actualization of what Mahd-yah-mah-kah's analysis was always pointing towards. This reading has real support within the tradition — Longchenpa's insistence that the ground is empty, not a subtle existent thing, points in the same direction — but it is not a settled cross-tradition consensus. Other lineages and commentators hold that Zog-chen constitutes its own resultant vehicle, not reducible to or guaranteed by Mahd-yah-mah-kah analysis alone. The equivalence of nature is a position I hold with real confidence, built carefully across this book. That it follows Mahd-yah-mah-kah analysis in a way that makes the two traditions simply two descriptions of one path is my own synthesis of a live and unresolved question, not a report of settled doctrine.

The desynchronization of view, experience, and insight. A central organizing claim of this book is that direct contemplative experience — the taste of anatta, of total exertion — can arrive complete while the conceptual view used to understand it remains, unnoticed, substantialist, and that this mismatch, rather than any deficiency in the experience itself, is what keeps a mind from opening fully. The underlying phenomenon this points to — that realization and view can run at different paces — is recognized in various forms across contemplative traditions. The specific framing of it as a 'desynchronization', and the use of that framing as the engine organizing this entire book from the collapse of the observer through to the final chapters, is my own construction. It is offered because I have found it clarifying and, I believe, accurate to how this process actually unfolds — not because it is a term or a diagnostic framework drawn from any classical source.

None of this diminishes what the book is trying to do. It simply means the book should be read as what it is: one practitioner's attempt to synchronize direct contemplative experience with a rigorously non-substantialist view, built using the tools Mahd-yah-mah-kah and Zog-chen provide, and organized with distinctions of my own devising where the tradition itself does not supply one ready-made. Where a claim is demonstrated by the classical arguments themselves, I have tried to say so. Where a claim is my own extension, reading, or synthesis, I have tried to say that too, both here and at each point in the text where it becomes relevant again.
