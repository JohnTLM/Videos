title: Note on Method
album: The Observer and the Taste of Presence

The Observer and the Taste of Presence. Note on Method.

This book takes a position, and it is important that the reader knows which parts are the author's synthesis and which are the classical material.

The classical material includes the arguments of the Moo-lah-mahd-yah-mah-kah-kah-ree-kah, Chun-drah-keer-tee's reply to Dig-nah-gah, Tsong-kah-pah's account of valid conventions, the Bahiya sutta, and the positions of Mipham, Gorampa and Longchenpa as they are reported in the secondary literature. These are presented as established, and the reader can compare them with the sources.

The synthesis is of eight kinds. The first is the reading of analysis as a response to reification, rather than as a standing activity. The second is the claim that the question "why do appearances spring forth?" is itself a last reification, and that its dissolution is what the Zog-chen phrase "beyond cause and effect" names. The third is the claim that dependent arising and lhun grub, spontaneous presence, are not two interchangeable descriptions, though neither is higher than the other. Any attempt to divide appearing belongs to dependent arising, and when the reifying habit stops, the same appearing is spontaneous presence. The two traditions converge on this. The fourth is the reading of Doh-gen's Gabyō through the concept of designation. The fifth is the reading of the two claims (that analysis leaves no foundation, and that appearances arise when conditions align) as one event described in two ways, with the flame as the book's illustrative device. The sixth is the insubstantial reading of self-appearing: the alignment of conditions is the appearing, and the appearing has no owner. The seventh is the three-assumptions framing of Chapter 17, which is the author's scheme and not one that Mahd-yah-mah-kah presents. The eighth, in Chapter 3, is the claim that the seer-seen split is felt in the body as contraction, and that contraction is felt as a self. That claim is offered for inspection, and readers can test it in their own experience before accepting it. These are the author's own, and they are marked as such where they appear.

The reader should also know that the traditions do not agree on the synthesis. Some Zog-chen lineages hold that the view is its own resultant vehicle, not reducible to Mahd-yah-mah-kah analysis. Gorampa (1429–1489) read the Mahd-yah-mah-kah critique of intrinsic nature more absolutely than Mipham (1846–1912) did. The two never debated: Mipham wrote four centuries later and drew on Gorampa's work. The contrast between their readings is useful because both agree on the danger of reification. The synthesis is that Prah-sahng-gee-kah Mahd-yah-mah-kah, as Chun-drah-keer-tee and Tsong-kah-pah read it, and Nyingma Zog-chen share the view of appearing set out here. Gelug is a school within Prah-sahng-gee-kah, not a third party to the claim. No single tradition has stated this exact formulation, and the claim that the two share a view is the author's synthesis. This book does not decide between Gorampa's and Mipham's readings. It argues that the two traditions converge on undoing the substantialist grasp, and it presents the claim that they share a view of appearing and its emptiness as a reading that can be defended, not as a settled point.

The view itself can be stated in one sentence. It is a rangtong reading of the Moo-lah-mahd-yah-mah-kah-kah-ree-kah: everything, mind included, is empty of its own nature (MMK 24.19), and that emptiness is a non-implicative negation, which removes the fixing and puts nothing in its place. On luminous clarity the book follows Nyingma Zog-chen, which takes the Moo-lah-mahd-yah-mah-kah-kah-ree-kah as its view. Luminosity is the knowing clarity of appearing itself (Chapter 2), as empty as everything else. It is never an ultimate that is empty of other things but not of its own nature, as the shentong ("empty of other") reading holds.

The book's position is best described as a Mahd-yah-mah-kah reading of Zog-chen, not as a plain account of Zog-chen's own self-understanding. The reading belongs to a recognised stream in which Zog-chen is read through Mahd-yah-mah-kah, but it departs from the standard Zog-chen presentation in four places, and the reader should know them before the argument begins.

First, Zog-chen describes a basis (gzhi) from which the path and its fruition unfold, and in some systems it speaks of an unproduced primordial purity (ka dag). Longchenpa describes the ground as a ground only from a relative perspective, empty of being a ground in the true nature of reality. This book follows that line. It rejects any foundation that appearances are produced from or rest on, and reads gzhi as the emptiness of appearing, not as a ground behind it. Where the traditional language of basis is used, the book means this reinterpreted sense.

Second, Zog-chen calls the ground primordially pure and unproduced, which can sound like an uncaused source. This book holds that freedom is not produced and is not a result of causes, but also that it is not an uncaused base. The correction is to the self-description of the tradition, not an interpretation of it, and the book states it as such.

Third, the book uses "openness" in a narrower sense than Zog-chen does. In Zog-chen sources, openness (stong, as in rig stong and gsal stong) is one of the characterisations of the ground, paired with clarity and luminosity, and Longchenpa's ground-awareness is described as an openness that is at once empty and radiant. The book instead treats openness as the way unfixed appearing is seen, not as a property of emptiness or of the ground. Its use of "nature" for emptiness is also narrower than the standard usage of rang bzhin and chos nyid, which the sources sometimes gloss as intrinsic nature. Readers who know the traditional idiom should take both as deliberate terminological choices, defined in the Glossary.

Fourth, the book's chapter on recognition takes a position on a term that Zog-chen treats as decisive. In Zog-chen, knowledge of the basis comes from recognising it, and recognition (ngo shes) is often identified with awakening. The book's chapter title, "Recognition, Not Effort," means only that recognition is not produced by effort or accumulated as a state. It does not deny that recognition is decisive, and the reader should take the title in that sense.

Some Zog-chen authors, Longchenpa among them, state that the ground is empty and not a subtle existent. The book's reading draws on that statement. It does not claim that this reading is the only one available within Zog-chen.

Terms are used in their standard senses where the tradition has one. Where the book uses a term in a sense of its own, it says so in the Glossary.
