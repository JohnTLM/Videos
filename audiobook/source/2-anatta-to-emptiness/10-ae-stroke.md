# The Logic of the Single Stroke & The Eye as a Living Chariot

*The Transition from Anatta to Emptiness* · Part 1

Up to this point, the journey has unfolded through what appears to be a series of distinct insights. It began with the collapse of the observer through anatta, deepened through Dōgen’s total exertion, found its conceptual grammar in Nāgasena’s chariot, was sharpened by Candrakīrti’s sevenfold analysis, and culminated in Nāgārjuna’s dismantling of every ontological foundation. Read sequentially, these movements may appear to be separate teachings or successive philosophical developments. They are not. As the Introduction promised, they are one logic, and only now, after the analytical work has been completed, can that logic be stated directly.

The distinction between conceptual designation and dependent designation, drawn in the chariot chapter, is far more than a theory of language. It determines whether reality is understood as a pre-given world awaiting conceptual articulation, or whether conventional reality itself is dependently articulated. There is no pre-given world—not even a pre-given, ever-changing world. If designation is understood as the attachment of concepts to an already existing thing, or even to an already constituted process of change, then reality has already been granted ontological status prior to designation. Whether conceived as static substance or dynamic process, such a view quietly preserves a subtle substrate upon which designation subsequently operates.

Dependent designation eliminates this possibility entirely. Reality is not first constituted and then described, nor is there first a world of changing processes that concepts subsequently organize. Such accounts merely replace substance with process while leaving untouched the assumption that reality already exists as something determinate before its articulation. Dependent designation therefore prevents the reification of process just as decisively as it prevents the reification of substance. Convention is not the conceptual labelling of a pre-existing world; it is the simultaneous articulation of dependent arising itself. Conventional reality is therefore neither discovered as an independently existing world nor constructed by conceptual thought. It is dependently articulated through relational functioning without ontological remainder.

This is why Candrakīrti’s sevenfold analysis is so radical. It does more than eliminate independently existing entities; it eliminates the very possibility of treating reality as a pre-given basis awaiting designation. The usual interpretation imagines two moments: first there is the basis, and then there is the designation. Yet if this were so, the basis would already constitute a determinate world prior to dependent designation. The sevenfold analysis leaves no room for such a world. The chariot is never assembled first and designated later; it is already one indivisible articulation of dependent arising.

The standard interpretive framework proceeds in two stages: first, there are wheels, an axle, a frame, and other parts; then the mind conceptually designates them as a 'chariot'. While this appears to preserve conventionality after intrinsic existence has been denied, it quietly assumes that a determinate basis already exists prior to designation. This work rejects that interpretation. There is no independently existing collection of parts waiting to become a chariot, nor is there first an objective process that concepts later organize. Such accounts merely relocate ontology from substance to aggregation or process.

The chariot is already a single stroke. Its parts, their relations, their intelligibility, their function, and their designation do not arise in sequence; they are simultaneous aspects of one dependently arisen event. Even this requires careful attention. A wheel is already a dependent designation, identified only through its relations, functions, contrasts, and conditions. It is not a self-existing primitive from which larger wholes are subsequently built. The same is true of the axle, the frame, every so-called part, and ultimately the chariot itself. Every part is already a conventional articulation of dependent arising.

Wheels are therefore not first wheels in isolation that later become parts of a chariot. Their very identity as wheels, axle, or frame is already articulated within a relational whole. Likewise, the term 'chariot' is not an additional concept imposed upon this whole; it is the conventional articulation of that very relational functioning. Dependent designation is therefore not the conceptual naming of an already constituted object. It is the simultaneous articulation of dependent arising itself.

When the chariot is sought as something existing apart from, identical with, possessing, or containing its parts, nothing can be found. What remains is not an independently existing aggregate subsequently labelled a chariot; nothing is left over as a remainder; the chariot simply functions, empty and conventionally intelligible, never having required an underlying bearer in the first place. Seen in this way, the chariot is not merely an illustration of dependent designation. It is already the complete logic. Nothing exists prior to the event—not the parts, not the whole, not the designation, not even the conventional world in which the chariot supposedly resides. The chariot is simply one indivisible articulation of dependent arising in which emptiness, designation, conventional validity, and functioning are inseparable.

## The Central Theorem

The entire argument of this work may now be stated in a single proposition: dependent designation is not the conceptual labelling of a pre-existing world, but the simultaneous articulation of dependent arising itself. Everything established thus far follows from this theorem, and everything that follows depends upon it.

If dependent designation is simultaneous with dependent arising, then there can be no pre-given world awaiting conceptual articulation. 'Pre-given' here means already carved and existing from its own side—a world whose edges and identities are drawn before any designation—not merely a world that has not yet been named. Conventionally, the mountain is there before anyone names it, as Part 2 insists; what is denied is a mountain whose boundary and oneness come with it independently of designation. The distinction is argued in full in The Logical Necessity of a Substrateless World (section 2.6). Without a pre-given world, there can be no ontological priority. And if there is no ontological priority, there can be no independently existing subject, object, substance, process, relation, or convention existing before the event itself. Conventional reality is therefore not constructed upon an underlying world; it is the intelligibility of dependent arising itself. It is only after this theorem has been recognized that the logic implicit throughout the preceding chapters can be seen in its entirety, a framework termed here the Logic of the Single Stroke.

It is 'single' because nothing is ontologically prior. Within this view, there is no inside and outside, no subject and object, no observer and observed, no ground and manifestation, no process preceding designation, no hierarchy, and no sequence. Nothing first exists so that something else may subsequently arise; everything is expressed in one indivisible stroke.

Within this single stroke, emptiness, dependent arising, dependent designation, conventional validity, causal efficacy, and the two truths are not successive stages. They are inseparable aspects of one appearance viewed from different analytical perspectives. The distinctions remain indispensable for explanation, but they do not describe the architecture of reality itself. Reality is not layered; it is one stroke—and the single stroke, too, is empty. The phrase is shorthand for the absence of anything prior, not a description of what reality is: it says that nothing stands first, and says nothing about what reality is made of.

## The Eye as a Living Chariot

Nothing appears more ordinary, more undeniably physical, or more unquestionably real than the organ of vision. Yet precisely because it seems so self-evident, it provides the ideal ground to observe how substantialist thinking quietly reconstructs an independently existing world. The eye becomes a living chariot.

Ordinarily, human perception operates under the assumption that a single, persistent physical eye exists. Sometimes it sees; sometimes it closes; sometimes it sleeps; sometimes it meditates; sometimes it undergoes surgical alteration. Throughout these changing activities, common sense posits that one identical physical object persists beneath them. Even after abandoning belief in an enduring, substantial self, many contemplative practitioners quietly preserve this physical assumption. They readily concede that 'eye' is merely a conventional designation, while implicitly maintaining that the biological organ itself remains exactly the same underneath its various activities. This assumption appears harmless, yet it is simply substantialism wearing biological clothing.

The first transformation of this assumption occurs through the lens of anatta. When one searches rigorously for the seer behind vision, none can be located. Seeing continues; vision functions flawlessly. Yet nowhere within or behind the activity can an independently existing observer be found. The eye is no longer understood as an instrument employed by an internal subject. There is simply the act of seeing. Anatta effectively removes the observer, yet a subtle reification often survives: the eye itself as a discrete, objective entity.

The second transformation unfolds through the insight of Dōgen. Here, the eye no longer appears as an independently existing object that produces vision. Rather, the eye completely spends itself in being this act of seeing. Seeing is not a function performed by an eye; the eye is the very event of seeing itself. Nothing remains behind it, nothing directs it, and nothing possesses it. The event completely exhausts itself in being exactly what it is—an act of total exertion.

Nāgasena’s classic inquiry regarding the chariot applies directly to the eye. Where is this eye located? Is it identical with the retina? The optic nerve? The blood vessels? The ocular muscles? The lens? The cornea? The neural pathways? The visual cortex? Is it the collection of these parts, or is it something distinct from them? Under rigorous analysis, every option collapses. Exactly as with Nāgasena’s chariot, the eye cannot be isolated as an intrinsically existing entity. Yet seeing continues unimpeded.

At this juncture, a subtle misunderstanding frequently arises. It is easy to conclude that only the concept of the eye has changed, while the physical eye itself remains an enduring substrate. This is the final refuge of physical realism. The non-substantialist view goes considerably further. The eye that sees is not merely conceptually different from the eye in meditation; it is physically different as well. The eye participating in the act of seeing is designated together with a unique configuration of neural activity, muscular tension, vascular flow, hormonal balance, posture, environmental conditions, illumination, and attention. When meditation occurs, every one of these constituent conditions shifts. Consequently, the physical event shifts alongside the conceptual designation. They shift together because both are dependently arisen.

Candrakīrti’s sevenfold reasoning ceases to be an abstract exercise when applied directly to the physical organ. The eye is not identical to its parts, nor is it separate from them. It does not possess its parts; it is not in them, nor are they in it. It is not merely the aggregate of its parts, nor their shape. This analysis does not dismantle the physical eye; it dismantles only the unexamined assumption that the eye ever possessed intrinsic essence.

Nāgārjuna introduces the ultimate analytical question: If the eye is dependently arisen, what is the status of dependent arising itself? Can dependent arising be elevated to the status of an ultimate physical reality? It cannot. Dependent arising itself is empty of intrinsic existence. Otherwise, the impulse towards reification merely shifts its ground once more. The eye is empty, seeing is empty, the physical conditions are empty, the conceptual designations are empty, and even the framework of dependence is empty. Nothing can be established as an ultimate, self-grounded foundation, yet nothing loses its functional capacity.

Functioning has never depended upon intrinsic essence. The eye is not unique; it merely illuminates the architecture of every phenomenon. The hand writing is not identical to the hand grasping. The body walking is not the body sleeping. The mountain today is not the mountain of yesterday. What is called 'the world' is fresh dependent participation, designated together with its present functioning, not composed of anything.
