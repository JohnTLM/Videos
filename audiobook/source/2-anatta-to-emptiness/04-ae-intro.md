# Introduction: The Architecture of a Direct Realization

*The Transition from Anatta to Emptiness* · Part 1

This book is neither a historical survey of Buddhist philosophy nor a commentary upon any single tradition. It is an attempt to articulate the logical architecture implicit in a direct realization.

The journey began with the collapse of the observer through the realization of anatta. When no seer could be found behind seeing, no hearer behind hearing, no thinker behind thinking, and no self behind experience, the familiar substantialist picture of reality dissolved. Yet this was only the beginning.

The realization deepened through what Dōgen calls total exertion. Reality no longer appeared as subjects interacting with objects, nor as an underlying ground manifesting phenomena. Every event completely exhausted itself in being precisely that event. Nothing stood behind or outside the immediacy of experience. At that stage, however, the realization remained largely experiential. Its logical implications had not yet been fully unfolded.

The encounter with Nāgasena's chariot revealed that the same insight extended beyond the self. The chariot was not merely an illustration of personal identity; it exposed the structure of all conventional reality. What we call objects are not independently existing entities that later receive names. Conventionality itself required rethinking. Candrakīrti's sevenfold analysis sharpened this insight. It demonstrated that no independently existing bearer could be found—not identical to the parts, not other than the parts, not possessing the parts, not in them nor containing them, not their mere collection, not their shape. Yet conventional functioning remained completely intact.

Finally, Nāgārjuna's Mūlamadhyamakakārikā revealed that this logic was universal. The same analysis dismantled not only persons and objects, but causes and effects, motion and rest, time and change, agent and action, arising and ceasing. Every attempt to establish an ontological foundation dissolved under examination. The question then became unavoidable. If all substantialist assumptions are relinquished, what remains? This book is an attempt to answer that question.

Its central thesis is that the insights of anatta, total exertion, Nāgasena, Candrakīrti, and Nāgārjuna are not isolated teachings but different expressions of one coherent non-substantialist logic. Rather than presenting them separately, this work reconstructs their implicit architecture into a single systematic framework.

I call this the Logic of the Single Stroke. It is stated in full only once the analysis has earned it, in the chapter of that name; here it is enough to say that nothing in it is ontologically prior to anything else.

Although expressed as a formal philosophical system, the Logic of the Single Stroke is not an abstraction imposed upon experience. It is an attempt to articulate the immediate actuality of spontaneous presence without allowing substantialist habits of thought to re-enter through language, ontology, or conceptual sequence. The purpose of this book is therefore neither to invent a new doctrine nor to replace the classical masters. It is to make explicit the logical unity implicit within their insights and to demonstrate that the realization of dependent arising is not only directly knowable but also philosophically coherent. The chapters that follow are not separate investigations. They are successive clarifications of one indivisible realization until the entire architecture becomes transparent in a single stroke.
