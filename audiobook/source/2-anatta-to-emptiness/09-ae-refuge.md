# The Last Refuge of Ontology: Dependent Arising Dismantles Itself

*The Transition from Anatta to Emptiness* · Part 1

Before encountering the insights of Nāgasena, the practitioner typically operates under the assumption that conventional reality consists of independently existing things to which language merely attaches names. Through the analysis of the chariot, however, this assumption quietly collapses. Convention is no longer understood as a label imposed upon an independently existing object, but as the conceptual articulation of dependent functioning itself. The ordinary world does not disappear; rather, it is completely reinterpreted.

Yet, this very success gives rise to a deeper philosophical question. If conventional reality is now understood through dependent functioning, what exactly is the ontological status of dependent functioning itself? One must ask whether substantialism has truly disappeared, or if it has merely migrated. At this juncture, the practitioner stands at one of the most subtle turning points of the entire contemplative journey.

## The Last Foundation

At first glance, dependent functioning appears to solve every philosophical problem. There are no independently existing selves, objects, causes, or wholes; everything functions dependently. This appears to complete the Middle Way. Yet, careful reflection reveals a danger. Having abandoned independently existing substances, the mind quietly begins treating dependent functioning itself as an objectively existing process. Static ontology is thus transformed into dynamic ontology.

## Substantialism Migrates Again: From Things to Process

The subtlety of this transition explains why many mature practitioners unknowingly stop short of the Middle Way. Having dismantled independently existing things, they naturally begin speaking of dependent processes, relational events, dynamic networks, or interconnected becoming. These descriptions appear entirely compatible with emptiness, for nothing is said to exist independently.

Yet the substantialist habit has not disappeared. It has merely migrated.

Previously, one imagined independently existing things interacting with one another. Now, one imagines independently existing relations connecting things, or independently existing processes unfolding beneath appearances. Static ontology quietly becomes process ontology. Substance becomes relation. Being becomes becoming.

The language has changed. The ontological instinct has not.

This explains why many philosophical systems appear to approach Madhyamaka while never fully arriving there. Process philosophy, relational ontology, dynamic holism, systems thinking, and even certain interpretations of dependent arising all successfully abandon static substances while quietly preserving another form of ontological establishment. They remain committed to the reality of process itself.

Nāgārjuna’s critique is considerably more radical. He asks whether process itself can withstand ultimate analysis.

## Why the Mind Cannot Stop Reifying

The persistence of reification is not primarily a philosophical problem; it is a cognitive habit. The mind has been conditioned since infancy to understand reality through independently existing nouns. When those nouns collapse under analysis, the same habit immediately searches for another foundation. If objects cannot exist independently, perhaps relations do. If relations fail, perhaps processes. If processes become unstable, perhaps awareness itself. The target changes, but the instinct remains the same.

This explains why substantialism is so resilient. It is not committed to any particular ontology. It is committed to ontology itself. Whatever appears most fundamental becomes the new object of reification. The mind is not attached to substances; it is attached to the existence of something that can finally stand on its own.

## Why Phenomenology Alone Cannot Complete the Journey

This subtle shift also explains an important feature of contemplative development. Following the realization of anatta, total exertion reveals an intimacy in which every event completely manifests itself. Nothing stands outside the event. Nothing observes it from elsewhere.

Yet this phenomenological transformation does not automatically determine how the event is understood conceptually. One may correctly experience intimacy while still interpreting that intimacy through the language of process, relation, or becoming. Experience has changed; ontology has merely become more refined.

The task is no longer to transform experience, but to transform the ontology silently projected onto that experience. The phenomenology is already liberated. The ontology is not.

Dependent functioning is subtly reified into another hidden foundation. The practitioner no longer searches for substances, but they are still searching for a foundational process.

## The Convergence of the Three Gateways

At this precise point, the three great gateways converge. Each gateway has now reached its natural limit. Anatta has dismantled the observer. Total exertion has dismantled separation. Nāgasena has dismantled the view of convention as labels on independently existing objects.

None of these, however, asks the final question: does dependent functioning itself possess ontological status?

Only Nāgārjuna asks that question. Confronting this final possibility is the unique domain of the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, which systematically examines whether dependent arising itself can withstand ultimate analysis. The answer is unequivocal: it cannot.

## The Completion of the Middle Way

This realization completely changes the meaning of emptiness. Emptiness is no longer understood merely as the absence of independently existing entities, nor is dependent arising understood as the final, foundational explanation of reality. Instead, dependent arising itself becomes the final object of analysis.

This is Nāgārjuna’s reversal. Dependent arising is initially introduced to undermine independently existing entities. Yet precisely because dependent arising functions as an analytical tool, it must eventually turn upon itself. If it remained exempt from analysis, it would become the very foundation that all previous analysis had sought to avoid. The Middle Way would quietly terminate in another metaphysical principle. The analysis must therefore turn upon dependent arising itself. Nothing less will suffice.

Dependent arising dismantles itself not because it fails as an explanation of conventional functioning, but because its explanatory success does not grant it ontological privilege. The very principle that dismantles intrinsic existence cannot itself become intrinsically established. Otherwise, the Middle Way would terminate precisely where it intended to free itself.

The Middle Way therefore reaches completion only when dependent arising dismantles its own ontological status while conventional functioning continues undiminished.

## Beyond Nihilism

At this stage, it is common to assume that Nāgārjuna has destroyed the conventional world. The opposite is true. By refusing to reify dependent arising itself, Nāgārjuna prevents the practitioner from replacing one ontology with another. Substances do not become processes, processes do not become relations, and relations do not become hidden structures. Every ontological refuge quietly exhausts itself. What remains is not nihilism, but the complete freedom of conventional functioning.

## The Final Reversal

Even with Nāgārjuna's analysis complete, one final question remains. If neither objects, nor relations, nor dependent functioning, nor dependent arising possess intrinsic existence, why does conventional reality continue functioning with such precision? Why does medicine still heal, language still communicate, and compassion still relieve suffering? Why does the world not collapse into nothingness?

The answer requires one final reversal. Dependent arising must no longer be understood merely as dependence, but as the complete exhaustion of every ontological commitment. Yet this exhaustion is not merely negative. When every attempt to establish an underlying foundation has finally been relinquished, something else becomes apparent. Reality has not become less vivid. It has become immeasurably lighter. Nothing now remains fixed, constrained, or burdened by intrinsic existence. Appearances lack any inherent substrate, and, met without fixing, they are seen to be already open, non-abiding, effortless, infinitely responsive, and inexhaustibly capable of manifestation—not as a positive remainder left after negation, but as the way appearing was never otherwise, as the clarification below insists.

## The Exhaustion of Ontological Predication

The opening chapter of the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā asks what it is that arises. Take an illustration in its spirit: when asked what occurs when a bell is struck, ordinary language immediately answers, 'The sound'. Yet, finding the precise location of this sound proves impossible. It cannot be found independently inside the bell, the striker, the air, the ear, or consciousness. The search for an isolated, inherent entity never succeeds. In revealing this, Nāgārjuna dismantles not merely the concept of the sound, but the underlying assumption that independently existing nouns form the basis of experience. The noun survives solely as a conventional designation; its ontology does not.

As the analysis deepens, it moves from the object to the event. If the noun cannot be found, one must ask whether the verb possesses independent reality. Birth, for instance, appears to mark the definitive beginning of life. Yet, this very same event equally marks the beginning of death, as every moment of life is simultaneously a moment of dying. When asked which event actually occurred independently, the answer must be neither. The same appearance is simply designated differently according to conventional purpose. Therefore, the examination of birth dismantles the verb just as thoroughly as the noun. Arising and ceasing are revealed not as objective, standalone events, but as dependent designations.

At this juncture, another shift occurs. Ordinary realism exhausts its grammar, stripped of both independently existing nouns and verbs. Yet, the habit of reification seeks one final refuge. The mind assumes that even if things and actions lack inherent existence, perhaps the overarching process itself exists. It posits that dependence, relation, or causation must possess some fundamental, objective reality. It is towards these structural assumptions that Nāgārjuna turns his ultimate analysis.

The decisive shift is not merely the recognition that dependent arising is empty, but the realization that dependent arising is already free from elaboration. This distinction fundamentally transforms the nature of the inquiry. To state that dependent arising is empty is to negate intrinsic existence. To recognize that dependent arising is already free from elaboration, however, is to recognize dependent arising as it is once every ontological projection has been relinquished.

This insight unfolds through a rigorous exhaustion of ontology. The analysis leaves nothing standing. The intrinsic observer dissolves through anatta. Independently existing nouns dissolve under scrutiny, verbs cease to denote objectively existing events, and processes lose their status as hidden realities. Finally, dependent arising itself is released from ontological privilege. A common unease arises at this stage: if dependent arising dismantles itself, what remains? The answer is exactly what has always remained. Throughout this complete exhaustion of inherent existence, conventional functioning remains entirely intact. The bell still rings. Medicine still heals. Language still communicates. Compassion continues to relieve suffering. Convention survives intact. Consequently, Madhyamaka is not a philosophy of nihilism or destruction, but one of liberation.

This uninterrupted functioning reveals the true significance of non-implicative negation. One of the most persistent misunderstandings of Madhyamaka is the assumption that the exhaustion of ontology leaves behind only absence. Nāgārjuna does not dismantle ontology to reveal a void opposed to appearance. Negation neither produces openness nor creates a new reality; it simply removes the projections that prevented dependent arising from being recognized exactly as it has always been.

At this conceptual threshold, the meaning of niṣprapañca changes entirely. Freedom from elaboration can no longer be understood as merely a temporary meditative suspension of conceptuality, nor as belonging exclusively to a non-conceptual spontaneous presence. Rather, it is recognized as the very nature of dependent arising itself. Dependent arising has never possessed the elaborations projected upon it. It has never been an objectively existing process, a hidden causal mechanism, or an ontological structure awaiting discovery. Its emptiness and its functioning have always been inseparable. Neither grounds the other. Emptiness does not produce functioning, and functioning does not prove an underlying reality. They are simply two inseparable aspects of the same empty dependent arising.

At this juncture, a critical clarification is necessary. The openness, effortlessness, responsiveness, non-abiding nature, and inexhaustible capacity for the manifestation of appearances must not be misunderstood as positive realities inferred from emptiness. Such an interpretation would convert a non-implicative negation into an implicative one. Nothing is added to dependent arising through analysis, nor does negation uncover a hidden metaphysical ground. These qualities are not consequences produced by emptiness; they are simply the manner in which dependent arising has always functioned once the false attribution of intrinsic existence has been abandoned. Non-implicative negation removes only the mistaken projection. It neither subtracts from appearances nor adds anything to them. The openness of dependent arising is therefore not a new conclusion reached after emptiness, but the direct recognition of what was never absent.

Reality, in this light, does not become emptier; it is recognized as having always been light. While nothing vanishes and everything continues to function, no event remains burdened by intrinsic existence. No phenomenon carries ontological weight. The world is no longer perceived as being imprisoned within static things, dynamic processes, or abstract relations. As the burden of inherentness quietly falls away, it becomes clear that conventional reality never required liberation from ontology. Only conceptual thought required liberation.

Ultimately, analytical deconstruction does not create a new reality. It merely removes the ontological projections that prevented dependent arising from being recognized as already primordially open, non-abiding, effortless, and free from elaboration.

The exhaustion of ontology does not culminate in a new vision of reality; it culminates in the cessation of every demand that reality conform to an ontology at all. Reality has not become open. It is recognized as open because the demand that it conform to an intrinsic ontological structure has finally ceased. Dependent arising never required rehabilitation; only ontology did.

This recognition is achieved not by adding a metaphysical ground after negation, but by recognizing what the removal of ontological projection has disclosed all along. Analysis carries the inquiry to the threshold. Prajñā, which exhausts every ontological elaboration, then completes itself as ye shes, which directly recognizes the primordially open, non-abiding, effortless, and vividly functioning character of dependent arising. The work of analysis is therefore fulfilled not by discovering another reality, but by ceasing to obscure the reality that had never required ontological support in the first place.

## An Unaccountable World

I had expected emptiness to feel like a subtraction — as though the world would grow thinner, more provisional, less vivid, once its hidden foundations were shown to be absent. The opposite occurred. Nothing in the world had ever been resting on those foundations to begin with, so nothing was lost when they were seen through. What remained was the same world, exactly as vivid, exactly as functional — and yet no longer explicable in the old way, because the old way of explaining had always secretly relied on a where, an origin, a something-first that could be pointed to and named as the source. With that gone, appearance no longer arrives as the output of a process. It simply arrives, complete, the way a rabbit appears from a hat only in the story we tell about it having been hidden there all along — except here, there was never a hat, never a hiding place, never a trick performed offstage. There is only the appearing.

This is why the chariot cannot be the end of the matter, only the beginning of it. The chariot teaches the mind to stop demanding a bearer. But once that lesson is taken seriously and followed all the way down — through causality, through qualia, through the very question of where appearance originates — it does not leave the world merely explained. It leaves the world unaccountable, in the most precise and wonderful sense of that word: no longer answerable to an account, no longer owing its appearing to some prior thing that produced it. Every sound arrives without having travelled from anywhere. Every sight is complete without having been carried across any distance to reach completion. This is not a flaw in the world, discovered late. It is what was true the entire time, obscured only by a grammar that insisted everything must come from somewhere before it could be here.

This is the sense in which the logic is not merely different, but overturning. It does not offer a better explanation of how appearances originate. It shows that the demand for an origin was itself the illusion, and once that demand falls silent, what is left standing is not a diminished world but an entirely unaccountable one — arising, vivid, functioning perfectly, owing its existence to nowhere, and needing to owe it to nothing.

## Beyond Cause and Effect: Why Spontaneous Presence Is Not a Hidden Ground

It would be easy, having come this far, to reach for one final consolation: that the apple's enacting, appearing whole and vivid the instant conditions align, must at least point towards something — some spontaneous source, uncaused and prior, quietly producing the appearing from a place analysis cannot reach. This is the last and subtlest place substantialism can hide, precisely because it no longer looks like substance. It wears the most beautiful word available to it. It calls itself spontaneous presence.

This temptation deserves to be named and refused as carefully as every other refuge already examined. A substrate does not explain why appearances spring forth; it only relocates the question beneath a more sophisticated name, exactly as an underlying structure, a field, or an informational process each perform the same relocation without resolving anything. The mystery is never removed by this move. It is only renamed and pushed one step further back, waiting to be asked again. If spontaneous presence were offered as such a substrate — an inexplicable ground standing behind the apple's appearing, uncaused, prior, quietly producing what shows up — it would not be beyond the demand for a producer. It would be the demand for a producer, granted one final, unquestionable exemption from having to justify itself.

This is not what beyond cause and effect means. Three assumptions will be examined in detail in the chapter on the principle of formation: that appearance requires an intrinsic essence, that functioning requires an intrinsic causal power, and that the coherent alignment of conditions requires a deeper ontological foundation beneath it. A fourth assumption survives even after these three are gone, and it is the most difficult to catch because it no longer asks what produces appearances. It asks only why they spring forth at all, as though the coherent alignment of conditions were still an incomplete answer, still owed some further, final justification. This is the last spell. It no longer searches for essence, or power, or substrate. It searches only for permission to stop searching, and mistakes spontaneous presence for the ground that finally grants it.

Beyond cause and effect does not mean beyond dependent arising, and it does not mean the discovery of an exception to it. It means beyond intrinsic cause and intrinsic effect — beyond the requirement that appearing must be produced by something standing prior to it, whether that something is called a self, a power, a substrate, or, dressed most convincingly of all, a spontaneous ground. The apple does not appear because some spontaneous presence beneath it has caused it to. It appears together with its conditions, conditions and appearing designated at once, and nothing further is required for that appearing to be complete — not because the requirement has been secretly satisfied by something hidden, but because the requirement itself, examined all the way to its final and subtlest form, turns out to have never applied.

This is what allows the word spontaneous to be used honestly rather than as a disguise. It does not name a positive metaphysical claim about an uncaused source. It names, descriptively, how appearing was never otherwise, once every place the mind might attempt to stand has already been exhausted — essence gone, causal power gone, substrate gone, and finally the demand for an explanation of the demand itself gone. Prajñā does this work by removing, one by one, every location an explanation could occupy. Ye shes is not a further discovery once that work is finished. It is simply the recognition that nothing more has ever been required. The apple was never waiting on an explanation to be complete. It was already, from the very beginning, exactly what spontaneous presence has always meant: appearing with nothing behind it to be found, because nothing behind it was ever needed.
