# Examination of the Twelve Links

*Nāgārjuna's Middle Way* · Chapter 26

Why does suffering keep coming back? We get what we wanted and want something else. We lose what we loved and grieve. One life, the tradition says, leads to another in the same way. The Buddha's answer was a chain of twelve links: from confusion to action, from action to a new life, from contact to feeling, from feeling to craving, and from craving to birth, ageing and death. This chapter sets out that chain.

It is the one chapter of the work with no refutation in it. After twenty-five chapters showing that nothing is found standing on its own, Nāgārjuna explains how things work. That is the point. Emptiness does not stop the world working; a world of own-natures could not work at all ([Chapter 24](https://atr-passerby.com/nagarjuna-24-examination-of-the-four-noble-truths/)). Dependent arising, the subject of the homage in [Chapter 1](https://atr-passerby.com/nagarjuna-1-examination-of-conditions/), is here shown doing its everyday job: producing suffering when ignorance is present, and ending it when ignorance ends.

## The verses

The translation is new, made from the Sanskrit for this site. The Sanskrit is given above each verse; it follows the [GRETIL](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/) e-text (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0).

#### Verse 26.1

> *punarbhavāya saṃskārān avidyānivṛtas tridhā \|  
> abhisaṃskurute yāṃs tair gatiṃ gacchati karmabhiḥ \|\|*

Covered over by ignorance, one makes the three kinds of formations that lead to rebirth. Through these actions one goes to a new destiny.

#### Verse 26.2

> *vijñānaṃ saṃniviśate saṃskārapratyayaṃ gatau \|  
> saṃniviṣṭe 'tha vijñāne nāmarūpaṃ niṣicyate \|\|*

With formations as its condition, consciousness enters a new destiny. Once consciousness has entered, name-and-form takes shape.

#### Verse 26.3

> *niṣikte nāmarūpe tu ṣaḍāyatanasaṃbhavaḥ \|  
> ṣaḍāyatanam āgamya saṃsparśaḥ saṃpravartate \|\|*

When name-and-form has taken shape, the six sense fields come about. Depending on the six sense fields, contact arises.

#### Verse 26.4

> *cakṣuḥ pratītya rūpaṃ ca samanvāhāram eva ca \|  
> nāmarūpaṃ pratītyaivaṃ vijñānaṃ saṃpravartate \|\|*

Depending on the eye, on visible form and on attention, that is, depending on name-and-form, consciousness arises.

#### Verse 26.5

> *saṃnipātas trayāṇāṃ yo rūpavijñānacakṣuṣām \|  
> sparśaḥ sa tasmāt sparśāc ca vedanā saṃpravartate \|\|*

The coming together of the three, form, consciousness and eye, is contact. And from contact, feeling arises.

#### Verse 26.6

> *vedanāpratyayā tṛṣṇā vedanārthaṃ hi tṛṣyate \|  
> tṛṣyamāṇa upādānam upādatte caturvidham \|\|*

With feeling as condition there is craving, for one craves for the sake of feeling. Craving, one takes up the four kinds of grasping.

#### Verse 26.7

> *upādāne sati bhava upādātuḥ pravartate \|  
> syād dhi yady anupādāno mucyeta na bhaved bhavaḥ \|\|*

When there is grasping, becoming arises for the one who grasps. If one did not grasp, one would be freed, and there would be no becoming.

#### Verse 26.8

> *pañca skandhāḥ sa ca bhavo bhavāj jātiḥ pravartate \|  
> jarāmaraṇaduḥkhādi śokāḥ saparidevanāḥ \|\|*

That becoming is the five aggregates. From becoming, birth arises; and ageing, death, suffering and the rest, sorrow with lamentation,

#### Verse 26.9

> *daurmanasyam upāyāsā jāter etat pravartate \|  
> kevalasyaivam etasya duḥkhaskandhasya saṃbhavaḥ \|\|*

distress and despair: all this arises from birth. In this way this whole mass of suffering comes to be.

#### Verse 26.10

> *saṃsāramūlaṃ saṃskārān avidvān saṃskaroty ataḥ \|  
> avidvān kārakas tasmān na vidvāṃs tattvadarśanāt \|\|*

So the one who does not know makes the formations that are the root of saṃsāra. The one who does not know is the maker; the one who knows is not, because they see how things are.

#### Verse 26.11

> *avidyāyāṃ niruddhāyāṃ saṃskārāṇām asaṃbhavaḥ \|  
> avidyāyā nirodhas tu jñānasyāsyaiva bhāvanāt \|\|*

When ignorance has ceased, formations do not arise. And ignorance ceases through cultivating this very knowledge.

#### Verse 26.12

> *tasya tasya nirodhena tat tan nābhipravartate \|  
> duḥkhaskandhaḥ kevalo 'yam evaṃ samyag nirudhyate \|\|*

By the ceasing of each link, the next does not arise. In this way this whole mass of suffering rightly comes to an end.

## The twelve links

The twelve links (dvādaśāṅga pratītyasamutpāda) are the Buddha's own map of how suffering keeps itself going. The chapter walks round them in order:

1.  **Ignorance** (avidyā): not seeing how things are; taking them to exist from their own side.
2.  **Formations** (saṃskāra): actions of body, speech and mind shaped by that ignorance.
3.  **Consciousness** (vijñāna), which those actions carry into a new life.
4.  **Name-and-form** (nāmarūpa): the mind and body of that life.
5.  **The six sense fields** (ṣaḍāyatana): eye, ear, nose, tongue, body and mind.
6.  **Contact** (sparśa): sense, object and consciousness meeting.
7.  **Feeling** (vedanā): pleasant, unpleasant or neither.
8.  **Craving** (tṛṣṇā): wanting more of the pleasant and less of the unpleasant.
9.  **Grasping** (upādāna): holding on.
10. **Becoming** (bhava): the build-up toward a new existence.
11. **Birth** (jāti).
12. **Ageing and death** (jarāmaraṇa), with all their sorrow.

![Diagram 1](../../figures/mmk-26-diagram-1.svg)

The wheel of twelve links. Ignorance, craving and grasping (filled) keep it turning; the rest follow from them. Verse 26.11 places the cut at the start: where ignorance ends, formations no longer arise, and the wheel stops.

The links are often read over three lives: ignorance and formations in a past life; consciousness through becoming in this one; birth, ageing and death in the next. But the same chain can be seen in a single afternoon. Confusion shapes an action; the action shapes how the next moment is met; something is seen, a feeling comes, craving follows, and the grip of 'I must have this' gives birth to a new little world of worry, which grows old and dies. Both readings are traditional.

## From ignorance to a new life

The chain starts with ignorance (26.1). Covered over by it, a person makes 'formations' in three ways, through body, speech and mind. These are not just deeds done and gone. They shape a direction, the way a river cuts its bed, and the next life flows along that bed.

Consciousness, conditioned by formations, 'enters' the new destiny (26.2). This sounds like a traveller moving house. It is not meant that way. Nothing passes over; the old flame lights a new one, as Nāgasena said of rebirth. Once consciousness has entered, name-and-form, the mind and body of the new life, takes shape, and with it the six sense fields (26.3).

## How experience is built

Verses 26.4–5 slow down at the moment that matters most for practice: contact. Seeing does not happen inside a finished eye looking at a finished object. Visual consciousness arises in dependence on the eye, on visible form, and on attention (26.4). The verse sums these up as 'name-and-form': eye and visible form are the 'form' side, attention the 'name' (mental) side. The meeting of the three, eye, form and consciousness, is called contact. From contact comes feeling.

![Diagram 2](../../figures/mmk-26-diagram-2.svg)

Verses 26.4–6. Seeing arises from a meeting, not from a self looking out. From contact comes feeling; from feeling, craving; from craving, grasping. The step from feeling to craving is where a practitioner can watch the chain at work.

Then craving (26.6). We crave for the sake of feeling: more of the pleasant, an end to the unpleasant. Craving grows into grasping, which the tradition lists as four kinds: grasping at pleasures, at views, at rules and rituals, and at a self. With grasping, becoming arises, the build-up toward a new existence (26.7). And the verse adds its own escape clause: one who did not grasp would be freed, and there would be no becoming.

Becoming is the five aggregates, the body and mind of a new life (26.8). From it comes birth, and from birth everything that follows: ageing, death, sorrow, lamentation, pain, distress and despair (26.9). 'This whole mass of suffering' is the Buddha's own phrase. It is called 'whole' or 'only' (kevala) because there is nothing else in it. Candrakīrti reads the word as 'without a self or anything belonging to a self': no one who owns the suffering, only the suffering, arising from its conditions.

## Who does it, and how it stops

Verse 26.10 gives the moral. The one who does not know is the maker of the formations, the root of saṃsāra. The one who knows makes none, because they see how things are (tattva). This is not a change of fate imposed from outside. It is the plain consequence of the chain: the formations that lead to new suffering are made by not-knowing. Take away the not-knowing, and nothing is left to make them.

So the chain can stop (26.11–12). When ignorance ceases, formations do not arise. And ignorance ceases through 'cultivating this very knowledge': for Candrakīrti, seeing dependent arising as it really is, which is seeing its emptiness, the insight the whole work has been teaching. Then each link, losing its condition, does not arise: no formations, so no new consciousness thrown into a new life, and so on round the wheel. The whole mass of suffering comes to an end.

![Diagram 3](../../figures/mmk-26-diagram-3.svg)

Verses 26.11–12. The chain does not have to be broken link by link. Remove the first condition, and the rest, losing their conditions in turn, do not arise.

## Why the chapter is here

A reader who has followed the earlier chapters may be surprised. Chapter 1 found no arising from self, other, both or neither. Chapter 19 found no time standing on its own, and Chapter 18 no self. How can this chapter speak so plainly of one thing arising from another, of consciousness entering a new life?

The answer is the two truths ([Chapter 24](https://atr-passerby.com/nagarjuna-24-examination-of-the-four-noble-truths/)). The earlier chapters asked whether things arise from their own side, and found that they do not. This chapter describes how they arise as the world speaks: in dependence, each link named in relation to the others. There is no conflict, because the two descriptions answer different questions. More than that, the working chain needs the emptiness found earlier. A chain whose links had natures of their own could never stop. Ignorance with its own nature could never be removed, and the mass of suffering would be permanent. Because the links are empty, the chain works, and because it works, it can be ended.

Candrakīrti, whose reading the books follow, treats the chapter as the conventional account, fully endorsed, of how saṃsāra runs. Nothing in it conflicts with emptiness, and its last verses make emptiness the cure: the knowledge that ends ignorance is the seeing of how things are.

## What is left standing

Everything the tradition relies on is kept here: karma, rebirth, the link between what we do and what we become, and the path that ends suffering. What is left out is only the owner. There is no self that travels through the twelve links, no 'I' who ages and dies from its own side. Verse 26.9 calls it a mass of suffering with nothing else in it, and verse 26.10 makes the maker of saṃsāra not a person but not-knowing.

The chain can be watched in any hour. Notice the next time a pleasant feeling turns into wanting, or an unpleasant one into pushing away. That step, from feeling to craving, is where the wheel turns. Seen clearly, as a meeting of conditions with no one behind it, it does not have to turn.

## Words to know

- **The twelve links** (dvādaśāṅga pratītyasamutpāda): the chain from ignorance to ageing and death.
- **Ignorance** (avidyā): not seeing how things are; taking them to exist from their own side.
- **Formations** (saṃskāra): actions shaped by ignorance, which give direction to what follows.
- **Contact** (sparśa): the meeting of sense, object and consciousness.
- **Craving** (tṛṣṇā) and **grasping** (upādāna): wanting, then holding on.
- **Becoming** (bhava): the build-up toward a new existence.
- **How things are** (tattva): what knowing sees; for Madhyamaka, emptiness.
