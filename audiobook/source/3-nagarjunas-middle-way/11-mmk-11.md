# Examination of the Initial and Final Limits

*Nāgārjuna's Middle Way* · Chapter 11

Where did it all begin? Children ask it about the universe, and adults ask it about their own lives: the first mistake, the first habit, the first moment of being me. The question feels as if it must have an answer. If there is a story, it must have a first page.

The Buddha refused to name a first beginning of the round of birth and death (saṃsāra). This chapter shows that the refusal is not modesty about missing facts. If there is no first edge, the ideas of 'before' and 'after' stop working the way we picture them, and even birth and death cannot be lined up as two things in a row. It continues the work of [Chapter 7](https://atr-passerby.com/nagarjuna-7-examination-of-the-conditioned/) on arising, abiding and ceasing, and it prepares [Chapter 19](https://atr-passerby.com/nagarjuna-19-examination-of-time/) on time.

## The verses

The translation is new, made from the Sanskrit for this site. The Sanskrit is given above each verse; it follows the [GRETIL](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/) e-text (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0).

#### Verse 11.1

> *pūrvā prajñāyate koṭir nety uvāca mahāmuniḥ \|  
> saṃsāro 'navarāgro hi nāsyādir nāpi paścimam \|\|*

The great sage said that no first limit is known. For the round of birth and death is without beginning or end; it has no start and no finish.

#### Verse 11.2

> *naivāgraṃ nāvaraṃ yasya tasya madhyaṃ kuto bhavet \|  
> tasmān nātropapadyante pūrvāparasahakramāḥ \|\|*

Where there is no beginning and no end, how could there be a middle? So here 'before', 'after' and 'at the same time' do not hold.

#### Verse 11.3

> *pūrvaṃ jātir yadi bhavej jarāmaraṇam uttaram \|  
> nirjarā maraṇā jātir bhavej jāyeta cāmṛtaḥ \|\|*

If birth came first and ageing-and-death came after, there would be birth without ageing and death, and someone who never dies would be born.

#### Verse 11.4

> *paścāj jātir yadi bhavej jarāmaraṇam āditaḥ \|  
> ahetukam ajātasya syāj jarāmaraṇaṃ katham \|\|*

If birth came after and ageing-and-death came first, how could there be ageing and death without a cause, for someone not yet born?

#### Verse 11.5

> *na jarāmaraṇaṃ caiva jātiś ca saha yujyate \|  
> mriyeta jāyamānaś ca syāc cāhetukatobhayoḥ \|\|*

Ageing-and-death and birth cannot be at the same time either. Then what is being born would be dying, and neither would have a cause.

#### Verse 11.6

> *yatra na prabhavanty ete pūrvāparasahakramāḥ \|  
> prapañcayanti tāṃ jātiṃ taj jarāmaraṇaṃ ca kim \|\|*

Where 'before', 'after' and 'at the same time' do not apply, why do people elaborate such a birth, and such an ageing and death?

#### Verse 11.7

> *kāryaṃ ca kāraṇaṃ caiva lakṣyaṃ lakṣaṇam eva ca \|  
> vedanā vedakaś caiva santy arthā ye ca ke cana \|\|*

Effect and cause, what is marked and its mark, feeling and the one who feels: whatever things there are, the same holds.

#### Verse 11.8

> *pūrvā na vidyate koṭiḥ saṃsārasya na kevalam \|  
> sarveṣām api bhāvānāṃ pūrvā koṭī na vidyate \|\|*

It is not only the round of birth and death that has no first limit. No first limit is found for anything at all.

## No first page

The chapter opens with a saying of the Buddha (11.1): no first limit of the round of birth and death is known. The early discourses repeat it often. Trace any life back through its causes, and each cause has causes before it. No point is reached where the chain starts from nothing.

Nāgārjuna draws out what this means (11.2). We usually picture a stretch of time as a line with two ends and a middle. But a middle is defined by its ends: it is the part between the start and the finish. Take away both ends and there is no middle to point to. So the familiar frame of 'before', 'after' and 'at the same time' cannot be laid over the round of birth and death as if it were a ruler with marks on it.

Verses 11.1–2. A middle is the part between two ends. Without a first or last edge, there is no fixed frame of before and after to place things in.

## Birth and death cannot be lined up

To test the point, Nāgārjuna takes the most natural sequence there is: birth, then ageing and death (11.3–5). Surely birth comes first? Three orders are possible, and he checks each.

**Birth first, death after (11.3).** Then for a while there would be a birth that is not yet tied to ageing and death. Something would be born that is free of death. But birth is precisely the start of what ages and dies. A birth with no ageing and death built into it is not the birth of anything mortal.

**Death first, birth after (11.4).** Then ageing and death would happen to someone not yet born, with no cause. That is plainly absurd.

**Both at once (11.5).** Then what is being born would at that moment be dying. And since each would be there at the same time as the other, neither could be the cause of the other: causes are supposed to come before what they cause.

![Diagram 2](../../figures/mmk-11-diagram-2.svg)

Verses 11.3–5. Birth and ageing-and-death, taken as two self-standing events, cannot be put in any order. They are not two things in a row but one life, named in two ways.

The reason all three fail is the same. Each order treats birth and death as two separate events, each complete in itself, which then have to be arranged on the time line. But birth is identified as the beginning of what will age and die, and death as the end of what was born. Each is defined by the other. They are not two beads on a string; they are two ends of one designation, a life.

Verse 11.6 draws the conclusion. If before, after and at the same time do not apply, why elaborate (prapañca) a birth here and an ageing and death there, as if they were things we could take hold of and arrange? The word is the same as in the homage of [Chapter 1](https://atr-passerby.com/nagarjuna-1-examination-of-conditions/): elaboration, the mind spinning out positions around what it takes to be there.

## Every pair, everywhere

The last two verses widen the result (11.7–8). It is not only birth and death. Cause and effect, a thing and the mark by which it is known, a feeling and the one who feels it: every such pair has the same structure. A cause is a cause only of an effect, and an effect only of a cause. A mark is a mark only of what it marks. Ask which came first and you meet the same three dead ends.

![Diagram 3](../../figures/mmk-11-diagram-3.svg)

Verse 11.7. The same structure runs through every pair of related terms. Neither member is there first, waiting for the other.

So, finally, no first limit is found for anything (11.8). This is a strong claim and it is easy to mishear. It does not say that the universe is infinitely old, as a fact of cosmology. It says that no beginning can be found from the side of the things themselves: no first moment that stands on its own, uncaused, before which there was nothing. The question 'what was first?' assumes things with their own place on a time line, and that assumption is what fails.

## What is left standing

Birthdays are still celebrated and deaths are still mourned. A doctor can still say when a child was born and when an old man died, and be right. 'First' and 'last' work perfectly well within any framework we choose: the first page of a book, the first day of term. What the chapter removes is a first page of reality itself, standing on its own and needing nothing before it.

Practically, this frees the search for an origin of suffering from a false form. The Buddha did not teach that the round of birth and death ends by finding where it began. It ends by seeing how it is kept going now, by conditions that can change. Looking for a first cause is replaced by looking at present ones.

## Words to know

- **The round of birth and death** (saṃsāra): the ongoing cycle of lives driven by craving and ignorance.
- **Without beginning or end** (anavarāgra): having no first or last limit that can be found.
- **First limit** (pūrvā koṭi): a starting edge, with nothing before it.
- **Before, after, at the same time** (pūrva, apara, saha): the three ways two self-standing events could be ordered.
- **Ageing-and-death** (jarāmaraṇa): the last of the twelve links (see [Chapter 26](https://atr-passerby.com/nagarjuna-26-examination-of-the-twelve-links/)).
- **Elaboration** (prapañca): spinning out positions around what is taken to be there.
