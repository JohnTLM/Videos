# Examination of Motion

*Nāgārjuna's Middle Way* · Chapter 2

Walk across a room. Nothing seems simpler. There is you, there is the walking, and there is the floor you cross. You were at the door, and now you are at the window. The second chapter asks where, in that plain event, the moving is actually found. Is it in the part of the floor already crossed? In the part not yet crossed? In the spot under your foot right now? And who is the walker, apart from the walking?

[Chapter 1](https://atr-passerby.com/nagarjuna-1-examination-of-conditions/) tested causes, the way one thing is supposed to bring about another. Chapter 2 turns to motion, the most vivid kind of change, and uses a pattern of analysis that Nāgārjuna will use again and again: divide the field into the done, the not yet done and the being done, and look for the event in each. It also gives the classic test of agent and action: is the doer the same as the deed, or different?

## The verses

#### Verse 2.1

> *gataṃ na gamyate tāvad agataṃ naiva gamyate \|  
> gatāgatavinirmuktaṃ gamyamānaṃ na gamyate \|\|*

To begin with, what has been gone over is not being gone over. What has not been gone over is not being gone over either. And apart from the gone over and the not gone over, a 'being gone over' is not being gone over.

#### Verse 2.2

> *ceṣṭā yatra gatis tatra gamyamāne ca sā yataḥ \|  
> na gate nāgate ceṣṭā gamyamāne gatis tataḥ \|\|*

Objection: where there is movement of the feet, there is going. That movement is in what is being gone over, not in what has been gone over or what has not. So going is in what is being gone over.

#### Verse 2.3

> *gamyamānasya gamanaṃ kathaṃ nāmopapatsyate \|  
> gamyamānaṃ vigamanaṃ yadā naivopapadyate \|\|*

How could the going belong to what is being gone over, when a 'being gone over' without going makes no sense at all?

#### Verse 2.4

> *gamyamānasya gamanaṃ yasya tasya prasajyate \|  
> ṛte gater gamyamānaṃ gamyamānaṃ hi gamyate \|\|*

Whoever holds that the going belongs to what is being gone over must accept a 'being gone over' without going, since for them what is being gone over is what gets gone over.

#### Verse 2.5

> *gamyamānasya gamane prasaktaṃ gamanadvayam \|  
> yena tad gamyamānaṃ ca yac cātra gamanaṃ punaḥ \|\|*

If what is being gone over has a going, two goings follow: the one by which it counts as 'being gone over', and the going that then takes place on it.

#### Verse 2.6

> *dvau gantārau prasajyete prasakte gamanadvaye \|  
> gantāraṃ hi tiraskṛtya gamanaṃ nopapadyate \|\|*

If there are two goings, there must be two goers, for a going without a goer makes no sense.

#### Verse 2.7

> *gantāraṃ cet tiraskṛtya gamanaṃ nopapadyate \|  
> gamane 'sati gantātha kuta eva bhaviṣyati \|\|*

If going makes no sense without a goer, then where there is no going, how could there be a goer?

#### Verse 2.8

> *gantā na gacchati tāvad agantā naiva gacchati \|  
> anyo gantur agantuś ca kas tṛtīyo 'tha gacchati \|\|*

To begin with, a goer does not go, and a non-goer does not go. Apart from goer and non-goer, what third person goes?

#### Verse 2.9

> *gantā tāvad gacchatīti katham evopapatsyate \|  
> gamanena vinā gantā yadā naivopapadyate \|\|*

How can it make sense to say 'the goer goes', when a goer without going makes no sense at all?

#### Verse 2.10

> *pakṣo gantā gacchatīti yasya tasya prasajyate \|  
> gamanena vinā gantā gantur gamanam icchataḥ \|\|*

Whoever holds the thesis 'the goer goes' wants the going to belong to the goer, and so must accept a goer without going.

#### Verse 2.11

> *gamane dve prasajyete gantā yadyuta gacchati \|  
> ganteti cājyate yena gantā san yac ca gacchati \|\|*

If the goer goes, two goings follow: the one by which he is called a goer, and the one in which, being a goer, he goes.

#### Verse 2.12

> *gate nārabhyate gantuṃ gantuṃ nārabhyate 'gate \|  
> nārabhyate gamyamāne gantum ārabhyate kuha \|\|*

Going is not begun on what has been gone over. It is not begun on what has not been gone over. It is not begun on what is being gone over. Where, then, is it begun?

#### Verse 2.13

> *na pūrvaṃ gamanārambhād gamyamānaṃ na vā gatam \|  
> yatrārabhyeta gamanam agate gamanaṃ kutaḥ \|\|*

Before going begins, there is no 'being gone over' and no 'gone over' where going could begin. And how could going be in what has not been gone over?

#### Verse 2.14

> *gataṃ kiṃ gamyamānaṃ kim agataṃ kiṃ vikalpyate \|  
> adṛśyamāna ārambhe gamanasyaiva sarvathā \|\|*

When the beginning of going is nowhere to be seen, what is there to think of as gone over, being gone over, or not gone over?

#### Verse 2.15

> *gantā na tiṣṭhati tāvad agantā naiva tiṣṭhati \|  
> anyo gantur agantuś ca kas tṛtīyo 'tha tiṣṭhati \|\|*

To begin with, a goer does not stop, and a non-goer does not stop. Apart from goer and non-goer, what third person stops?

#### Verse 2.16

> *gantā tāvat tiṣṭhatīti katham evopapatsyate \|  
> gamanena vinā gantā yadā naivopapadyate \|\|*

How can it make sense to say 'the goer stops', when a goer without going makes no sense at all?

#### Verse 2.17

> *na tiṣṭhati gamyamānān na gatān nāgatād api \|  
> gamanaṃ saṃpravṛttiś ca nivṛttiś ca gateḥ samā \|\|*

One does not stop from what is being gone over, from what has been gone over, or from what has not. Going, the starting of going and the stopping of going are all examined in the same way.

#### Verse 2.18

> *yad eva gamanaṃ gantā sa eveti na yujyate \|  
> anya eva punar gantā gater iti na yujyate \|\|*

It does not make sense that the going is the very same as the goer. Nor does it make sense that the goer is something other than the going.

#### Verse 2.19

> *yad eva gamanaṃ gantā sa eva hi bhaved yadi \|  
> ekībhāvaḥ prasajyeta kartuḥ karmaṇa eva ca \|\|*

If the going were the very same as the goer, then doer and deed would be one and the same.

#### Verse 2.20

> *anya eva punar gantā gater yadi vikalpyate \|  
> gamanaṃ syād ṛte gantur gantā syād gamanād ṛte \|\|*

If the goer were thought to be other than the going, there could be going without a goer, and a goer without going.

#### Verse 2.21

> *ekībhāvena vā siddhir nānābhāvena vā yayoḥ \|  
> na vidyate tayoḥ siddhiḥ kathaṃ nu khalu vidyate \|\|*

Two things that cannot be established either as one or as different: how could they be established at all?

#### Verse 2.22

> *gatyā yayājyate gantā gatiṃ tāṃ sa na gacchati \|  
> yasmān na gatipūrvo 'sti kaścid kiṃcid dhi gacchati \|\|*

The going by which someone is shown to be a goer is not a going that he goes, since there is no goer before that going; and in going, someone goes somewhere.

#### Verse 2.23

> *gatyā yayājyate gantā tato 'nyāṃ sa na gacchati \|  
> gatī dve nopapadyete yasmād eke tu gantari \|\|*

Nor does he go a going other than the one by which he is shown to be a goer, since two goings make no sense in one goer.

#### Verse 2.24

> *sadbhūto gamanaṃ gantā triprakāraṃ na gacchati \|  
> nāsadbhūto 'pi gamanaṃ triprakāraṃ sa gacchati \|\|*

A goer who really exists does not go any of the three kinds of going. A goer who does not exist does not go any of the three kinds either.

#### Verse 2.25

> *gamanaṃ sadasadbhūtaḥ triprakāraṃ na gacchati \|  
> tasmād gatiś ca gantā ca gantavyaṃ ca na vidyate \|\|*

A goer who both exists and does not exist does not go any of the three kinds. So going, goer and the place to be gone are not found.

## Where is the moving?

The chapter opens with a simple division (2.1). Take the path of any movement, say your walk from the door to the window. At any moment the path falls into three parts. There is the stretch already crossed. There is the stretch not yet crossed. And there is, it seems, the stretch being crossed now. If motion is really happening, it must be happening in one of these.

It is not in the stretch already crossed. That part is finished; the moving there is over. It is not in the stretch not yet crossed. The moving there has not begun. So it must be in the third part, the stretch being crossed. But look for that stretch. Wherever you draw the line, the floor divides into what is behind the foot and what is ahead of it. The 'now' of the step has no width of its own. Take away the crossed and the not crossed, and no third strip is left over for the moving to live in.

![Diagram 1](../../figures/mmk-2-diagram-1.svg)

Verse 2.1. Motion is looked for in the three parts of a path. Two parts are ruled out at once. The third, the place 'being gone over', turns out to have no extent of its own apart from the other two.

The opponent replies (2.2): the movement of the feet is clearly happening, and it is not happening behind you or ahead of you. So going must be in the place being gone over. Nāgārjuna's answer (2.3–6) is subtle and worth slowing down for.

## Two goings, two goers

What makes a stretch of floor 'the place being gone over'? Only the going happening on it. Without a going, it is just floor. So the name 'being gone over' already contains a going (2.3).

Now the opponent says the going is *in* that place. Then there are two goings: the one that made the place count as 'being gone over', and the one that is now said to happen in it (2.5). If the opponent denies the first, he has a 'place being gone over' that nothing is going over (2.4), which is no longer that place at all. And if he accepts both, a going needs a goer, so two goings need two goers (2.6). One person crossing a room has become two.

![Diagram 2](../../figures/mmk-2-diagram-2.svg)

Verses 2.3–6. If going is a thing located in a place, the place needs a going to be 'the place being gone over', and the going said to be in it is a second one. Each going needs its own goer.

This sounds like word-play, but it exposes a real habit. We picture the going as a thing sitting somewhere, like water in a cup. Nāgārjuna shows that the cup (the place being gone over) is itself made of the water (the going). You cannot put the going into the place, because without the going there is no such place to put it in.

## Who goes?

The same pattern is then applied to the person (2.8–11). Who is it that goes? Not a goer: someone already counted as a goer is already going, so 'the goer goes' says the going twice. Not a non-goer: someone who is not going does not go. And there is no third kind of person between the two (2.8).

The double counting is spelled out in 2.11. If 'the goer goes', there is the going by which he is called a goer, and the going he then performs. Two goings again. If you insist on a goer who then goes, you must first have a goer without going (2.10), which is not a goer.

The point is not that no one walks. It is that 'walker' and 'walking' are not two items, one of which picks up the other. The walker is named from the walking, and the walking is named as someone's. Our grammar, subject then verb, makes it look as if a walker stood ready and then started to walk. Look for that ready walker, and you find only someone already walking, or someone not walking at all.

## Starting and stopping

If motion really happened, it would have to start somewhere (2.12–14). Where does going begin? Not on the stretch already gone over, for there it has already happened. Not on the stretch not yet gone over, for nothing is going on there. Not on the stretch being gone over, for that already has going in it. Before the going starts, there is no 'gone over' or 'being gone over' at all, only the 'not yet', and going is not in the not yet (2.13). And if no beginning of going is found, the three parts of the path, which were all defined by the going, have nothing to be defined by (2.14).

Stopping meets the same fate (2.15–17). A goer does not stop, since a goer is one who is going. A non-goer does not stop, since he was not going. And one does not stop from any of the three parts of the path. Nāgārjuna then generalises: going, starting and stopping are all analysed the same way (2.17).

Try it in slow motion. Lift a cup from the table. When did the lifting start? At the first tensing of the arm? When the cup's weight came off the wood? At every instant you choose, either the lifting is already under way, or it is not yet happening. The moment of starting, taken as a thing of its own, is not found. Yet the cup is lifted.

## Same or different?

Verses 2.18–21 give one of the most important tests in the whole text. If walker and walking were real things, each with its own nature, they would have to be either the same thing or two different things. Neither works.

![Diagram 3](../../figures/mmk-2-diagram-3.svg)

Verses 2.18–21. The test of identity and difference. A pair that can be neither one thing nor two separate things is not a pair of self-standing things at all.

If they were the same (2.19), doer and deed would be one. Then 'the walker' and 'the walking' would be two names for one item, and it would make as much sense to say 'the walking walks' or 'the walker is being done'. If they were different (2.20), each could exist without the other: a walker who is never walking, and a walking with no one doing it, drifting about on its own. Neither is ever met.

Something that can be established neither as one with its partner nor as different from it cannot be established as a thing at all (2.21). This is the shape of the argument, not a trick about walking. Fire and fuel ([Chapter 10](https://atr-passerby.com/nagarjuna-10-examination-of-fire-and-fuel/)), agent and action ([Chapter 8](https://atr-passerby.com/nagarjuna-8-examination-of-the-agent-and-action/)), and the self and its parts ([Chapter 18](https://atr-passerby.com/nagarjuna-18-examination-of-the-self-and-entities/)) will all be tested the same way.

Verses 2.22–23 close the gap from the other side. Going always means someone going somewhere: a goer, and something he goes to. Now take the going by which someone is recognised as a goer. It cannot be a going he goes, as if it were a road ahead of him, because there was no goer before it to set out on it. Nor can he go some other going, because one goer does not perform two goings at once.

## Existent, non-existent or both

The chapter ends (2.24–25) with the four-cornered pattern met in [Chapter 1](https://atr-passerby.com/nagarjuna-1-examination-of-conditions/). A goer who already exists, complete, does not need to go any of the three ways. A goer who does not exist goes nowhere. One who both exists and does not exist has both problems. Commentators read 'the three kinds of going' in different ways: as going over the three parts of the path, or as a going that is itself existent, non-existent or both. So going, goer and the place to be gone are not found (2.25).

'Not found' is the key phrase. Nāgārjuna is not saying, as Zeno of Elea was taken to say, that motion is an illusion and the arrow never flies. Zeno's paradoxes try to prove that motion is impossible. Nāgārjuna's analysis shows something narrower: that motion is not found as a thing of its own, located in a strip of path, carried by a walker who stands apart from it. In the reading of Candrakīrti and Tsongkhapa, what the analysis refutes is going established by its own nature, not the going that the world sees.

## What is left standing

You still walk to the window, and you get there. Trains run to timetables, and the timetable is right. Nothing in the chapter stops any of this. What it removes is the picture behind the words: a self-standing walker carrying a self-standing walking across a self-standing floor.

What remains is walking, met without those three pieces. Walker, walking and path are designated together, each in dependence on the others. The walker is called a walker because of the walking; the walking is a walking because someone is walking somewhere; the path is a path because it is walked. None stands first, waiting for the others to arrive.

Try it the next time you walk. Feel the walking itself: the shift of weight, the floor arriving under the foot, the room sliding past. Look for a walker standing behind it, steering. The walking is vivid and exact. The walker, as something apart from it, is not found. Nothing is missing.

## Words to know

- **Going** (gamana, gati): moving, walking, travelling.
- **Goer** (gantṛ): the one who goes, the mover.
- **Gone over, not gone over, being gone over** (gata, agata, gamyamāna): the three parts of a path. The same threefold analysis is applied to time.
- **Doer and deed** (kartṛ, karma): agent and action. The identity test asks whether they are one thing or two.
- **Same or different** (ekatva, anyatva): if two things with their own natures were related, they would have to be one or the other.
- **Not found** (na vidyate): not found when looked for, which is not the same as 'does not happen'.
