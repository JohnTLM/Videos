# Examination of Fire and Fuel

*Nāgārjuna's Middle Way* · Chapter 10

Light a log in the fireplace. There is the fire, and there is the wood it burns. Nothing seems simpler. Yet ask how the two are related and the simplicity goes. Is the fire the same thing as the burning wood, or something different that has taken hold of it? Which comes first: the fire, which needs fuel, or the fuel, which is called fuel only because it burns?

In Nāgārjuna's India, fire and fuel were the standard example for a much bigger question: how the self is related to the body and mind it seems to own. The Personalists (Pudgalavāda) said the person is to the aggregates as fire is to fuel: neither the same nor different, yet real in its own right. This chapter takes the example seriously and shows that fire and fuel, examined honestly, give no support to anything standing on its own. At the end it applies the result to the self, to pots and to cloth. It follows [Chapter 9](https://atr-passerby.com/nagarjuna-9-examination-of-the-prior-entity/), on the person said to exist before seeing and hearing, and it uses the method of [Chapter 2](https://atr-passerby.com/nagarjuna-2-examination-of-motion/), on motion.

## The verses

The translation is new, made from the Sanskrit for this site. The Sanskrit is given above each verse; it follows the [GRETIL](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/) e-text (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0).

#### Verse 10.1

> *yad indhanaṃ sa ced agnir ekatvaṃ kartṛkarmaṇoḥ \|  
> anyaś ced indhanād agnir indhanād apy ṛte bhavet \|\|*

If the fire were the fuel, the one who acts and what is acted on would be one. If the fire were other than the fuel, it could exist even without fuel.

#### Verse 10.2

> *nityapradīpta eva syād apradīpanahetukaḥ \|  
> punarārambhavaiyarthyam evaṃ cākarmakaḥ sati \|\|*

It would burn for ever, with nothing needed to light it. Lighting it again would be pointless, and so it would be a fire with nothing to do.

#### Verse 10.3

> *paratra nirapekṣatvād apradīpanahetukaḥ \|  
> punarārambhavaiyarthyaṃ nityadīptaḥ prasajyate \|\|*

Because it would not depend on anything else, nothing would be needed to light it. It would follow that it burns for ever, and that lighting it again is pointless.

#### Verse 10.4

> *tatraitat syād idhyamānam indhanaṃ bhavatīti cet \|  
> kenedhyatām indhanaṃ tat tāvanmātram idaṃ yadā \|\|*

Suppose you say: fuel is what is being burned. But if the fuel is only that much, what is it that burns it?

#### Verse 10.5

> *anyo na prāpsyate 'prapto na dhakṣyaty adahan punaḥ \|  
> na nirvāsyaty anirvāṇaḥ sthāsyate vā svaliṅgavān \|\|*

If the fire is other, it will not reach the fuel. Not reaching it, it will not burn it. Not burning it, it will not go out. Not going out, it will stay, keeping its own marks.

#### Verse 10.6

> *anya evandhanād agnir indhanaṃ prāpnuyād yadi \|  
> strī saṃprāpnoti puruṣaṃ puruṣaś ca striyaṃ yathā \|\|*

Suppose you say: though other than the fuel, the fire reaches it, as a woman reaches a man and a man reaches a woman.

#### Verse 10.7

> *anya evendhanād agnir indhanaṃ kāmam āpnuyāt \|  
> agnīndhane yadi syātām anyonyena tiraskṛte \|\|*

Very well, the fire, being other, might reach the fuel, if fire and fuel each existed apart, the one without the other.

#### Verse 10.8

> *yadīndhanam apekṣyāgnir apekṣyāgniṃ yadīndhanam \|  
> katarat pūrvaniṣpannaṃ yad apekṣyāgnir indhanam \|\|*

If fire depends on fuel, and fuel depends on fire, which of the two is established first, so that the other can depend on it?

#### Verse 10.9

> *yadīndhanam apekṣyāgnir agneḥ siddhasya sādhanam \|  
> evaṃ satīndhanaṃ cāpi bhaviṣyati niragnikam \|\|*

If fire depends on fuel, then a fire already established would be established a second time. And the fuel, too, would be fuel without any fire.

#### Verse 10.10

> *yo 'pekṣya sidhyate bhāvas tam evāpekṣya sidhyati \|  
> yadi yo 'pekṣitavyaḥ sa sidhyatāṃ kam apekṣya kaḥ \|\|*

If a thing is established in dependence on another, and that other is established in dependence on the first, then which is established in dependence on which?

#### Verse 10.11

> *yo 'pekṣya sidhyate bhāvaḥ so 'siddho 'pekṣate katham \|  
> athāpy apekṣate siddhas tv apekṣāsya na yujyate \|\|*

How can a thing that is established by depending do the depending while it is not yet established? And if it depends once it is established, the depending is pointless.

#### Verse 10.12

> *apekṣyendhanam agnir na nānapekṣyāgnir indhanam \|  
> apekṣyendhanam agniṃ na nānapekṣyāgnim indhanam \|\|*

There is no fire that depends on fuel, and no fire that does not. There is no fuel that depends on fire, and no fuel that does not.

#### Verse 10.13

> *āgacchaty anyato nāgnir indhane 'gnir na vidyate \|  
> atrendhane śeṣam uktaṃ gamyamānagatāgataiḥ \|\|*

Fire does not come from somewhere else, and fire is not found in the fuel. The rest about fuel is covered by what was said of the path being gone over, the gone and the not yet gone.

#### Verse 10.14

> *indhanaṃ punar agnir na nāgnir anyatra cendhanāt \|  
> nāgnir indhanavān nāgnāv indhanāni na teṣu saḥ \|\|*

Fire is not the fuel. Nor is fire somewhere other than the fuel. Fire does not possess the fuel. The fuel is not in the fire, and the fire is not in the fuel.

#### Verse 10.15

> *agnīndhanābhyāṃ vyākhyāta ātmopādānayoḥ kramaḥ \|  
> sarvo niravaśeṣeṇa sārdhaṃ ghaṭapaṭādibhiḥ \|\|*

By fire and fuel the whole account of the self and what it takes up has been explained, completely, together with pots, cloth and the rest.

#### Verse 10.16

> *ātmanaś ca satattvaṃ ye bhāvānāṃ ca pṛthak pṛthak \|  
> nirdiśanti na tān manye śāsanasyārthakovidān \|\|*

Those who teach that the self is real in itself, or that things are each separate and distinct, I do not consider to understand the meaning of the teaching.

## The same, or different?

The chapter begins with a simple fork (10.1). If fire and fuel really are two things, each with a nature of its own, then they are either the same thing or different things. There is no third choice for two self-standing items.

**The same.** If the fire is the fuel, then what burns and what is burned are one. The cook would be the dinner; the cutter would be the bread. We could no longer say 'the fire burns the wood', because there would be nothing for the burning to pass between.

**Different.** If the fire is a separate thing, it should be possible to have it without fuel, as a cup can be had without a saucer. And then strange results follow (10.2–3). A fire that needs no fuel needs nothing to light it, so it would burn for ever. No one would ever have to light it again, and it would be a fire that burns nothing. A fire with no fuel is not a fire at all.

![Diagram 1](../../figures/mmk-10-diagram-1.svg)

Verses 10.1–3. Two things with natures of their own must be either one thing or two. As one, fire and fuel lose the burning between them. As two, fire could exist without fuel, which no fire does.

## 'Fuel is what is being burned'

An opponent tries to define the terms (10.4): fuel is simply what is being burned. Then fire and fuel are linked by definition. But this only moves the problem. If the fuel is just 'what is being burned', what is doing the burning? Either the fire is part of the fuel, and we are back to 'the same', or it is something else, and we are back to 'different'.

Verse 10.5 spells out what 'different' really costs. A fire wholly other than its fuel would never reach it. Not reaching it, it would not burn it. Not burning it, it would never go out, since a fire goes out when its fuel is used up. It would sit there for ever, unchanged, holding its own marks. That is not a description of any fire anyone has seen.

The opponent replies with an image (10.6): different things meet all the time, as a woman and a man meet. Nāgārjuna agrees, with a condition (10.7). Two people can meet because each exists without the other: she was somewhere before they met, and so was he. Fire and fuel are not like that. There is no fire waiting somewhere before it meets its fuel, and no fuel that is fuel before any fire. So the image does not fit.

## Which comes first?

The heart of the chapter is about dependence itself (10.8–11). Everyone agrees that fire and fuel depend on each other. Fire needs fuel to burn; wood is called fuel only because it can burn or is burning. But if each depends on the other, which one is there first, so that the other can lean on it?

Take the first option: fire depends on fuel, and fuel is already there as fuel. Then fuel is fuel without any fire (10.9), which contradicts the starting point that fuel depends on fire. Take the other: fire is there first. Then the fire is already established, and depending on fuel would only establish what is already established (10.9). Verse 10.10 sums up the tangle: if each is established only by leaning on the other, there is nothing already standing for either one to lean on.

Verse 10.11 puts it in general form. A thing that becomes what it is by depending must either already exist when it depends, or not. If it does not yet exist, there is nothing to do the depending. If it already exists, it does not need to depend. Either way, dependence between two self-standing things cannot get started.

![Diagram 2](../../figures/mmk-10-diagram-2.svg)

Verses 10.8–11. Mutual dependence between two finished things cannot begin. It makes sense only when neither was a finished thing to begin with: fire and fuel are designated together, as one burning seen from two sides.

This is not an argument against dependence. It is an argument against two self-standing things depending on each other. When neither fire nor fuel is a finished thing waiting for the other, the puzzle goes away: 'fire' and 'fuel' are two names given together to one burning, as 'buyer' and 'seller' are two names given together to one sale. Nobody asks whether the buyer or the seller existed first.

Verse 10.12 states the result in all four directions. There is no fire that depends on fuel, and no fire that does not; no fuel that depends on fire, and no fuel that does not. The negations deny a self-standing fire and a self-standing fuel. They do not deny the fire in the grate.

## The fivefold search

Verse 10.13 links the chapter to [Chapter 2](https://atr-passerby.com/nagarjuna-2-examination-of-motion/). Fire does not arrive from somewhere else, and it is not hidden in the wood waiting to come out. The same reasoning that found no 'going' in the road already gone over, the road not yet gone over, or the road being gone over applies here to burning.

![Diagram 3](../../figures/mmk-10-diagram-3.svg)

Verse 10.14. The last three options all assume fire and fuel are two separate things, so they fail with the second. The same fivefold search is used for the self and its body and mind (10.15; compare [Chapter 22](https://atr-passerby.com/nagarjuna-22-examination-of-the-tathagata/)).

The last three options are versions of 'different'. To own something, or to contain it, or to sit in it, two separate things are needed. Since fire separate from fuel has already failed, all three fail with it. Candrakīrti later adds two more places (the collection of parts and the shape of the parts) to make the sevenfold search of the chariot, which the companion book explores at length.

## Self and body, pot and clay

Now the point of the example (10.15). Replace fire with the self and fuel with the body and mind it takes up (upādāna). The self is not the same as the body and mind: then it would come and go with every thought and every cell. It is not something apart from them: no one has ever found a self left over when body and mind are taken away. It does not own them, contain them, or sit inside them, for each of those needs two separate things. Yet 'I' is said, correctly, in dependence on them, as 'fire' is said in dependence on burning wood.

The same holds for a pot and its clay, a cloth and its threads, and everything else that is called a whole in dependence on parts. The Personalists were right that the self is like fire and fuel. They drew the wrong conclusion. The example does not show a self that is real in its own right. It shows a self, like a fire, that is real only as designated.

![Diagram 4](../../figures/mmk-10-diagram-4.svg)

Verse 10.15. One analysis, many cases. Whatever is called a whole, or an owner, in dependence on something else is found in none of the five ways, and still works as named.

The chapter closes with a strong line (10.16). Those who teach that the self is real in itself, or that things are each separate and distinct, have not understood the teaching. The target is not only the self. It is the habit of seeing each thing as a separate unit with its own edges.

## What is left standing

The fire still warms the room. You can still add wood, and the fire still goes out when the wood is gone. Nothing in the chapter stops a cook from cooking. What goes is the picture of two finished things, a fire-thing and a wood-thing, that meet and act on each other. In its place is one burning, described from two sides, with each name making sense only through the other.

That is also how the self is kept. 'I am tired', 'I will come tomorrow', 'I made a mistake': all of this remains true. The 'I' is designated in dependence on body and mind, works as designated, and is not found when looked for in any of the five ways. As the books put it, mutual dependence is not a circle that needs a first link; it is what being designated together means.

## Words to know

- **Fuel** (indhana): what is burned; in the analogy, the body and mind the self is designated on.
- **What is taken up** (upādāna): the aggregates that the self is designated in dependence on.
- **Identity and difference** (ekatva, anyatva): the two ways two self-standing things would have to be related. Neither holds.
- **Mutual dependence** (parasparāpekṣā): each named only through the other, with neither first.
- **Personalists** (Pudgalavāda): an early school that held the person to be real, neither the same as nor different from the aggregates.
- **The fivefold search**: same, different, possessor, container, contained. Candrakīrti's chariot adds two more to make seven.
