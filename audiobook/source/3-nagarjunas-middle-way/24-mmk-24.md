# Examination of the Four Noble Truths

*Nāgārjuna's Middle Way* · Chapter 24

Here is the worry that anyone meeting emptiness has sooner or later. If nothing has a nature of its own, does anything matter? If suffering is empty, why end it? If the path is empty, why walk it? If the Buddha is empty, who taught? It sounds as if emptiness, taken seriously, would dissolve the very teaching that teaches it, and leave us with nothing to stand on.

Chapter 24 meets this worry head on, and it is the turning point of the whole work. Up to here Nāgārjuna has taken apart conditions, motion, self, time and much else ([Chapter 1](https://atr-passerby.com/nagarjuna-1-examination-of-conditions/) onward). Now an opponent from his own tradition says: you have gone too far, you have destroyed Buddhism. Nāgārjuna's answer turns the charge around completely. It is not emptiness that would destroy the four noble truths. It is the opposite of emptiness. Only in a world without own-nature can anything arise, change, be practised or be attained.

Dependent arising is what we call emptiness. That is a designation in dependence, and that itself is the middle way.

If all this were not empty, there would be no arising and no passing away. Then it would follow, for you, that the four noble truths do not exist.

When suffering, its origin and its cessation do not exist, what path could bring about the cessation of suffering?

If suffering is by its own nature not fully understood, how could it later be fully understood? Is not an own-nature something that stays fixed?

For one who holds to own-nature, how could a fruit that by its own nature has not been attained ever be attained?

Without the fruits, there is no one who has attained a fruit and no one on the way to one. If those eight kinds of noble person do not exist, there is no community.

#### Verse 24.30

> dharme cāsati saṃghe ca kathaṃ buddho bhaviṣyati \|\|*

Without the noble truths, there is no true teaching either. With no teaching and no community, how could there be a Buddha?

#### Verse 24.31

> *apratītyāpi bodhiṃ ca tava buddhaḥ prasajyate \|  
> apratītyāpi buddhaṃ ca tava bodhiḥ prasajyate \|\|*

For you, a Buddha would follow who does not depend on awakening, and awakening would follow that does not depend on a Buddha.

For you, one who is by his own nature not a Buddha would never reach awakening, however hard he strove on the bodhisattva path.

No one would ever do right or wrong. What could be done by what is not empty? An own-nature is not something that is made.

For you, a fruit would exist without right or wrong action. For you, no fruit would come from right or wrong action.

> *dharmādharmanimittaṃ vā yadi te vidyate phalam \|

Or if, for you, a fruit does come from right or wrong action, how can a fruit that arose from them be not empty?

#### Verse 24.36

> *sarvasaṃvyavahārāṃś ca laukikān pratibādhase \|  
> yat pratītyasamutpādaśūnyatāṃ pratibādhase \|\|*

You undermine all the everyday conventions of the world when you undermine emptiness, which is dependent arising.

#### Verse 24.37

> *na kartavyaṃ bhavet kiṃcid anārabdhā bhavet kriyā \|  
> kārakaḥ syād akurvāṇaḥ śūnyatāṃ pratibādhataḥ \|\|*

For one who undermines emptiness, there would be nothing to be done, action would be without a start, and an agent would do nothing.

#### Verse 24.38

> *ajātam aniruddhaṃ ca kūṭasthaṃ ca bhaviṣyati \|  
> vicitrābhir avasthābhiḥ svabhāve rahitaṃ jagat \|\|*

The world would be unborn, unceasing and fixed like an anvil, empty of all its varied states, if it had its own nature.

#### Verse 24.39

> *asaṃprāptasya ca prāptir duḥkhaparyantakarma ca \|  
> sarvakleśaprahāṇaṃ ca yady aśūnyaṃ na vidyate \|\|*

Reaching what has not been reached, the act of ending suffering, and the abandoning of all afflictions would not exist if things were not empty.

#### Verse 24.40

> *yaḥ pratītyasamutpādaṃ paśyatīdaṃ sa paśyati \|  
> duḥkhaṃ samudayaṃ caiva nirodhaṃ mārgam eva ca \|\|*

Whoever sees dependent arising sees suffering, its origin, its cessation and the path.

## The objection: emptiness destroys everything

The chapter opens with the opponent's case, stated fairly and in full (24.1–6). It runs like a row of dominoes. If everything is empty, nothing arises and nothing passes away. Then there are no four noble truths, because the truths are about the arising of suffering and its ending (24.1). Without the truths, there is nothing to understand, abandon, cultivate or realise: no practice (24.2). Without practice, no one reaches the four stages of the noble path, and no one is on the way to them (24.3). Without those noble persons, there is no Sangha, the community; and without the truths, no Dharma, the teaching (24.4). Without Dharma and Sangha, how could there be a Buddha? So emptiness undermines all three jewels (24.5). And it undermines more besides: the fruits of action, right and wrong, and all the ordinary dealings of the world (24.6).

The opponent is not a straw man. He is a serious Buddhist, probably of the Abhidharma schools, for whom the elements of experience had to have natures of their own in order to function. To him, 'empty' means 'non-existent', and a non-existent suffering cannot arise or end.

Verses 24.1–6. Each step of the objection follows from the one before. Everything hangs on the first box: the opponent takes 'empty' to mean 'non-existent'. Nāgārjuna's reply goes straight to that box.

## The reply: you have misunderstood emptiness

Nāgārjuna does not deny that the chain would follow if emptiness meant what the opponent thinks. He says the opponent has misunderstood three things: the purpose of emptiness, emptiness itself, and the meaning of emptiness (24.7). Candrakīrti spells them out. The purpose of emptiness is to quiet elaborations (prapañca), the mind's spinning-out of 'it is' and 'it is not' around things it takes to be there. Emptiness itself is not a thing, not a void and not a hidden absolute; it is the absence of own-nature. And the meaning of emptiness is dependent arising (24.18). The opponent has heard 'empty' and pictured nothing at all. That picture is what troubles him, not emptiness.

## The two truths

To explain, Nāgārjuna introduces the two truths (24.8). The Buddhas teach by relying on two: the conventional truth of the world (saṃvṛti-satya), and the truth in the ultimate sense (paramārtha-satya). Conventional truth is the world as it appears and works: tables hold cups, medicine heals, people suffer and are kind. Candrakīrti notes three senses of the word saṃvṛti: what conceals (ignorance hides how things are), what exists in mutual dependence, and worldly convention, the naming and agreement we live by. Ultimate truth is what analysis finds when it looks for the table's own nature, apart from its parts, causes and name: no such nature is found. Not finding it is the ultimate truth, emptiness.

The two truths are not two worlds, one false and one real, stacked on top of each other. They are one appearance, taken in two ways. The same table is the cup-holder of convention and the empty of analysis. Its working and its emptiness are not two facts about it; they are one fact seen from two sides. A thing works because it depends, and because it depends it has no nature of its own.

![Diagram 2](../../figures/mmk-24-diagram-2.svg)

Verses 24.8–9. The two truths are two ways of taking the same appearance, not two layers of reality. The table's working (convention) and its lack of own-nature (the ultimate) do not compete; each implies the other.

Whoever misses this distinction misses the depth of the Buddha's teaching (24.9). And the two truths need each other in a definite order. Without relying on convention, the ultimate cannot be taught: emptiness is pointed out using words, examples and reasoning, all of which belong to convention (24.10). And without coming to the ultimate, nirvāṇa is not reached: liberation comes from seeing through the grasping at own-nature, not from rearranging conventions. Convention is the ladder; the ultimate is where it leads; and the ladder is not thrown away, because the ultimate is only ever the emptiness of conventional things.

## A snake grasped the wrong way

Emptiness is powerful, and like anything powerful it can harm. Wrongly seen, it ruins the one who sees it, like a snake picked up by the wrong end or a spell said wrongly (24.11). Picked up behind the head, a snake is safe; picked up by the tail, it turns and bites. Emptiness has two wrong ends. Grasped as nothingness, it becomes nihilism: nothing matters, nothing has consequences. Grasped as a thing, an ultimate essence or cosmic ground, it becomes a new substance, the very reification it was meant to cure. Grasped correctly, it is simply the absence of own-nature in things that keep working.

![Diagram 3](../../figures/mmk-24-diagram-3.svg)

Verse 24.11. The snake image: emptiness has two wrong ends. Taken as nothing, it licenses carelessness; taken as something, it becomes a hidden absolute. Only the middle grip, absence of own-nature, is safe.

This danger, the tradition says, is why the Buddha hesitated to teach after his awakening: the teaching is deep and easily misheld (24.12). The faults the opponent charges against emptiness are real faults, but they are not the faults of emptiness. They belong to the nothingness he has mistaken it for (24.13).

## For whom emptiness is possible, everything is possible

Then comes the verse at the centre of the chapter, and perhaps of the whole book: for whom emptiness is possible, everything is possible; for whom emptiness is not possible, nothing is possible (24.14). The verb, yujyate, means 'is possible', 'fits', 'makes sense', and so 'works'. The main book quotes the verse in these same words. If emptiness makes sense, then arising, ceasing, cause, effect, practice and awakening all make sense. If emptiness does not make sense, none of them does.

Why? Because to work is to depend. A seed works by turning into a sprout, which means it is not fixed as a seed. Medicine works by changing a body, which means the illness was not fixed as illness. Practice works by changing a mind, which means the mind was not fixed as it was. Everything that functions does so in dependence on conditions, and depending on conditions is exactly what being 'empty of own-nature' comes to. A thing with a nature truly its own would be sealed off from everything else. It could neither act nor be acted upon.

The opponent, Nāgārjuna says, is like a man riding a horse who accuses others of stealing it: he has forgotten the horse he is sitting on (24.15). He relies on dependent arising every moment, in every cause and effect he speaks of, and then charges the teaching of dependent arising with destroying cause and effect.

## Dependent arising is emptiness

If you see things as existing by their own nature, you see them as needing no causes or conditions (24.16), for what is complete in itself needs nothing else. Then you undermine everything causal: effect and cause, agent, means and action, arising, ceasing and fruit (24.17).

Verse 24.18 gives Nāgārjuna's positive statement, as close as he comes to one: dependent arising is what we call emptiness; that is a designation in dependence; and that itself is the middle way. Four names for one fact. Dependent arising is how things exist: only in dependence. Emptiness is what that means: no own-nature. Designation in dependence says that even emptiness is a name given in relation to what it qualifies, not a thing in its own right. And the middle way is the result: free of the extreme of existence by own-nature and of the extreme of nothingness.

![Diagram 4](../../figures/mmk-24-diagram-4.svg)

Verse 24.18. Not four doctrines but four names for one fact. Read 'designation in dependence' carefully: emptiness itself is designated in relation to what is empty, so it cannot become a new ground.

The middle clause of 24.18 carries two points, and Candrakīrti's commentary makes both. First, 'that' is emptiness: emptiness too is only a designation in dependence, not a thing in its own right. Second, dependent arising is itself designation in dependence: a chariot is designated in dependence on its wheels and other parts, and what is designated in this way has not arisen by its own nature. Tsongkhapa develops both. Either way, the point is that nothing, emptiness included, escapes dependence.

Verse 24.19 draws the conclusion. There is no phenomenon that has not arisen in dependence. So there is no phenomenon that is not empty. This is not a guess from checking things one by one. Anything put forward as existing apart from dependence would have to be picked out and told apart from other things, and that already places it in relation. Emptiness is not a special region of reality, reserved for the enlightened. It is the condition of every cup, thought and breath.

## The objection turned around

Now Nāgārjuna repeats the opponent's first verse word for word, with one change: 'empty' becomes 'not empty' (24.20). If all this were not empty, there would be no arising and no passing away, and the four noble truths would not exist. Then he runs the whole chain again, link by link, and shows that own-nature, not emptiness, breaks every one.

![Diagram 5](../../figures/mmk-24-diagram-5.svg)

Verses 24.20–39. The reversal. Every link the opponent feared emptiness would break is broken instead by own-nature. Emptiness is what lets each one hold.

**Suffering** (24.21–22). Suffering is taught as impermanent: it comes and goes. But what has its own nature cannot come and go, because coming and going are changes, and an own-nature does not change. So suffering with an own-nature would have no origin. It would simply be, always.

**Cessation** (24.23). For the same reason it could never end. Clinging to own-nature makes the third truth, the ending of suffering, impossible.

**The path** (24.24–25). A path with an own-nature could not be cultivated, because to cultivate is to bring about what was not there. And if it is cultivated, it is not the fixed thing the opponent claims. Without arising, origin and cessation, what would the path lead to?

**Understanding and the fruits** (24.26–30). If suffering is by its own nature not yet understood, it could never come to be understood, for an own-nature stays as it is. The same goes for abandoning, realising and cultivating, and for the four fruits of the path (24.27–28). Without them, the chain of the opponent's objection follows after all: no noble persons, no Sangha, no Dharma, no Buddha (24.29–30). But now it follows from his view, not from emptiness.

**The Buddha** (24.31–32). With own-nature, a Buddha would be a Buddha independently of awakening, and awakening would exist independently of any Buddha. And one who is by nature not a Buddha would never become one, however hard he practised the bodhisattva path.

**Ethics** (24.33–35). No one could ever act rightly or wrongly, for what is not empty cannot be done or made: an own-nature is not something produced. Fruits would come without actions, or actions would bear no fruit. And if a fruit does come from actions, then it arose dependently, and so it is empty.

**The world** (24.36–38). Undermining emptiness undermines all the conventions of the world. Nothing would need doing, action would never begin, and agents would do nothing (24.37). The world would be unborn, unceasing and fixed like an anvil (kūṭastha), empty of all its varied states (24.38). That is the real picture of a world with own-nature: not a solid, reliable world, but a frozen one, where nothing can move because everything already is what it is.

Reaching what was not yet reached, ending suffering, abandoning the afflictions: none of these would be possible if things were not empty (24.39). And so the chapter ends where it began, with the four truths, now secured: whoever sees dependent arising sees suffering, its origin, its cessation and the path (24.40).

## Why own-nature freezes the world

The reversal can seem like a clever trick, so it is worth seeing why it works. Picture a thing with a nature truly its own. Its nature belongs to it independently of anything else. Then nothing else can change it, because if something could, its nature would depend on that thing not happening. And it cannot change itself, because a change would make it other than its own nature. So it is stuck. It cannot come into being from causes, because then its nature would come from them. It cannot pass away, because then its nature would depend on conditions continuing.

![Diagram 6](../../figures/mmk-24-diagram-6.svg)

Verses 24.16–17 and 24.38. A thing with own-nature would be sealed off: conditions could not reach it, and it could not become anything else. The seed that can sprout is the empty seed.

This is why the two books on this site keep returning to this chapter. Emptiness is not a loss of the world. The world we actually live in, where seeds sprout, wounds heal, minds learn and suffering can end, is only possible as an empty world. A substance would not be a firmer foundation. It would be a stopped clock.

## What is left standing

Everything the opponent feared to lose is kept, and kept on firmer ground: the four noble truths, the path and its fruits, the Buddha, the teaching and the community, right and wrong, cause and effect, and the whole web of ordinary dealings. Conventional truth is not demoted. It is the truth by which we live, act and practise, and the only way the ultimate can be taught at all (24.10).

What goes is only own-nature, the idea that things are what they are from their own side, sealed off from conditions. You can test the verse at any moment. Think of a habit you have changed, a skill you have learned, a grief that has softened. Each change was possible only because the old state was not fixed. The old state was never fixed from its own side: that is its emptiness, seen at work in your own life.

## Words to know

- **The four noble truths** (ārya-satya): suffering, its origin, its cessation and the path to its cessation.
- **Conventional truth** (saṃvṛti-satya): the world as it appears and works, named and agreed upon.
- **Ultimate truth** (paramārtha-satya): what analysis finds when it seeks a thing's own nature: emptiness.
- **Emptiness** (śūnyatā): the absence of own-nature; itself empty, never a thing or a ground.
- **Dependent designation** (prajñaptir upādāya): naming in dependence on a basis, as a chariot is named on its parts.
- **The middle way** (madhyamā pratipad): free of the extremes of existence by own-nature and of nothingness.
- **Fixed like an anvil** (kūṭastha): unchanging and inert; what a world with own-nature would be.
- **The three jewels** (triratna): the Buddha, the Dharma and the Sangha.
