# Examination of Views

*Nāgārjuna's Middle Way* · Chapter 27

Was I someone before this life? Will I be someone after it? Has the world always existed, and will it ever end? These are some of the oldest questions people ask, and some of the most anxious. In the Buddha's time teachers built whole systems on their answers, and the Buddha famously refused to answer them at all. This last chapter asks why the refusal was right.

It gathers up the whole work. The tool is one used again and again since [Chapter 1](https://atr-passerby.com/nagarjuna-1-examination-of-conditions/): is the thing the same as what it depends on, or different from it? Applied to the self of past and future lives, both answers fail, as they failed for fire and fuel in [Chapter 10](https://atr-passerby.com/nagarjuna-10-examination-of-fire-and-fuel/) and for the self in [Chapter 18](https://atr-passerby.com/nagarjuna-18-examination-of-the-self-and-entities/). Then the views about the world fail in the same way. The text ends where it began: with a bow to the Buddha, who taught in order to let go of all views.

## The verses

#### Verse 27.1

> *abhūm atītam adhvānaṃ nābhūvam iti dṛṣṭayaḥ \|  
> yās tāḥ śāśvatalokādyāḥ pūrvāntaṃ samupāśritāḥ \|\|*

The views 'I existed in time past' and 'I did not exist', and those such as 'the world is permanent', rest on the earlier limit.

#### Verse 27.2

> *dṛṣṭayo na bhaviṣyāmi kim anyo 'nāgate 'dhvani \|  
> bhaviṣyāmīti cāntādyā aparāntaṃ samāśritāḥ \|\|*

The views 'I will not exist in time to come', 'I will be someone else' and 'I will exist', and those such as 'the world has an end', rest on the later limit.

#### Verse 27.3

> *abhūm atītam adhvānam ity etan nopapadyate \|  
> yo hi janmasu pūrveṣu sa eva na bhavaty ayam \|\|*

'I existed in time past' does not hold. For the one who lived in earlier births is not simply this one.

#### Verse 27.4

> *sa evātmeti tu bhaved upādānaṃ viśiṣyate \|  
> upādānavinirmukta ātmā te katamaḥ punaḥ \|\|*

You may say the self is that very same one; but what it takes up is different. Apart from what it takes up, what is this self of yours?

#### Verse 27.5

> *upādānavinirmukto nāsty ātmeti kṛte sati \|  
> syād upādānam evātmā nāsti cātmeti vaḥ punaḥ \|\|*

Once it is granted that there is no self apart from what it takes up, then what it takes up would itself be the self; and then, on your view, there is no self.

#### Verse 27.6

> *na copādānam evātmā vyeti tat samudeti ca \|  
> kathaṃ hi nāmopādānam upādātā bhaviṣyati \|\|*

But what is taken up is not the self. It passes away and it arises. How could what is taken up be the one who takes it up?

#### Verse 27.7

> *anyaḥ punar upādānād ātmā naivopapadyate \|  
> gṛhyeta hy anupādāno yady anyo na ca gṛhyate \|\|*

Nor does a self other than what it takes up make sense. If it were other, it would be grasped without anything taken up; and it is not grasped.

#### Verse 27.8

> *evaṃ nānya upādānān na copādānam eva saḥ \|  
> ātmā nāsty anupādāno nāpi nāsty eṣa niścayaḥ \|\|*

So the self is not other than what it takes up, and it is not simply what it takes up. There is no self without anything taken up; yet it is not non-existent either. That is the conclusion.

#### Verse 27.9

> *nābhūm atītam adhvānam ity etan nopapadyate \|  
> yo hi janmasu pūrveṣu tato 'nyo na bhavaty ayam \|\|*

'I did not exist in time past' does not hold either. For this one is not other than the one who lived in earlier births.

#### Verse 27.10

> *yadi hy ayaṃ bhaved anyaḥ pratyākhyāyāpi taṃ bhavet \|  
> tathaiva ca sa saṃtiṣṭhet tatra jāyeta cāmṛtaḥ \|\|*

If this one were other, it would exist even if that one were set aside. That one would stay on just as it was, and this one would be born there without that one having died.

#### Verse 27.11

> *ucchedaḥ karmaṇāṃ nāśaḥ kṛtam anyena karma ca \|  
> pratisaṃvedayed anya evam ādi prasajyate \|\|*

Cutting off, actions lost, an action done by one and its result experienced by another: these and other consequences would follow.

#### Verse 27.12

> *nāpy abhūtvā samudbhūto doṣo hy atra prasajyate \|  
> kṛtako vā bhaved ātmā saṃbhūto vāpy ahetukaḥ \|\|*

Nor does it come into being from not having been before, for then faults follow: the self would be something made, or would arise without a cause.

#### Verse 27.13

> *evaṃ dṛṣṭir atīte yā nābhūm aham abhūm aham \|  
> ubhayaṃ nobhayaṃ ceti naiṣā samupapadyate \|\|*

So the views about the past, 'I did not exist', 'I existed', both, and neither, do not hold.

#### Verse 27.14

> *adhvany anāgate kiṃ nu bhaviṣyāmīti darśanam \|  
> na bhaviṣyāmi cety etad atītenādhvanā samam \|\|*

The views 'Will I exist in time to come?' and 'I will not exist' are the same as those about time past.

#### Verse 27.15

> *sa devaḥ sa manuṣyaś ced evaṃ bhavati śāśvatam \|  
> anutpannaś ca devaḥ syāj jāyate na hi śāśvatam \|\|*

If the god were the very same as the human, that would make it permanent. And the god would be unborn, for what is permanent is not born.

#### Verse 27.16

> *devād anyo manuṣyaś ced aśāśvatam ato bhavet \|  
> devād anyo manuṣyaś cet saṃtatir nopapadyate \|\|*

If the human were other than the god, it would follow that it is impermanent. If the human were other than the god, there could be no continuity.

#### Verse 27.17

> *divyo yady ekadeśaḥ syād ekadeśaś ca mānuṣaḥ \|  
> aśāśvataṃ śāśvataṃ ca bhavet tac ca na yujyate \|\|*

If one part were divine and one part human, it would be both impermanent and permanent; and that is not right.

#### Verse 27.18

> *aśāśvataṃ śāśvataṃ ca prasiddham ubhayaṃ yadi \|  
> siddhe na śāśvataṃ kāmaṃ naivāśāśvatam ity api \|\|*

If 'both impermanent and permanent' were established, then 'neither permanent nor impermanent' could be established too, if you wished.

#### Verse 27.19

> *kutaścid āgataḥ kaścit kiṃcid gacchet punaḥ kva cit \|  
> yadi tasmād anādis tu saṃsāraḥ syān na cāsti saḥ \|\|*

If someone had come from somewhere and then went on somewhere else, saṃsāra would be without beginning. But there is no such someone.

#### Verse 27.20

> *nāsti cec chāśvataḥ kaścit ko bhaviṣyaty aśāśvataḥ \|  
> śāśvato 'śāśvataś cāpi dvābhyām ābhyāṃ tiraskṛtaḥ \|\|*

If no one is permanent, who will be impermanent? Who will be both permanent and impermanent, or set apart from both?

#### Verse 27.21

> *antavān yadi lokaḥ syāt paralokaḥ kathaṃ bhavet \|  
> athāpy anantavāl lokaḥ paralokaḥ kathaṃ bhavet \|\|*

If the world had an end, how could there be a next world? And if the world were endless, how could there be a next world?

#### Verse 27.22

> *skandhānām eṣa saṃtāno yasmād dīpārciṣām iva \|  
> tasmān nānantavattvaṃ ca nāntavattvaṃ ca yujyate \|\|*

Since this continuum of the aggregates goes on like the flame of a lamp, neither endlessness nor having an end makes sense.

#### Verse 27.23

> *pūrve yadi ca bhajyerann utpadyeran na cāpy amī \|  
> skandhāḥ skandhān pratītyemān atha loko 'ntavān bhavet \|\|*

If the earlier aggregates perished and these later ones did not arise in dependence on them, then the world would have an end.

#### Verse 27.24

> *pūrve yadi na bhajyerann utpadyeran na cāpyamī \|  
> skandhāḥ skandhān pratītyemāl loko 'nanto bhaved atha \|\|*

If the earlier aggregates did not perish and these later ones did not arise in dependence on them, then the world would be endless.

#### Verse 27.25

> *antavān ekadeśaś ced ekadeśas tv anantavān \|  
> syād antavān anantaś ca lokas tac ca na yujyate \|\|*

If one part had an end and one part were endless, the world would both have an end and be endless; and that is not right.

#### Verse 27.26

> *kathaṃ tāvad upādātur ekadeśo vinaṅkṣyate \|  
> na naṅkṣyate caikadeśa evaṃ caitan na yujyate \|\|*

How could one part of the one who takes up perish, while another part did not? This does not make sense.

#### Verse 27.27

> *upādānaikadeśaś ca kathaṃ nāma vinaṅkṣyate \|  
> na naṅkṣyate caikadeśo naitad apy upapadyate \|\|*

And how could one part of what is taken up perish, while another part did not? This does not hold either.

#### Verse 27.28

> *antavac cāpy anantaṃ ca prasiddham ubhayaṃ yadi \|  
> siddhe naivāntavat kāmaṃ naivānantavad ity api \|\|*

If 'both having an end and endless' were established, then 'neither having an end nor endless' could be established too, if you wished.

#### Verse 27.29

> *atha vā sarvabhāvānāṃ śūnyatvāc chāśvatādayaḥ \|  
> kva kasya katamāḥ kasmāt saṃbhaviṣyanti dṛṣṭayaḥ \|\|*

Or rather: since all things are empty, where, for whom, which, and why would views such as permanence arise?

#### Verse 27.30

> *sarvadṛṣṭiprahāṇāya yaḥ saddharmam adeśayat \|  
> anukampām upādāya taṃ namasyāmi gautamam \|\|*

Out of compassion he taught the true Dharma for the letting go of all views. To him, to Gautama, I bow.

## Views that rest on a beginning and an end

The chapter opens with a list (27.1–2). One group of views looks back: 'I existed in the past', 'I did not exist', and with them 'the world is permanent', 'the world is not permanent', and so on. These rest on the earlier limit, a beginning. The other group looks forward: 'I will exist', 'I will not exist', 'I will be someone else', and 'the world has an end', 'it has no end'. These rest on the later limit. Each question has the familiar four corners: yes, no, both, neither.

![Diagram 1](../../figures/mmk-27-diagram-1.svg)

Verses 27.1–2. The views the chapter takes apart: four questions, four answers each. They differ in content, but each takes for granted a self or a world with a nature of its own, which could have a beginning or an end.

## Did I exist in the past?

Take the first view: 'I existed in the past' (27.3). Suppose there was a person in an earlier life, and you are that very person. But look at what makes a person: a body, feelings, perceptions, habits, awareness, the five aggregates, which the verses call 'what is taken up' (upādāna). The body of that earlier person is gone. The feelings and thoughts are gone. What is taken up now is different (27.4). So what is it that stayed the same? Something apart from all of that?

Here the argument of [Chapter 18](https://atr-passerby.com/nagarjuna-18-examination-of-the-self-and-entities/) returns, in four steps.

**Not something apart (27.4–5).** A self apart from the body and mind is never found. Try to locate it, as distinct from the sensations, thoughts and awareness going on now, and nothing is there to point to.

**Not the aggregates themselves (27.6).** Then is the self simply the body and mind? But they arise and pass away, moment by moment. A self that was the passing aggregates would pass away with them, and nothing would be 'the same' as the past person. Worse, what is taken up cannot be the one who takes it up, any more than the fuel is the fire.

**Not other than them (27.7).** If the self were something different from the aggregates, it would be seen on its own, without them. It never is.

**But not nothing either (27.8).** So the self is neither the aggregates nor other than them. That does not mean there is no self at all. The verse says so plainly: the self is not non-existent either, and this is its conclusion (niścaya). Like the chariot, the self is designated in dependence on its parts. It is neither the parts nor something else, and yet it is there as designated, acting and experiencing.

![Diagram 2](../../figures/mmk-27-diagram-2.svg)

Verses 27.4–8. The self is not the aggregates and not something other than them. What remains is the self designated in dependence on them, like the chariot on its parts: not found when sought, and still able to act.

## Or did I not exist?

Then perhaps the opposite: 'I did not exist in the past' (27.9). That is, the person in the earlier life was someone entirely other than you. This fails in the other direction. If you were wholly other than that earlier person, you could exist even if that person had never lived (27.10). That person could carry on as they were, or you could be born without their having died. The connection that makes it a series of lives would be gone.

And the consequences matter (27.11). If the earlier and later persons were wholly other, the stream would be cut off. What was done would be lost, with no result. Or one person would act and a stranger would reap the result. Nor could you simply come into being from nothing, with no past at all (27.12). Then the self would be something newly made, like a pot, which is not the self the view had in mind; or it would arise without a cause, which [Chapter 1](https://atr-passerby.com/nagarjuna-1-examination-of-conditions/) ruled out (1.1).

![Diagram 3](../../figures/mmk-27-diagram-3.svg)

Verses 27.3–12. Both answers to 'was I someone before?' fail. What links the lives is not a traveller passing over, and not a gap. It is continuity designated on a series that is neither the same nor different, like one flame lit from another.

So none of the four views about the past holds (27.13): not 'I existed', not 'I did not exist', not both, not neither. And the views about the future, 'Will I exist?' and 'I will not exist', fall in exactly the same way (27.14). This is the middle way between the two great fears: that something of me must last for ever, and that at death I am simply wiped out.

## God and human: permanent or not?

Verses 27.15–20 test the views about permanence with a traditional example: a being reborn as a god and later as a human. If the god and the human were exactly the same, the being would be permanent (27.15). But then the god would never have been born, for what is permanent is not born. If the human were wholly other than the god, the being would be impermanent, and there would be no continuity between them at all (27.16). Half god and half human will not do either: that would be permanent and impermanent at once (27.17). And if 'both' cannot be established, neither can 'neither' (27.18), which only makes sense against the other two.

Verse 27.19 adds a quiet point. If there were someone who came from somewhere and went on somewhere else, saṃsāra would truly be without beginning: one traveller, walking for ever. But there is no such someone. And if nothing is found that is permanent, nothing is found to be impermanent either (27.20). Permanence and impermanence, like long and short, are named together.

## Does the world have an end?

The last group of views looks to the future of the world (27.21–28). If the world had an end, how could there be a next world? If it were endless, how could there be a next one, either? Both pictures freeze the world: one cuts it off, the other makes it one unending thing.

The answer is the lamp (27.22). The continuum of the aggregates goes on like the flame of a lamp. The flame at midnight is not the flame at dusk; the oil and air that feed it have all been replaced. Yet it is not an unrelated flame; each moment of burning arises in dependence on the one before. Such a series is neither endless nor with an end, because there is no single thing there to last for ever or to stop.

![Diagram 4](../../figures/mmk-27-diagram-4.svg)

Verses 27.22–24. 'The world ends' and 'the world goes on for ever' both picture one thing that either stops or lasts. The lamp shows a third way that is not a third view: a series designated on moments, each arising from the last, with nothing carried and nothing cut off.

The remaining verses close the exits (27.23–28). If the earlier aggregates perished and the later did not arise from them, the world would end. If the earlier did not perish, and the later did not need to arise from them, it would be endless. Could part of the world end and part go on? Then part of the person who takes up the aggregates would perish and part would not, or part of what is taken up would perish and part would not. Neither makes sense, because a person and the aggregates are not split into a lasting half and a perishing half. And 'neither ending nor endless', taken as one more position, depends on the first two.

## All views let go

Then the chapter steps back (27.29). Or rather, says Nāgārjuna, since all things are empty, where would views like 'permanent' arise, for whom, which ones, and why? In the end the sixteen views do not need to be refuted one by one. They lose their footing all at once, because each needed a self or a world with a nature of its own to be about.

'Letting go of all views' does not mean having no opinions, or refusing to say anything. Nāgārjuna has argued all through the book, and the Buddha taught for decades. Candrakīrti and Tsongkhapa read it this way: what is abandoned are views that grasp at things as existing from their own side. A view in that sense is a way of holding on. Right view, the seeing of emptiness, is not one more thing to cling to; it is what loosens the clinging. As [Chapter 13](https://atr-passerby.com/nagarjuna-13-examination-of-compounded-phenomena/) warns, one who makes emptiness itself into a view is beyond help.

And the text ends as it began, with a bow (27.30): to Gautama, who out of compassion taught the true Dharma so that all views could be let go. The first chapter opened with a bow to the Buddha who taught dependent arising as the quieting of elaborations. The last closes with a bow to the same teacher for the same gift. Even here the key word of the work returns: he taught 'taking up' (upādāya) compassion, the word used for designation in dependence (24.18). The teaching, too, arose in dependence: on compassion, for those who suffer.

## What is left standing

None of this denies past lives, future lives, or the moral weight of what we do. Continuity is kept: one life conditions the next, as one flame lights another, and actions bear their results. Responsibility is kept: the mere I designated on body and mind keeps its promises and meets the results of what it has done. What is removed is the traveller, the self that would walk unchanged from life to life, and its shadow, the self that would be wiped out at death. The fear of both goes with them.

Try it with an old photograph of yourself as a child. Is that child you? Not simply: every cell, every thought has changed. Someone else? Not simply: your family, your memories and your name run straight from that afternoon to this one. Neither the same nor different, the child is you as designated. Nothing had to travel through the years for that to be true.

## The whole text in one bow

Looking back over the twenty-seven chapters, one movement repeats. Each chapter takes something we are sure of, conditions, motion, the senses, the self, time, the Buddha, nirvāṇa, and asks whether it is found standing on its own. Each time it asks whether the thing is the same as what it depends on or other than it, and each time both answers fail. And each time, the thing is still there, working, as designated: the sprout grows, the walker walks, the fire burns, the path leads to freedom.

That is what the homage of [Chapter 1](https://atr-passerby.com/nagarjuna-1-examination-of-conditions/) promised: dependent arising, without arising and without ceasing, without coming and without going, the quieting of elaborations, peace. The text does not end in a new theory. It ends in letting go, and in gratitude.

## Words to know

- **View** (dṛṣṭi): a position held as true about how things are in themselves; here, a way of holding on.
- **Earlier limit** (pūrvānta) and **later limit** (aparānta): a beginning and an end, on which the views rest.
- **What is taken up** (upādāna): the aggregates of body and mind on which a self is designated.
- **The one who takes up** (upādātṛ): the self, designated in dependence on what it takes up.
- **Continuum** (saṃtāna): a series of moments, like a flame, with nothing carried through it.
- **Permanence and annihilation** (śāśvata, uccheda): the two extremes about the self, lasting for ever or wiped out.
